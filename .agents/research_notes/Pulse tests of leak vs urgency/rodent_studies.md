# Rodent and cross-species studies: estimating leak, self-excitation and urgency from pulsed / time-varying evidence

Scope: rodent (rat, mouse) and cross-species-with-rodents studies only. Every finding carries a DOI/URL and, where read, a locator (figure, table, section). "abstract only" marks papers whose full text could not be retrieved. Numbers are quoted as printed; "not reported" means the quantity was absent from the text we could read. Sign convention used throughout: Brunton-style lambda (λ) with λ < 0 = leaky (OU decay), λ > 0 = unstable/self-excitatory, τ = 1/λ.

## Q1. Brunton, Botvinick & Brody 2013 (Science): how is λ identified from click trains, what values were found, and what constrains it?

### Takeaway
λ is one of nine parameters of a trial-by-trial, click-by-click generative model fitted by maximum likelihood to choices in a fixed-duration (experimenter-controlled, go-cue) task; the best fits put |λ| at 0.91 ± 0.15 s⁻¹ (rats, mean ± SE) and 0.23 ± 0.071 s⁻¹ (humans), i.e. |τ| comparable to or longer than the longest stimulus (1 s rats, 4 s humans), with λ statistically indistinguishable from zero in the example rat. λ is constrained mainly by (i) the psychophysical reverse correlation (flat click-influence over the trial) and (ii) the dependence of accuracy on stimulus duration, both of which the authors used as model-independent checks.

### Cited Findings
- Task: Poisson click trains from two speakers (rats) / headphones or flashed tilted bars (humans); "On each trial, the stimulus was presented for a duration controlled by the experimenter. The sum of the two pulse rates was kept fixed within each task, and discrimination difficulty was controlled on each trial by the ratio of the two rates (Fig. 1, C to E)"; longest stimulus 1 s for rats and 4 s for humans; the rat makes a choice after fixation and sounds end (Fig. 1 legend) — [Brunton et al. 2013, main text p. 95–96](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1233912) (PDF copy read: http://www.gatsby.ucl.ac.uk/tea/tea_archive/attached_files/brunton2013rats.pdf)
- Model (Eq. 1): da = σa dW + (δ_R·η_R·C − δ_L·η_L·C) dt + λ a dt; if |a| ≥ B then da/dt = 0; nine parameters λ, σa², σs², σi², B, φ, τφ, bias, lapse. "λ parameterizes consistent drift in the memory a. In the 'leaky' or forgetful case (λ < 0) drift is toward a = 0, and later pulses affect the decision more than earlier pulses. In the 'unstable' or impulsive case (λ > 0) ... earlier pulses affect the decision more than later pulses. The memory's time constant τ = 1/λ." Ideal performance: λ = 0, B = ∞, σa² = σs² = σi² = 0, φ = 1, bias = 0 (Eq. 3) — [Brunton et al. 2013, Fig. 2 model box](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1233912)
- Values: "λ was statistically indistinguishable from zero. That is, the decision dynamics were neither leaky nor unstable, suggesting that sensory evidence from throughout the entire stimulus period was given equal weight. The best-fit B was large enough that it produced model fits indistinguishable from those produced by B = ∞." (example rat, Fig. 2B) — [Brunton et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1233912)
- "Across subjects (Fig. 2, E and H), species, sensory modalities, and task parameters, the accumulator's memory time constant |τ| = 1/|λ| was long (|λ| = 0.91 ± 0.15 s⁻¹ mean ± SE across rats; |λ| = 0.23 ± 0.071 s⁻¹ across humans), in the sense that |τ| was comparable to or greater than the longest stimulus duration used [1 s for rats, 4 s for humans]. The best-fit values of B and λ were thus in the gradual accumulation regime (Fig. 2A, top)." Note these are magnitudes; per-subject signs are shown only in Fig. 2E — [Brunton et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1233912)
- Noise: "in 13 out of 19 rats and in all three humans performing the auditory task and all four humans performing the visual task, the value that best fit the data was the ideal σa² = 0"; σs² = 1.90 ± 0.28 pulses² per pulse (rats), 0.50 ± 0.059 (human auditory), 0.24 ± 0.10 (human visual); rats showed strong rapidly recovering depression, φ = 0.17 ± 0.021, τφ = 0.080 ± 0.024 s (main text; the Fig. 2 legend gives mean τφ = 0.040 s) — [Brunton et al. 2013, Fig. 2C,F,G,I,J](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1233912)
- n = 19 rats in the Brunton dataset (stated in a later re-analysis: "data from 19 rats analyzed in Brunton et al.") — [Gupta et al. 2024, Methods](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-44880-5)
- Confidence intervals: "Confidence intervals are given by the parameter width of the maximum (blue contours)" on two-dimensional slices of the nine-dimensional likelihood landscape; "2 SD of the multidimensional normal distribution fit to the likelihood landscape"; a related model "was proven to have a concave log likelihood, suggesting that our model may also be provably concave and have a single maximum" — [Brunton et al. 2013, Fig. 2B–D and legend](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1233912)
- What constrains λ (model-independent checks, Fig. 3): "To assess the memory time constant τ, we calculated the 'psychophysical reverse correlation' ... This analysis indicated that all periods of the trial have similar influence on the decision (approximately constant separation between the two traces in Fig. 3A), which is consistent with the long τ found in the model-based analysis." The reverse correlation was the "excess pulse rate difference (right pulses/s – left pulses/s, relative to the value expected)" for each choice, shown for the longest quarter of trials — [Brunton et al. 2013, Fig. 3A and legend](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1233912)
- Duration dependence: the model was built "To examine a large variety of possible mechanisms consistent with the performance improvement at longer stimulus durations seen in Fig. 1E"; and for σa², trials were grouped by exact (#right, #left) clicks: "Large σa² would predict decreasing within-group performance at longer stimulus durations. The data showed the opposite trend and was precisely predicted by the best-fit model, where σa² = 0 and clicks are depressing (Fig. 3C and fig. S13)." — [Brunton et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1233912)
- Fig. 2 legend lists alternative mechanisms the same model can express (burst detector via low sticky bound; precedence detector via adaptation) and states "The best-fit parameter values found, which are λ ≈ 0, B >> 1, σa² ≈ 0, σs² >> 0, and φ << 1, correspond to pulse accumulation with a perfect memory but imperfect processing of sensory inputs" — [Brunton et al. 2013, Fig. 2 legend](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1233912)
- Later Brody-lab summary of the same data: "Previous results have shown that rats exhibit a range of accumulation strategies spanning these values of λ (Brunton et al., 2013)" (i.e., individual rats ranged from leaky to unstable) — [DePasquale et al. 2024, Results](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.84955)
- Independent per-rat estimates from the same task and model in 14 other rats (Erlich 2015, Table 1): λ = 1.409, 1.226, 0.810, 1.087, 0.620, −0.098, 2.047, 0.349, −2.739, −2.070, −1.542, 2.258, −0.790, 1.371; "meta-rat" λ = 1.227 (τ ≈ 0.8 s, "slightly unstable"), B ≈ 16, lapse ≈ 0.10 — [Erlich et al. 2015, Table 1 and Results](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.05457)

### Inferences
- In the Brunton framework λ is identified from choices alone because click timing is known exactly: a leaky accumulator makes late clicks more influential (recency) and an unstable one makes early clicks more influential (primacy); with stimulus durations spanning 0.1–1 s, the duration dependence of accuracy and the reverse-correlation slope jointly pin λ. In a free-response task the "end of accumulation" is set by the subject, so the same kernel features are confounded by bound-crossing (see Q4, Prat-Ortega), which is the main difference from our RT design.
- The reported |λ| ≈ 0.9 s⁻¹ for rats (τ ≈ 1.1 s, our arithmetic) is of the same order as the ~1.3 s window in which our pulse effect is present; but Brunton's λ is a mean of magnitudes across rats whose individual signs differ (Erlich Table 1 shows both signs with |λ| up to ~2.7 s⁻¹), so "λ ≈ 0" is a population statement, not a per-animal guarantee.

### Gaps
- The Science supplementary materials (fits to synthetic data, likelihood-landscape details, fig. S4/S13) were not accessible (science.org 403); whether a formal parameter-recovery study is reported there is unverified.
- Per-rat λ values with signs (Fig. 2E) are only graphical; no table in the main text.

## Q2. Piet, El Hady & Brody 2018 (Nat Commun): adaptive leak in a dynamic environment — how the time constant was measured and what values were found

### Takeaway
In a fixed-duration dynamic-clicks task (hidden state switching at hazard h = 1 Hz; rates ≈ 38 vs 2 Hz), rats' integration timescale was measured two ways: an exponential fit to the psychophysical reverse-correlation kernel (slope b, validated to recover λ in simulated agents) and maximum-likelihood fits of the Brunton model with the sticky bound removed. Fitted discounting rates were |λ| ≈ 4.3–6.6 s⁻¹ (τ ≈ 150–230 ms) versus accuracy-maximizing values ≈ 4.6–5.9 s⁻¹, i.e. rats discounted slightly faster than optimal but within <1% accuracy of optimal; halving the hazard rate roughly halved the discounting rate.

### Cited Findings
- Task: "Except where noted, the hazard rate h = 1 Hz, and click rates γ = log(r1/r2) = 3, r1 ≈ 38 Hz, and r2 ≈ 2 Hz were kept constant"; "The hidden state toggles between two states according to a telegraph process with hazard rate h"; rats hold center fixation until the go signal and choose after the stimulus ends (not a reaction-time task); stimulus durations 0.5–2 s (arXiv Methods) — [Piet et al. 2018, Results/Methods](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-06561-y); [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.05945)
- Theory: optimal nonlinear discounting da = δR − δL − (2h/κ) sinh(κa) dt, with κ the per-click reliability reduced by sensory noise n; linear approximation "da = δR,t − δL,t − λa dt, where λ is the discounting rate and 1/λ is the integration timescale"; "Given that a linear discounting function matches the accuracy of the nonlinear model, we analyze rat evidence discounting behavior by looking for the appropriate discounting rate or equivalently the appropriate integration timescale"; "Increasing sensory noise decreases the discounting rate" (Fig. 2g) — [Piet et al. 2018, Results, Eq. 13 and Fig. 2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-06561-y)
- Kernel method: click trains smoothed with a causal Gaussian (SD 5 ms), expressed as excess click rate relative to the hidden-state expectation, averaged by choice; "To quantify the timescale of the reverse correlation curves, we fit an exponential of the form a·e^{bt} to each curve"; "The parameter b reliably recovers the discounting rate λ (Fig. 3d)" (synthetic agents, 20,000 trials); "The weaker the discounting rate, the flatter the reverse correlation curves" — [Piet et al. 2018, Methods and Fig. 3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-06561-y)
- Model fits: Brunton model with multiplicative click noise σs, adaptation φ/τφ, accumulator noise σa, initial noise σi, bias, lapse; sticky bound removed ("detrimental to high performance" in this task); maximum likelihood with half-Gaussian priors on σi and σa; uncertainty from the inverse Hessian; "Figure 5a shows the comparison for an example rat, showing that the model captures the timescale of evidence discounting seen by the reverse correlation analysis" — [Piet et al. 2018, Methods and Fig. 5a](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-06561-y); [arXiv v2 Eqs. 14–15, 23–25](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.05945)
- Values (arXiv v2, Table 1; the published Table 1 could not be parsed from XML): λ per rat = −5.09 ± 0.07 (H033), −6.39 ± 0.092 (H037), −4.33 ± 0.063 (H038), −4.94 ± 0.30 (H039), −6.64 ± 0.099 (H040), −4.98 ± 0.19 (H043), −5.11 ± 0.13 (H045), −5.15 ± 0.15 (H058), −5.12 ± 0.15 (H061) [table rows beyond H061 not captured]; accuracy-maximizing λ_opt = −5.03, −5.92, −5.13, −4.56, −5.53, −5.94, −5.27, −4.87, −4.75 respectively; the table prints no units (hazard rates are in Hz, so λ is in s⁻¹ — our inference) — [Piet et al., arXiv v2 Table 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.05945)
- "The rats appear to have slightly larger discounting rates than predicted by the normative theory"; freezing all other parameters and optimizing λ: "the accuracy maximizing discounting level was very close to the rat's discounting level (Fig. 5d) meaning that other model parameters highly constrain the rats' discounting rates"; improvement in accuracy from re-optimizing λ was "less than 1%" for all rats (Fig. 5d–e) — [Piet et al. 2018, Results](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-06561-y)
- Static vs dynamic: "In the static task, the rats have small discounting rates indicating an integration timescale comparable to the longest trial the rats experienced" (Fig. 5b compares dynamic-task rats to Brunton 2013 rats) — [Piet et al. 2018, Fig. 5b](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-06561-y)
- Hazard manipulation: discounting at h = 0.5 Hz was "approximately half" that at h = 1 Hz, with three rats switched between h = 0.5, 0 (static) and 0.5 Hz environments (Fig. 6) — [Piet et al. 2018, Fig. 6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-06561-y)
- Identifiability caveats stated by the authors: σa and σi are "difficult to recover in synthetic datasets" because of the large discounting rates (arXiv Methods); reverse correlation is not biased by lapses, unlike logistic regression (arXiv Fig. 18); a last-click strategy was ruled out because recovered timescales would saturate near the 36-Hz limit, and rats are "well away from this limit" (arXiv Fig. 19B); for average noise the fixed point integrates about 5 clicks (arXiv Fig. 19A) — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.05945)
- Replication in a new cohort (Boyd-Meredith et al. 2022, same task, h = 1 Hz): "The behavioral model parameter fits for each rat confirm that all rats used a leaky integration strategy (λ < 0)"; "Best fit discounting parameters were significantly different from a previously reported dataset of rats integrating in a stationary environment (p < 0.01; two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum test, n = 5 in dynamic and n = 19 stationary environments) (Fig. 1g)"; the model's latent state was used to predict changes of mind and FOF firing reversals — [Boyd-Meredith et al. 2022, Fig. 1g and Results](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-30736-3)

### Inferences
- Piet 2018 is the clearest rodent demonstration that λ is behaviourally adjustable and that kernel-slope and model-based estimates agree when the kernel is exponential; its τ ≈ 150–230 ms is roughly an order of magnitude shorter than the Brunton static-task τ, showing that "rodents are near-perfect integrators" is task-dependent, not a species constant.
- Their validation (exponential fit recovers λ in synthetic agents) is in a fixed-duration task with no bound; in an RT task the kernel also decays because of bound crossing, so the same fit would overestimate leak unless the bound/urgency is modelled.

### Gaps
- Published Table 1 (per-rat λ with CIs) was not extractable from the PMC XML; values above are from the arXiv v2 table (first 9 rats). Exact kernel-derived timescales per rat (Fig. 4) are graphical only.
- No formal cross-validation or model-comparison against bounded alternatives is reported.

## Q3. Scott et al. 2015, Odoemene et al. 2018, Pinto et al. 2018: temporal weighting of pulsed evidence in rats/mice and what it implies about leak

### Takeaway
All three are fixed-duration (or fixed-space) tasks. Rats (Scott 2015, LED flashes) show flat kernels and model time constants ≥ 2 s for most rats; mice in VR (Pinto 2018) show near-zero leak (λ = 0.03 ± 0.09 m⁻¹, P = 0.78) but a mild primacy (early/late weight ratio 0.73 ± 0.06); mice judging flash rate (Odoemene 2018, abstract only) used information "throughout the 1000 ms trial ... with early information most influential", interpreted as "minimal accumulation leak".

### Cited Findings
- Scott 2015 design: left/right 10-ms LED flashes in fixed 250-ms bins, up to 15 bins per side (unrestrained) or 6 (head-restrained); rats report after an auditory go cue; 7 unrestrained rats, 525,073 trials; a reaction-time version is listed as future work — [Scott et al. 2015, Results and Methods](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.11308)
- Scott 2015 model: 8-parameter Brunton-style model; "near-zero estimates of accumulator memory noise ... and long accumulation time constants (Figure 2—figure supplement 1)"; figure-supplement legend: "For many, but not all, of the rats, the time constant is close to or greater than 2 s (λ <= 0.5)"; head-restrained fits gave "accumulation time constants longer than the trial duration and bounds higher than the number of possible flashes" — [Scott et al. 2015, Figure 2—figure supplement 1](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.11308)
- Scott 2015 kernels: "The flatness of the lines suggests that rats use early, middle and late flashes equally to guide their decision"; "This result suggests accumulation time constants that are longer than the trial duration" (Fig. 2B; Fig. 7F) — [Scott et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.11308)
- Scott 2015 noise conclusion: "the standard deviation in their internal estimate of flash number scaled linearly with the number of flashes" (scalar variability, not diffusion √N); a static dual-accumulator scalar-variability model had the highest likelihood (Fig. 4D–E); bootstrap (1,000 resamples) CIs; no synthetic-recovery analysis found in the text read — [Scott et al. 2015, Abstract and Fig. 4](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.11308)
- Odoemene 2018 (abstract only): "we measured decisions in mice judging the fluctuating rate of a flash sequence. An initial analysis (>500,000 trials, 29 male and female mice) demonstrated that information throughout the 1000 ms trial influenced choice, with early information most influential. This suggests that information persists in neural circuits for ∼1000 ms with minimal accumulation leak"; mice "were unable to entirely suppress the influence of stimulus brightness" — [Odoemene et al. 2018, Abstract](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.3478-17.2018); preprint abstract adds "although mice were capable of achieving similar accuracy levels as rats, differences in accumulation strategy were apparent" — [bioRxiv 195792](https://doi.org/10.1101/195792)
- Pinto 2018 design: head-fixed mice in a virtual T-maze; towers appear when the mouse is 10 cm away and vanish 200 ms later; Poisson rates 7.7 (majority) vs 2.3 (minority) towers/m over a 200-cm cue region plus 100-cm delay; nominal cue period 3.4 ± 0.1 s at 61.1 ± 2.4 cm/s; 25 mice with ≥1,000 trials (135,824 trials) — [Pinto et al. 2018, Methods and Results](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2018.00036)
- Pinto 2018 kernel: logistic regression of choice on net evidence in 5 spatial bins (10–200 cm); "weight decay ratio" 0.73 ± 0.06 (mean ± SEM), significantly different from one (P = 1.3 × 10⁻⁴, two-sided t-test); 10/25 mice individually below one; some mice flat, some primacy, a minority recency (Fig. 3A–C) — [Pinto et al. 2018, Fig. 3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2018.00036)
- Pinto 2018 model: Brunton model with λ, sticky bound B, σs², σa², σi², φ, τφ, bias, lapse; fit constraints −5 < λ < 5 and 5 < B < 25; "memory leaks close to zero" λ = 0.03 ± 0.09 m⁻¹ (mean ± SEM; P = 0.78 vs zero), only four animals with λ significantly different from zero (Fig. 6C); sensory noise dominant (P = 7.0 × 10⁻⁴ vs σi², P = 5.9 × 10⁻⁴ vs σa²); Brunton DDM vs spatial-bin logistic regression not significantly different in cross-validated performance (P = 0.58, n = 20) — [Pinto et al. 2018, Fig. 6–7](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2018.00036)
- Pinto 2018: "Cue duration and delay duration were not significant predictors of performance beyond the tower counts" (Fig. 5C); SDT fits to individual mice failed to give low-noise estimates; no parameter-recovery analysis in the text read — [Pinto et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2018.00036)

### Inferences
- Note the unit issue in Pinto 2018: λ is per metre because evidence is spatial; at 61 cm/s, λ = 0.03 m⁻¹ corresponds to ~0.02 s⁻¹ (our arithmetic), i.e. effectively no leak over ~3 s.
- The mild primacy in mice (Pinto ratio 0.73; Odoemene "early information most influential") is the opposite sign to a leak signature; in fixed-duration tasks it is usually read as a small bound/early commitment or unstable λ, not leak.

### Gaps
- Odoemene 2018 full text (kernel values, any λ fit, response mode) could not be retrieved (J Neurosci 403, PMC captcha, bioRxiv 429); only the abstracts were read.
- Scott 2015 per-rat λ values (Figure 2—figure supplement 1) are graphical.

## Q4. Prat-Ortega et al. 2021 and Hermoso-Mendizabal et al. 2020 (de la Rocha lab): kernels, attractor vs DDM, leak and bounds

### Takeaway
Prat-Ortega 2021 is a theory paper whose empirical tests use human data (brightness discrimination with T = 1–5 s; two-pulse RDK); it contains no rat psychophysical kernels. Its key result for interpreting kernels: a DDM with absorbing bounds yields primacy, reflecting bounds yield recency, a perfect integrator yields flat kernels, and a double-well attractor crosses from primacy to recency as stimulus fluctuations or duration increase — so kernel shape alone does not identify leak. Hermoso-Mendizabal 2020 is a rat reaction-time task (stimulus plays until the rat withdraws) about history biases; it reports no stimulus kernel or leak estimate, but documents express responses and RT modulation by expectation; the follow-up Molano-Mazón 2024 models the same rat task with a DDM that includes a leak parameter λ, afferent/efferent delays and a stimulus-independent urgency process that triggers movement.

### Cited Findings
- Prat-Ortega 2021 canonical-model kernels: "(1) In the DDMA ... absorbing bounds ... the fluctuations late in the stimulus are unlikely to affect the decision, yielding a decaying PK characteristic of a 'primacy' effect. (2) In the Drift Diffusion Model with Reflecting bounds (DDMR) ... early stimulus fluctuations are largely forgotten once the decision variable bounces against one bound and hence the PK shows a 'recency' effect. (3) In the Perfect Integrator (PI) ... the stimulus is integrated uniformly across time yielding a flat PK"; "For very weak stimulus fluctuations, all three models are trivially equivalent because the bounds are never reached and hence the PKs are flat (Fig. 1d–f)"; "Including collapsing bounds in the DDMA did not modify the picture qualitatively, with the integration becoming more transient as the velocity of the collapsing bounds increased (Supplementary Fig. 1)" — [Prat-Ortega et al. 2021, Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-21501-z)
- Attractor (DWM) regimes: "the model exhibits a crossover from primacy to recency as the stimulus fluctuations or stimulus duration are increased"; "The same crossover ... could be achieved, at fixed σS, by varying the stimulus duration (Fig. 2d, g)"; the DWM time constant "τ of the DWM was chosen to be 200 ms"; non-monotonic accuracy vs σS most prominent at P ≈ 0.75, long T, weak internal noise (Fig. 3) — [Prat-Ortega et al. 2021, Figs. 2–3, Methods](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-21501-z)
- Empirical data are human: "human subjects had to discriminate the brightness of visual stimuli of variable duration T = 1, 2, 3, or 5 s" (N = 21, 100-ms frames; Fig. 6), where average PKs changed from primacy to recency with duration and "these results were obtained with fixed model parameters for all stimulus durations"; plus a two-pulse RDK dataset (120-ms pulses). The word "rat" occurs only in the reference list of the PMC XML — [Prat-Ortega et al. 2021, Fig. 6 and Methods](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-21501-z)
- Hermoso-Mendizabal 2020 task: "By poking into the center port, rats triggered the presentation of the stimulus, which lasted until they freely withdrew from the port"; stimulus evidence levels e = 0, ±0.23, ±0.48, ±1; n = 10 rats; blocks of 200 trials with repeating probability Prep — [Hermoso-Mendizabal et al. 2020, Fig. 1 and Methods](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-14824-w)
- Hermoso-Mendizabal 2020 RT findings: after correct trials RT was shorter for expected than unexpected stimuli (block × repeating-stimulus category F(1,126) = 134.59, p < 1e-6; mean normalized RT difference 0.10, paired t-test p < 1e-10); after errors RT was not modulated by expectation (three-way interaction F(1,264) = 26.77, p < 1e-6). No psychophysical kernel of within-trial stimulus fluctuations and no accumulation-model (leak/bound) fit are reported; the choice model is a GLM combining lateral bias, transition bias and net stimulus evidence through a probit — [Hermoso-Mendizabal et al. 2020, Supplementary Fig. 1c,d; Fig. 3; Methods](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-14824-w)
- Molano-Mazón et al. 2024 (same rat task, n = 15 rats, 300-ms fixation, stimulus until withdrawal): "in 34.3 ± 12.2% of all trials (mean ± SD), the rat initiated a movement in less than 50 ms after stimulus onset"; these express responses have RTs independent of stimulus strength (Fig. 1c–d tachometric curves); a "splitting time" analysis measures when stimulus evidence first affects the trajectory; the model is "dx/dt = −λx + a_p s(t − t_aff) + η(t)" with parameters (t_aff, t_eff, z_P, a_p, λ, σ_DV, θ_DV, θ_CoM), "corresponding to the afferent and efferent delay, the weight of the prior, the drift, the leak, the sigma of the Gaussian noise ..., the bound of the process and the bound that the evidence has to reach to change its initial choice"; "the decision-making module includes an urgency process that can initiate movement independently of the decision variable, thus accounting for the prevalence of express responses"; fitted with a neural-network likelihood approximation trained on 10 million simulations; in humans "We explicitly inserted an urgency component into the task, forcing subjects to initiate their response within 300 ms after stimulus onset" — [Molano-Mazón et al. 2024, Figs. 1, 2i, 5 and Methods](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-53586-7)
- Pardo-Vazquez et al. 2019 (same lab, rats, auditory RT task; abstract only): reaction-time distributions scale uniformly with sound level ("time-intensity equivalence in discrimination"), which they argue "reveal[s] that discriminative choices must be based on bounded exact accumulation of evidence" (i.e., no leak); preprint abstract: "the only robust mechanism consistent with reaction time scale-invariance is based on perfect accumulation of evidence up to a constant bound" and "Weber's law breaks down if the stimulus duration is capped at values shorter than the typical reaction time" — [Pardo-Vazquez et al. 2019, Abstract](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-019-0439-7); [bioRxiv 333559](https://doi.org/10.1101/333559)

### Inferences
- The de la Rocha-lab rat RT work argues for perfect (λ ≈ 0) bounded accumulation plus a separate, stimulus-independent urgency/proactive process that times movement onset — a two-process alternative to "urgency as gain on evidence", relevant to our finding that pulse effects vanish for slow responses.
- Prat-Ortega's result that collapsing bounds only make DDMA integration "more transient" means that in an RT task a decaying pulse-effect-vs-time profile is expected from bounds alone; a flat pulse effect across onsets (our observation) is more naturally produced by a perfect integrator with bound crossing that happens after all pulse onsets.

### Gaps
- The specific "rat primacy vs recency depending on stimulus strength" result we were asked about was not found in Prat-Ortega 2021 or Hermoso-Mendizabal 2020; it may be in a thesis or in Pardo-Vazquez 2019 (full text not accessible, no PMC version).
- Molano-Mazón 2024 fitted parameter values (λ, t_aff, t_eff) are in supplementary tables/figures not captured in the XML text.

## Q5. Hyafil et al. 2023 (eLife): integration vs non-integration models across rats, humans, monkeys

### Takeaway
A logistic-regression "integration" model (free per-sample weights, no leak or bound parameters) was compared by AIC, psychophysical kernels, maximum-accuracy bounds, "disagree trials" and integration maps against extrema-detection and snapshot models in fixed-duration discrete-sample tasks; integration won in all three species including 5 rats (Pardo-Vazquez 2019 data, 10 or 20 × 50-ms samples). No leak or time-constant estimates are given.

### Cited Findings
- Datasets: monkeys (n = 2, 7 motion pulses of 150–166 ms; 72,137 and 33,416 trials), humans (n = 9, 5–10 gratings), rats (n = 5 Long-Evans, fixed-duration auditory intensity discrimination, 10 or 20 samples of 50 ms; e.g., 77,810 and 54,803 trials for two rats); "No reaction-time data were analyzed" — [Hyafil et al. 2023, Methods](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.84045)
- Models: integration p(r) = σ(β0 + Σβi Si) with arbitrary per-sample weights; extrema detection (first sample exceeding θ, with last-sample and position-varying-threshold variants); snapshot (one or K consecutive attended samples, EM mixture); compared via AIC, kernels, psychometric curves, boundary accuracy, disagree trials, subjective-weight regression — [Hyafil et al. 2023, Methods "Integration model", "Extrema-detection model", "Snapshot model"](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.84045)
- Rats: "Integration gave a better account on psychometric curves, accuracy, kernels, and AIC (Figure 6A–D)"; numeric ΔAIC not given in the text; humans: 8/9 and 6/9 subjects outperformed the maximum accuracy of snapshot and extrema models — [Hyafil et al. 2023, Results](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.84045)
- Leak: "Divisive normalization and leak are mentioned only as possible sources of unequal weighting"; "No time constants, leak rates, or bound values are reported"; identifiability caveat: extrema-detection subjective weights looked linear because of "very high estimated sensory noise"; no formal model-recovery simulation — [Hyafil et al. 2023, Results and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.84045)

### Inferences
- Hyafil 2023 establishes integration over non-integration in rats but is silent on leak magnitude; its rat kernels (Fig. 6C) are the only published stimulus-fluctuation kernels for the de la Rocha task that we located.

### Gaps
- Rat kernel shape (flat/primacy/recency) is shown graphically (Fig. 6C) and was not quantified in the text we read.

## Q6. Chakravarty et al. 2025 (eLife reviewed preprint 105116): time pressure, urgency and strategy switching in mice

### Takeaway
In a synchronized free-response flash task (rats n = 21, mice n = 95, adolescent humans n = 18; 10-ms flashes at 10 Hz in 100-ms bins, 80:20 ratio, flashes continue until the response), rodents were better fit by a collapsing-boundary DDM than by fixed-bound or linear-urgency DDMs (mice p < 0.0005, F = 22.12; rats p = 0.0014, F = 11.12; humans p = 0.13); mice had the fastest boundary collapse (lowest semi-saturation constant, p = 0.004) and the highest urgency slope and onset delay; mice were best fit by a 3-state GLM-HMM (accumulation vs left/right-bias states). Accuracy nevertheless increased with RT in all species (peaking at 0.7–1 s). Reviewers flagged unidentifiability of the variability parameters and the undefined urgency model.

### Cited Findings
- Design: "the flashes continued until the animal poked on one of the side ports"; 10 ms pulses binned in 100 ms bins at 10 Hz, 80:20 probability in the test stage; Poisson process for humans, Bernoulli for rodents; 21 rats, 95 mice, 18 adolescent humans (11–17 y) — [Chakravarty et al. 2025, Results and Methods](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.105116.1)
- Accuracy vs RT (Fig. 2F legend): "All of the species peak between 0.7-1 seconds but the humans present a higher peak than the rodents"; Pearson correlations per 0.2-s bin "Mouse: p < 0.017, R = 0.80, Rat: p < 0.011, R = 0.83, Human: p < 0.033, R = 0.85"; mice respond faster on errors than on correct trials — [Chakravarty et al. 2025, Fig. 2](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/105116/figures)
- Classic DDM (Fig. 3): BIC humans 2772 ± 262, rats 1492 ± 403, mice 2778 ± 136; rodents had lower drift (F = 77.72) and boundaries (F = 88.29) than humans; rats the slowest non-decision time (F = 347.26); "mice were the fastest and least accurate of the three species" — [Chakravarty et al. 2025, Fig. 3](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/105116/figures)
- Collapsing-bound and urgency DDMs (Fig. 4): collapsing boundary with three free parameters ("initial boundary separation, semi-saturation constant, and amount of collapse"); urgency with three free parameters ("slope of the signal, magnitude of the signal, and onset delay for the signal", linear); "Mice show the lowest semi-saturation constant (One-way ANOVA; p = 0.004, F = 5.69)"; "Mice present the highest urgency-slope (p = 0.027, F = 3.71) and urgency delay (p = 0.003, F = 6.06)"; rodent choices "limited by time-pressures" — [Chakravarty et al. 2025, Fig. 4 legend](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/105116/figures)
- Reward rate (Fig. 5): rats were close to the simulated reward-rate-optimal RT of a perfect accumulator (simulations at 80:20, 10 Hz) — [Chakravarty et al. 2025, Fig. 5](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.105116.1)
- Strategy switching (Fig. 6): 4-parameter GLM (bias, flash ratio, win-stay/lose-switch, previous choice); humans weight flash ratio more (F = 156.28), mice weight previous choice more (F = 18.2); "Mice showed greater increase in LL for a GLM-HMM with 3 latent states"; rats and humans best fit by the one-state GLM; mice show the most within-session variability (Supplementary Fig. 8; mouse sessions 60 min vs rat 120 min) — [Chakravarty et al. 2025, Fig. 6, Supp. Fig. 7–8](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/105116/figures)
- Recovery: "we simulated 50 datasets per species with 600 trials each" and compared fitted vs generative DDM parameters (Supplementary Fig. 3); no recovery reported for the collapsing-bound/urgency models or the GLM-HMM; no psychophysical kernels and no leaky/unstable accumulator fits reported — [Chakravarty et al. 2025, Supp. Fig. 3](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.105116.1)
- Reviews: Reviewer 2: "It is not clear how the urgency model used works ... In a one-dimensional DDM, it is not clear where the urgency is applied"; many parameters with one stimulus condition "may lead to unidentifiability problems"; the recovery analysis "shows that at least two of these variability parameters can not be recovered"; suggests extending the GLM "to capture the differential weighting of each sequential sample" (a kernel) and asks for correct-vs-error RT comparisons; Reviewer 3 notes rodents are "deciding under time pressure" and that recovery results are not discussed; Reviewer 1 asks whether species differences are driven by engagement-state occupancy. eLife assessment: "important", "solid" — [eLife reviews page](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/105116/reviews)

### Inferences
- This is the closest published analogue to our design (head-free, not head-fixed; flashes not motion), and it concludes that mouse RT distributions need a time-dependent termination (collapsing bound) while accuracy still rises with RT — the same qualitative pattern as our "pulse effect only for responses < ~1.3 s, choices toward the pulse faster". Note that they did not test leak at all, so collapsing bound vs leak was not discriminated.

### Gaps
- Numerical values of the collapse (semi-saturation constant, amount of collapse) and urgency parameters per species are only in figure panels; not reported in text.

## Q7. Shevinsky & Reinagel 2019 and other rat/mouse reaction-time studies arguing for urgency or collapsing bounds

### Takeaway
Rats in RT random-dot tasks show accuracy increasing with RT (Reinagel 2013; Shevinsky & Reinagel 2019: slope +0.16 ± 0.14 per epoch, humans −0.16 ± 0.18), which is incompatible with the slow-error signature of collapsing bounds/urgency as used for primates; the Reinagel lab's explanation is starting-point variability (Nguyen & Reinagel 2022, HDDM fits, acknowledged as degenerate), while Asadpour et al. 2024 fit the same rat data with time-variant gain applied to the drift only (an urgency-like gain on signal, producing fast errors). Reinagel 2013 concluded that rats' response timing "is governed more by elapsed time than by a criterion for sufficient evidence". In free-response flash tasks (Kane 2024; Chakravarty 2025) rats' accuracy also rises with RT, DDMs fit with high bounds, and collapsing bounds fit best.

### Cited Findings
- Shevinsky & Reinagel 2019 design: random-dot motion, free-response; coherence varied in interleaved trials or fixed per block; stimulus parameters differed between species but "the overall reaction times of rats and humans were similar, with a minimum response latency of ∼0.5 s, a median of ∼1 s, and 95th percentile of ∼3 s in both species"; N = 46 epochs from 11 rats and 79 epochs from 52 humans — [Shevinsky & Reinagel 2019, Results](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.01211)
- Main result: "For rats the average slope was 0.16 ± 0.14 overall (mean ± SD; N = 53 epochs from 11 rats), and 0.18 ± 0.14 in psychometric epochs with <1% lapse (N = 12). For humans the average slope was −0.16 ± 0.18 overall (N = 66 epochs from 48 humans ...), and −0.19 ± 0.19 in psychometric epochs with <1% lapse (N = 46)" (slope of accuracy vs RT regression; example rat epoch +0.26 with 8,149 trials; example human −0.23); "The magnitude of the effects increased with motion coherence"; "Rats' accuracy was dependent on the duration of stimulus presentation, and the increase in accuracy with reaction time was contingent on stimulus presence" — [Shevinsky & Reinagel 2019, Fig. 3 and Abstract](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.01211)
- Temporally local analysis: "compares the reaction times of errors to adjacent correct trials of the same coherence" to rule out non-stationarity; "for rats, error trials have shorter reaction times, while for humans errors have longer reaction times (Figure 4)"; mean RT of rats increased over the beginning of sessions, humans' decreased — [Shevinsky & Reinagel 2019, Fig. 4](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.01211)
- Modelling stance: no DDM was fitted in this paper; the Introduction notes that for the human/primate pattern "Several variants of the drift-diffusion model can account for the result ... variable drift rates, collapsing bounds, or urgency signals" and the Discussion states "some of the model modifications proposed to explain the primate data would be incompatible with the rat data" — [Shevinsky & Reinagel 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.01211)
- Reinagel 2013 (6 rats, 11 experiments, coherence 85–95% fixed blocks plus interleaved): "(1) When motion strength was constant across trials, the error trials had shorter reaction times than correct trials ... (3) Accuracy increased with reaction time for each motion strength ... (4) When stimulus duration was limited, accuracy improved with stimulus duration, whereas reaction time decreased. (5) Accuracy decreased with response latency after stimulus offset. We conclude that rats integrate visual evidence over time, but in this task the time of their response is governed more by elapsed time than by a criterion for sufficient evidence"; accuracy rose with RT "from about 500–1200 ms" and "approached 100% for reaction times above 1 second" — [Reinagel 2013, Abstract and Fig. 2](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0068505)
- Nguyen & Reinagel 2022: a DDM with trial-to-trial variability "can explain both effects with variable parameters: trial-to-trial variability in the starting point of the diffusion process produces the pattern typically observed in rats, whereas variability in the drift rate produces the pattern typically observed in humans"; "the same effects can be produced by deterministic biases"; simulation parameters producing rat-like behaviour: a = 1.84, t = 0.74, σv = 0.1, σz = 1.5, σt = 0.2; human-like: a = 2.0, t = 0.5, σv = 1.5, σz = 0.3, σt = 0.03 (Fig. 2); HDDM fits to the Shevinsky datasets: "human distributions were shifted toward those that favor late errors (higher σv, higher a and lower σz), and rats' parameters toward those that favor early errors (higher σz, lower a and σv)"; "We emphasize that fitting the parameters of this model is problematic"; "This explains why parameter fits to data are generally degenerate"; "we refrain from making statistical claims"; collapsing bounds/urgency are discussed as alternatives but not fitted — [Nguyen & Reinagel 2022, Figs. 2–3 and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.794681)
- Asadpour et al. 2024 (51 humans and 11 rats from Shevinsky & Reinagel, RTs 0.1–2.5 s, plus Roitman & Shadlen monkeys): using Ditterich (2006) model 4 (constant bounds, time-variant gain), four cases were fitted (gain on drift and noise, with or without coherence-dependent noise; gain on noise only; gain on drift only), 7 parameters each, selected by mean squared error with AIC = 14 − 2·logL; "time-variant gain just on the drift rate could account for rat data with faster error RTs, but applying this gain only to the noise term might be suitable to account for slower error RTs (as in monkey data). For human data, time-variant gain might have minimal effects" — [Asadpour et al. 2024, Abstract, Methods](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42113-023-00194-1)
- Reinagel 2021 preprint (abstract only): "two rats performing visual motion discrimination were found to have inverted chronometric response functions: their average response time paradoxically increased with stimulus strength. We hypothesize that corrective decision reversals may underlie this" — [bioRxiv 2021.10.26.465587](https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.10.26.465587)
- Kane, Senne & Scott 2024 (rats, free-response flashes; preprint text): in each 100-ms bin the correct port flashes (10 ms) with 75% probability vs 25% for the incorrect port; no fixation; "rats performed an average of 409 trials per session, reached high accuracy (76% correct on average) and exhibited relatively long response times (mean = 0.956s)"; "rats exhibited increased accuracy on trials with longer RTs (Figure 1E)"; "The traditional DDM provided a good description of the rats' RT distributions (Figure 2B) and indicated relatively high decision bounds"; a movement-based DDM (mDDM) with trial-wise pose variables fit better; "a period of relative immobility, prior to movement onset, and ... evidence presented during this period was a better predictor of subsequent choice than later evidence"; "delay in movement onset is correlated with decision bounds"; no leak, collapsing-bound or urgency model reported — [Kane et al. 2024 preprint](https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.09.11.556575); published: [J Neurophysiol 2024](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00181.2024)
- Zariwala et al. 2013 (rats, odor-mixture categorization, RT = odor sampling duration + movement time; partial text): near-boundary mixtures dropped accuracy to near chance while "odor sampling time increased only 30 ms"; rats trained under "low urgency" conditions (fixed minimum interval to reward) still showed "no change in accuracy" despite substantially longer sampling (Fig. 3D; Table 1); the authors discuss temporally correlated noise as defeating integration — [Zariwala et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.02.010)

### Inferences
- The rodent RT literature consistently reports fast errors / accuracy rising with RT, so any urgency implementation that predicts slow errors (gain on both signal and noise, or bound collapse with late evidence) must be reconciled with this; Asadpour's "gain on signal only" is a formulation that produces fast errors and is formally an urgency gain on accumulated evidence.
- None of these RT studies manipulated evidence within trials (no pulses), so they cannot separate leak from urgency; our pulsed design is complementary.

### Gaps
- Fitted parameter values for the rat gain model in Asadpour 2024 (gain slope, bound) are in their tables/figures and were not captured.
- Nguyen & Reinagel 2022 per-rat fitted parameter distributions (Fig. 3F,H) are graphical.

## Q8. Mouse reaction-time tasks with time-varying evidence or pulses (2018–2026), and mouse leak/urgency fits

### Takeaway
Apart from Chakravarty 2025 (Q6), we found no published mouse reaction-time study with motion pulses or fitted leak/urgency parameters. Orsolic et al. 2021 is the closest: a mouse RT change-detection task with continuously fluctuating evidence, where behaviour is explained by integration of the stimulus on a ~1 s timescale combined with temporal expectation (hazard rate) — an explicit time-dependent component — but with a GLM-style lick-hazard model rather than a DDM. Mouse RDK studies (Marques 2018, Stirman 2016, Samonds 2018) are fixed-duration or lick-rate designs without accumulation-model fits. No IBL paper fitting urgency or collapsing bounds was located.

### Cited Findings
- Orsolic 2021: 6 mice, change detection of temporal frequency (TF) in a drifting noisy grating; detection and RT scaled with change magnitude (19,734 noisy trials, 109 sessions); hazard rate of changes manipulated in early vs late blocks; "The early lick hazard rate ... was elevated at the start of the trial in early blocks (Figure 1I) when changes were more frequent"; a statistical model predicted the momentary lick hazard "based on the history of visual input (2.5 s) and time elapsed since the start of the trial (Figure 2B)"; "mouse behavior is primarily explained by a strategy involving integration of TF on the timescale of ∼1 s and by the expectation of when the sustained changes in stimulus speed might occur" — [Orsolic et al. 2021, Figs. 1–2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2021.03.031)
- Marques 2018: head-fixed 2AFC RDK; "mice reported the perceived direction of motion by licking one of two available lickports immediately after stimulus offset" (fixed duration, trained at 1 s; mice withheld licking during the stimulus); accuracy increased with stimulus duration (p = 1.38 × 10⁻⁸; 75 ± 5% at 1 s vs 80 ± 4% at 1.5 s, p = 0.28); 16% coherence above chance (56 ± 6%); no accumulation-model fit — [Marques et al. 2018, Figs. 1–2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2018.04.012)
- Stirman 2016: touchscreen RDK in 7 C57BL/6 mice; coherence threshold 22 ± 5% at 70% correct; response times reported as a function of learning/difficulty; no DDM — [Stirman et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2016.07.006)
- Samonds 2018: lick-based motion discrimination and motion aftereffect in mice (d′ analysis); no RT model — [Samonds et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1523/ENEURO.0065-18.2018)
- Pinto 2018 and Odoemene 2018 (Q3) are the only mouse pulse-accumulation datasets with leak estimates; both fixed-duration/space.
- Discovery searches (Europe PMC: mice AND ("collapsing bound" OR "urgency signal") AND "reaction time"; mice AND "random dot" AND "reaction time" AND "drift diffusion"; mice AND "motion pulse"; mice AND "leaky accumulat" AND "reaction time") returned no mouse RT study with pulsed evidence or fitted leak/urgency other than Chakravarty 2025 — [search record, Europe PMC REST](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search)

### Inferences
- The mouse literature therefore contains no prior estimate of λ or urgency from an RT motion task; Chakravarty 2025 (collapsing bound) and Orsolic 2021 (~1 s integration plus temporal expectation) are the only quantitative time-dependence results in mice.

### Gaps
- IBL task analyses with urgency/collapsing bounds: none found; the IBL wheel task is not pulsed, so this is likely a true absence rather than a retrieval failure, but it was not exhaustively checked.

## Q9. Methods papers estimating leak/instability from pulsed evidence with explicit recovery or identifiability analyses (Brody lab and related)

### Takeaway
Explicit synthetic-data recovery of λ exists for the joint neural-behavioural model of DePasquale 2024 (Figure 1—figure supplements 1–2; λ bounded in [−5, 5] s⁻¹) and for the FINDR/MMDDM analyses of Luo 2025; Erlich 2015 and Yartsev 2018 report bootstrap/Hessian-based uncertainty and discuss trade-offs (noise vs lapse; bound vs ∞). Regional estimates differ: PPC and ADS activity is best fit by leaky accumulation (λ < 0), FOF by unstable accumulation (λ > 0), while choices alone showed no cohort difference in λ; Luo 2025 finds the autonomous neural dynamics around the origin are unstable (self-excitatory) during accumulation and that commitment is a regime transition.

### Cited Findings
- DePasquale 2024 model: "da = λa dt + Δ(t) dt + σa dW + σs Σ(t) η dt ... λ is a leak parameter"; "Positive values of λ correspond to unstable dynamics so that a(t) grows exponentially. By contrast, negative values of λ correspond to leaky dynamics. When λ equals zero, the sensory clicks are perfectly integrated"; parameter bounds 8 ≤ B ≤ 40, −5 ≤ λ ≤ 5, 5e-3 ≤ τφ ≤ 1; 11 rats (5 FOF, 3 PPC, 3 ADS), Δt = 10 ms — [DePasquale et al. 2024, Results and Methods](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.84955)
- Recovery: "We verified that our method was able to recover the parameters that generated synthetic physiologically relevant spiking and choices data (Figure 1—figure supplement 1), and that parameter recovery was robust across a range of parameter values (Figure 1—figure supplement 2)"; example generative set σi = 5, B = 15, λ = −0.5, σa = 100, σs = 20, φ = 0.4, τφ = 0.02 (two sessions × 400 trials × 3 neurons); four further synthetic datasets with different parameters — [DePasquale et al. 2024, Figure 1—figure supplements 1–2](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.84955)
- Results: "Although there was no significant difference in the value of λ across the cohorts of animals in the choice-only model, we found substantial differences across brain regions in the joint model fits (Figure 3A). The PPC and ADS data were best fit by leaky accumulator models (λ<0). Surprisingly however, the FOF data was best described by a model with unstable accumulation dynamics (λ>0)"; low bound plus instability "causes a(t) to stop evolving early in the trial ... 'primacy encoding'"; choice-only fits had σa ≈ 0 and large σs; joint fits σa > 0, σi >> 0; B often hit its upper limit of 40 and "the log-likelihood surface as B grows very large becomes very flat"; CIs ±2 SD from a Laplace approximation; cross-validation did not qualitatively change parameters (Figure 3—figure supplement 4). Numeric λ values are only in Fig. 3A — [DePasquale et al. 2024, Fig. 3 and Methods](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.84955)
- Erlich 2015: control "meta-rat" λ = 1.227 (τ ≈ 0.8 s, "slightly unstable"); bilateral FOF inactivation λ = −4.144 (τ ≈ −0.24 s, "leaky accumulation over only a quarter of a second"), bootstrap (300 resamples) 95% CI [−7.91, −1.31] vs control 1.22; bilateral PPC λ = 1.331 (no significant change); model comparison for BiFOF (n = 1,809 trials): full 9-parameter model logL −1102.5, BIC 2272.5; λ-only change model logL −1121.1, BIC 2249.7 (lowest); bias-only −1221.6, BIC 2450.7; stated trade-off: "because noise and lapse covary strongly ... it is not possible to say which of them was significantly shifted"; likelihood surfaces over λ × lapse (Fig. 4B); kernel statement: "A further property of a leaky accumulator is that the more recent the clicks are [the more they matter]" with reverse correlations in Fig. 4D; stimulus durations 0.1–1 s, 14 rats — [Erlich et al. 2015, Tables 1–2, Fig. 4](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.05457)
- Yartsev 2018: stimulus 0.1–1.2 s; 8-parameter model plus 3 side-bias terms; "a leaky (i.e., forgetful) accumulator and an increased overall lapse rate both predict an overall performance impairment. But the leaky accumulator impairment will be greater for trials that by chance had their clicks earlier rather than later, whereas the lapse impairment will be independent of the timing of each trial's clicks"; trade-offs appear as likelihood ridges quantified by the Hessian; fits run from 10 seeds; BIC showed only B could be dropped ("confidence intervals include infinity"); "long accumulation time constants exhibited by the rats (see Supplementary file 1)" — [Yartsev et al. 2018, Results and Methods](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.34929)
- Luo 2025 (rats, fixed-duration clicks, hundreds of simultaneously recorded frontal/striatal neurons; 27 sessions): FINDR discovers "two sequential regimes: an initial phase dominated by sensory inputs, followed by a phase dominated by autonomous dynamics"; around the origin the autonomous dynamics are non-normal and "here the origin is unstable (Extended Data Fig. 10a,e)" with Jacobian eigenvalues λ in s⁻¹ (Extended Data Fig. 10); the simplified MMDDM has three fitted parameters (bound height B, starting mean μ0, per-click SNR) with adaptation fixed (φ = 0.001, k = 100) and yields a neurally inferred commitment time (nTc) per trial, tested by the prediction "evidence before this point should affect the decision, whereas evidence afterwards should not"; recovery: FINDR refit to synthetic data generated from fitted models ("FINDR reliably recovers the FINDR-inferred dynamics"); MMDDM encoding weights recovered from simulated data (coefficient of determination) and five-fold cross-validation — [Luo et al. 2025, Fig. 4, Extended Data Figs. 1, 7, 10](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09528-4)
- Gupta 2024: 152 rats, 37,522 ± 22,090 trials per rat, Poisson clicks with "trial difficulty, stimulus duration, and correct answer ... set independently on each trial"; Brunton model with λ ("inverse time constant of the consistent drift in memory") plus history-dependent initial state; 5-fold cross-validation on 10 rats "yielded very similar maximum likelihood parameters and virtually identical test and training log-likelihoods"; BIC favoured history-dependent initial states in 147/152 rats — [Gupta et al. 2024, Results and Methods](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-44880-5)
- Piet 2018 recovery (Q2): exponential kernel slope b recovers λ in simulated agents; σa and σi poorly recoverable with strong discounting — [Piet et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-06561-y)

### Inferences
- In rats the sign of λ is region- and analysis-dependent (choices: ~0; FOF neural: unstable; PPC/ADS neural: leaky), and neural evidence (Luo 2025) indicates self-excitatory autonomous dynamics at the origin during accumulation followed by a commitment transition. A behavioural λ ≈ 0 can therefore coexist with circuit-level leak and self-excitation, which cautions against over-interpreting a single behavioural λ.
- All explicit recovery checks are in fixed-duration tasks; none addresses recovery of λ jointly with a collapsing bound/urgency term in an RT task, which is exactly the identifiability problem our pulse test faces.

### Gaps
- Numeric λ values per region (DePasquale Fig. 3A), per rat (Yartsev Supplementary file 1) and the Luo eigenvalues (Extended Data Fig. 10) are only in figures/supplements.
- Roy et al. 2021 (PsyTrack) was not read; it concerns trial-to-trial weight dynamics during learning, not within-trial leak.

## Q10. Cross-cutting: which data features constrain leak versus urgency in rodent pulse tasks (synthesis)

### Takeaway
In every rodent study that estimated λ, the constraining data features were the time-course of pulse influence on choice (kernel) and the dependence of accuracy on stimulus duration in a fixed-duration design; urgency/collapsing bounds were only ever fitted to RT distributions in tasks without within-trial evidence manipulation. No rodent study has yet fitted leak and urgency jointly to pulsed RT data.

### Cited Findings
- Fixed-duration leak identification via kernel + duration dependence: Brunton 2013 (Fig. 3A, Fig. 1E) — [source](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1233912); Piet 2018 (Fig. 3d, 5a) — [source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-06561-y); Scott 2015 (Fig. 2B) — [source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.11308); Pinto 2018 (Fig. 3, 6C) — [source](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2018.00036)
- Leak vs lapse distinguished by click timing (early vs late clicks) rather than by psychometric shape: Erlich 2015 (Fig. 4A–B) — [source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.05457); Yartsev 2018 — [source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.34929)
- Kernel shape ambiguity under bounds (absorbing → primacy; reflecting → recency; attractor → duration-dependent crossover; collapsing bounds → more transient integration): Prat-Ortega 2021 — [source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-21501-z)
- RT-only urgency/collapse evidence in rodents: Chakravarty 2025 (collapsing bound best for rats and mice) — [source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.105116.1); Asadpour 2024 (gain on drift only reproduces rats' fast errors) — [source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42113-023-00194-1); Reinagel 2013 (response time "governed more by elapsed time") — [source](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0068505); counter-explanation by starting-point variability, Nguyen & Reinagel 2022 — [source](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.794681)
- Evidence weighting concentrated before movement onset in free-response rats (Kane 2024) — [source](https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.09.11.556575); evidence after the neurally inferred commitment time does not affect choice (Luo 2025) — [source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09528-4)

### Inferences
- For our data, the rodent precedent suggests: (i) a leak-only model predicts a pulse effect that depends on pulse onset relative to the response (later pulses stronger), independent of RT; (ii) a bound/commitment account predicts pulse effects that depend on whether the pulse precedes commitment (present for fast responses, absent for slow), which is what we observe; (iii) the flat onset dependence within 0.05–0.42 s combined with absence for responses > 1.3 s matches commitment/collapse more than OU leak with τ ≪ 1 s, but no rodent study provides a direct quantitative template for this contrast.

### Gaps
- No rodent paper combines pulsed evidence with free-response RTs and fits λ together with a collapsing bound or urgency gain; identifiability of that combination is unstudied in the rodent literature we could access.
