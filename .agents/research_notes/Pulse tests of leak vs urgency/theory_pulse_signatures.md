# Theory of brief evidence pulses in reaction-time bounded-accumulation tasks: perfect integration vs leak (OU) vs urgency / collapsing bound

Scope: theory and modelling only. Every claim carries a source and a locator (equation, figure, section). Items read only as abstract or via a secondary description are marked "abstract only" or "secondary". Locators for Zhou et al. 2009 are from the arXiv preprint (0901.2173, read in full via ar5iv) and were spot-checked against the PMC author manuscript (PMC2784641); equation and figure numbering agreed in every passage checked. Our project data were not sent anywhere; the only project facts used are those in the assignment.

## Key question 1: Predicted pulse signatures for each model class — (a) perfect integration with fixed bounds, (b) stable OU (leak), (c) unstable OU (self-excitation), (d) urgency gain / collapsing bound with perfect integration, (e) urgency-gating with a short low-pass filter (UGM)

### Takeaway
The literature gives clean, equation-level predictions for the effect of a pulse on the *decision variable* (perfect integrator: permanent displacement equal to the pulse area, time-shift invariant; stable OU: displacement that decays as e^(-|k|·(t−T)), so later pulses are "fresher"; unstable OU: displacement that grows, so earlier pulses dominate; UGM: displacement that decays with the filter time constant but is multiplied by the growing urgency; collapsing bound: permanent displacement that is a growing fraction of the shrinking bound) and robust simulation results for the effect on *mean decision time versus pulse onset* (Zhou et al. 2009, Fig. 2). All classes share a "thresholding" (bound-depletion) decline of the pooled effect with onset time, and the published work shows that noise, errors, non-decision "dead time", and short RTs can mask the class-specific signatures. No paper gives a closed-form choice shift as a function of time-to-decision; what exists is the kernel theory of Okazawa et al. 2018 (bound per se does not distort stimulus-aligned kernels in RT tasks; non-decision time, urgency, leak and inhibition do).

### Cited Findings

**Common reduced model and the generic "thresholding" decline (all classes)**
- Zhou, Wong-Lin & Holmes (2009) reduce a three-population firing-rate circuit to the scalar SDE "dX_t = b(X_t,t)dt + σ dW_t" (Eq. 5) with "b(X_t,t) ≡ kX_t + (I1(t) − I2(t))/τE", "k ≡ (α − 1)/τE contains the excitatory coupling strength and leak"; k < 0 is the stable OU (SOU), k > 0 the unstable OU (UOU), k = 0 with constant input is the pure drift-diffusion "CD" model (Eq. 6: "dX_t = b0 dt + σ dW_t"), and a time-dependent-drift "TD" model (Eq. 7: "dX_t = b(t)dt + σ dW_t", with "b(t) = b0 t") stands in for urgency ("some form of 'urgency' induced by the task design (Ditterich, 2006a; Ditterich, 2006b; Churchland et al., 2008)"). SOU/UOU "provide reduced descriptions of the leaky competing accumulator of Usher and McClelland, 2001 that respectively produce recency and primacy effects" (Sec. 2.2). — [Zhou et al. 2009, Neural Comput, DOI 10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)
- Single-pulse protocol (Eq. 9): additive drift perturbation b1(t) = p for T < t ≤ T + ΔT, "the step function used by Huk and Shadlen, 2005"; "We fix the duration ΔT at 10% of the unperturbed mean first passage time τ0/10" (Sec. 2.4.1). Result (Fig. 2, Sec. 3.1): "Imposed early during the integration process, a brief pulse in the same (opposite) direction as the drift rate significantly decreases (increases) mean decision times in all four models ... These effects fade as T increases due to a thresholding effect: for later perturbations, more trials have already crossed threshold, and so there are relatively fewer trials that are perturbed than unperturbed." Note: "unlike the work of Huk and Shadlen, 2005; Wong et al., 2007, trials that have crossed threshold before and during perturbation are not excluded in the averaging process." — [Zhou et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)
- Appendix A (Propositions 2–3, Table 3) proves, in the noise-free limit and for expected first-passage times with additive noise, that an early positive pulse advances and a negative pulse retards threshold crossing, and that the dependence on onset time is monotone with sign set by f′ (the sign of k): for f′ ≥ L > 0 (UOU-type) the earlier-delivered pulse produces the shorter passage time; for f′ ≤ −L < 0 (SOU-type) the reverse (Eqs. 17–27, Table 3). — [Zhou et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)
- Okazawa et al. (2018) define the psychophysical kernel "K(t) = E[s(t)|choice1] − E[s(t)|choice2]" (Eq. 1) and, for the RT task, with conditioning on trials not yet decided, "K(t) = E[s(t)|C¹_{T≥t}] − E[s(t)|C²_{T≥t}]" (Eq. 3). They prove that "in a simple DDM where decision bound and noise are constant over time and behavioral responses are generated as soon as the DV reaches one of the bounds (non-decision time = 0), psychophysical kernels are proportional to the sensory weights: K(t) = (2σ_s²/B) w(t)" (Eq. 2), i.e., "Neither the integration process nor the boundedness of the integration per se causes a systematic deviation of psychophysical kernels from true sensory weights." Fig. 3e: stimulus-aligned kernels in RT tasks are flat for stationary weights; Fig. 3f: "response-aligned kernels ... rise monotonically ... as expected for termination with bound crossing"; Fig. 3g: fixed-duration kernels "show a monotonic decrease because later stimuli are less likely to influence the choice". — [Okazawa et al. 2018, Nat Commun, DOI 10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)

**(a) Perfect integration with fixed bounds**
- Wong, Huk, Shadlen & Wang (2007), linearised one-variable analysis (Results, "Reverberation, not leak, gives rise to the observed violation of TSI"): for a stable leaky process "dS/dt ≈ −(S − S_ss)/τ + p(t)" with a step pulse of height p from T to T + Tp, "ΔS = pτ e^{(T−t)/τ}(e^{Tp/τ} − 1)"; "In the limit of large τ, as in a perfect integrator (diffusion) model, ... ≈ pTp, which is independent of the pulse onset time T, but dependent on the integral (total area) of the pulse pTp. This result is consistent with our initial hypothesis that the effect of a fleeting motion pulse on a perfect integrator (or a diffusion model) is time-shift invariant." — [Wong et al. 2007, Front Comput Neurosci, DOI 10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007](https://doi.org/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007)
- Zhou et al. 2009, Fig. 2A (CD): "CD and TD show near-constant changes for T/τ0 ≤ 0.5 that are substantially greater for CD"; Fig. 3 (SD of DT): "Both CD and TD exhibit similar near-constant changes for early onsets, but the directions of changes for TD are opposite to those for CD, and the curves cross before reaching maxima." Zero-effect pulse–antipulse (ZEP): for CD "antisymmetric pulses with λ* = 1, in which the pulses precisely cancel, will produce ZEPs" (Eq. 14–15 with k = 0; simulated λ* = 1.0013, Table 2). — [Zhou et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)
- Huk & Shadlen (2005) define time-shift invariance as "pulses exerting equal effects regardless of when they were presented"; their simulated perfect bounded integrator (Table 1: bound 68 spikes/s, MT latency 100 ms, LIP latency 125 ms, post-decision time 150 ms; pulse represented as a 100-ms change of MT rate) showed only a weak decline of the LIP pulse effect with onset time (−0.1 spikes/s per 100 ms; 95% CI −0.4 to 0.1; p = 0.29) against −0.6 spikes/s per 100 ms in LIP data (95% CI −0.9 to −0.3; p < 0.001; Fig. 10B, Eq. 10), and they conclude "bounded integration is not sufficient to explain the weak impact of later pulses on the LIP responses." Behaviourally, "Positive and negative pulses simply scaled and shifted the RT distributions seen on no-pulse trials" (KS tests p > 0.6). — [Huk & Shadlen 2005, J Neurosci, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005) (full text read via PMC6725829 in three passes; quotations from the WebFetch extraction)
- Kiani, Hanks & Shadlen (2008), fixed-duration task with bounded perfect accumulation ("the perfect accumulator that reaches its bound first, even if the motion has not terminated"; fitted k = 0.46, B = 19.9): later pulses are ineffective because "information is simply ignored once the decision bound is attained"; and "pulses should remain effective at later times for weaker motion than for stronger motion" (Fig. S2). — [Kiani et al. 2008, J Neurosci, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4761-07.2008](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4761-07.2008)
- Dependence on base coherence (perfect integrator): Huk & Shadlen state the pulse effect "was approximately additive with the effects of dot motion coherence", that pulses "have weaker effects at higher coherence because accumulation on those trials is closer to the bound at pulse onset", and expect "deviations from additivity to become more apparent for later pulses and for responses measured longer after the pulse" (Discussion; LIP: β1 = −2.5 spikes/s per 100% coherence, 95% CI −4.6 to −0.3, Fig. 10A, Eq. 9). — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005)
- Pulse strength/duration: for a robust (soft-threshold) integrator, Cain, Barreiro, Shadlen & Shea-Brown (2013) predict "that brief, stronger pulses will have greater impact on decision accuracy than longer, weaker pulses containing the same total evidence" (Discussion), in contrast to the area-only dependence of linear integration. — [Cain et al. 2013, J Neurophysiol, DOI 10.1152/jn.00976.2012](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00976.2012)

**(b) Stable OU (leak toward the starting point)**
- Bogacz et al. (2006), Eq. 10: "dx = (λx + A)dt + c dW, x(0) = 0"; for λ < 0 the fixed point −A/λ is an attractor with stationary density "N(−A/λ, c/√(−2λ))" (Eq. 11); "negative λ produces a recency or decay effect over the course of a trial because later inputs influence accumulated evidence more than earlier ones, whereas positive λ produces a primacy effect (earlier inputs have more influence)" (section "Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (O-U) Model"); Fig. 11a explanation: "When λ < 0 ... the effect of inputs decays over time, so that the most recent inputs have the biggest impact on the current state of the model." Interrogation error rate "ER(T) = Φ(−(A/c)·√(2(e^{λT} − 1)/(λ(e^{λT} + 1))))" (Eq. 12); free-response ER/DT expressions are Eqs. A55–A56 with asymptotics A64–A65; for λ ≤ 0 ER → 0 as thresholds → ∞ but "for λ < 0, as the thresholds are increased to achieve low ERs, DTs become excessively long" (Appendix Eq. A59; section "Free-Response Paradigm"). — [Bogacz et al. 2006, Psychol Rev, DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.113.4.700](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.113.4.700) (PDF at https://sites.engineering.ucsb.edu/~moehlis/moehlis_papers/psych.pdf)
- Wong et al. 2007: "intuitively one expects that a leak would lead to a violation of TSI in the opposite direction, namely the impact of a brief pulse should be larger with a later onset time ... This is because an earlier pulse is gradually 'forgotten' due to the leak"; from ΔS = pτ e^{(T−t)/τ}(e^{Tp/τ} − 1), "for a fixed pulse duration Tp, the factor e^{T/τ} is a monotonically increasing function of the pulse onset time T ... However, if the pulse is presented too late, the pulse effect should start to decrease, because now the ramping activity is truncated when the decision threshold is reached." — [Wong et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007)
- Zhou et al. 2009, Fig. 2C: "SOU exhibits a maximum at T/τ0 ≈ 0.5 ('optimal' perturbation)"; "due to thresholding, SOU is alone in having an onset time for which the perturbative effect is maximized"; SD changes "have a similar pattern to its mean" (Fig. 3C). ZEP: "responses to inputs decay with time for SOU, implying that the first pulse must be larger than the second for their net effect to cancel ... (λ* > 1)"; Eq. 15 "λ* = e^{−kΔT/2}" (k = −1, ΔT = 0.4 → λ* = 1.2214 theory, 1.2251 simulation; Table 2). — [Zhou et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)
- Okazawa et al. 2018, Fig. 6j–l: "when leak dominates, the integrators lose information and psychophysical kernels systematically underestimate the sensory weights, especially for earlier sensory evidence in the trial"; "When leak and inhibition balance each other out, the model acts similarly to a line attractor and the psychophysical kernels resemble those of a DDM." Model (Methods, competing accumulators): "d[v1; v2] = (w(t)S(t) − L[v1; v2] − I[v2; v1] + v0(L + I))dt + dW", "In the absence of mutual inhibition, the leak makes the model behave as an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process". — [Okazawa et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)
- Tsetsos, Gao, McClelland & Usher (2012), Eq. 3 (linear regime of the LCA, Eq. 1): "Δx = I − (k − β)x + N(0, √2σ)"; "When leak exceeds inhibition ... Since evidence that arrives early has a longer time to leak away than the information that arrives late, late information overweighs early information ... causing the recency effect"; Kiani et al.'s "leaky accumulation model predicts that late information is more important (a pattern called recency) ... This contrasts with the BD model, which predicts ... primacy". — [Tsetsos et al. 2012, Front Neurosci, DOI 10.3389/fnins.2012.00079](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2012.00079)
- Huk & Shadlen 2005 (Discussion) judge leak "less easily reconciled with the data": leak "can shorten pulse persistence but should not specifically weaken later pulses". — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005)
- Stine et al. (2020) formalise leaky integration as the OU process "dx = (−λx + μ)dt + ξ dt" with "λ = τ^{-1}" (Eq. 15) and note that "the time-constant becomes less identifiable as its value approaches and exceeds the longest decision times" (Results, Fig. 7A; parameter recovery good "especially when the data-generating time-constant was less than 1 second", Fig. 7—figure supplement 1). — [Stine et al. 2020, eLife, DOI 10.7554/eLife.55365](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365)

**(c) Unstable OU (self-excitation / attractor-like)**
- Wong et al. 2007: "If the integrator is exponentially unstable ... dS/dt ≈ (S − S_ss)/τ + p(t) in which case the pulse effect becomes ΔS = pτ e^{(−T+t)/τ}(1 − e^{−Tp/τ}). Therefore, the change in the activity decreases with pulse onset time" (Results); full attractor model: "the effect of the pulse is weaker when its onset time is later (Figure 8) ... for both the neural responses (Figure 8A) and choice behavior (Figure 8B)"; double-pulse protocol (Fig. 10, pulses at 150 and 250 ms): "the effect of the double pulse is not the same as the sum of the individual pulses ... the effect is dominated by the first pulse"; "The sum of single pulse effects would be cancelation were it not for the violation of TSI." — [Wong et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007)
- Zhou et al. 2009: UOU "shows monotonic decrease" of the mean-DT effect with onset (Fig. 2D); SD "exhibits initial decreases followed by increases to a peak" (Fig. 3D); ZEP λ* = e^{−kΔT/2} < 1 (k = 0.2, ΔT = 1.0 → 0.9048 theory, 0.9050 simulation; Table 2). The nonlinear subcritical-pitchfork model (Eq. 8, "SPB") "behaves in a manner similar to the unstable OU model under both perturbation protocols" (Discussion; Figs. 7–8; λ* ≈ 0.87). — [Zhou et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)
- Tsetsos et al. 2012: inhibition-dominant LCA "results in primacy (Figure 4B)"; Fig. 9 informal simulation (β = 0.15, k = 0.05, pulse of 40 time steps): "The effect diminishes as the time of the pulse onset increases"; footnote 4: the attractor model is expected to have "similar predictions as the inhibition-dominant LCA, as both have unstable Ornstein–Uhlenbeck dynamics"; the nonlinear (floor at 0) LCA can also produce "a transition from primacy at short durations to recency at long durations" (Fig. 5), which "does not occur in the linear case". Okazawa 2018 Fig. 6j–l: "When mutual inhibition dominates (leak/inhibition ratio < 1), psychophysical kernels show an early amplification but later converge on the true sensory weights." — [Tsetsos et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2012.00079); [Okazawa et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)

**(d) Urgency gain / collapsing bound with perfect integration**
- Equivalences: Murphy, Boonstra & Nieuwenhuis (2016), Methods: an additive urgency DDM "is mathematically identical to a model in which the standard DDM is coupled with time-varying decision bounds". Okazawa et al. 2018: "Urgency is effectively a reduction of decision bound over time (Fig. 6a) and leads to inflation of the psychophysical kernel (Fig. 6b)"; their urgency implementation is a hyperbolic bound "B(t) = b − u∞ t/(t + τ_{1/2})" (Eq. 15), "the rate of bound collapse is defined by τ_{1/2}—the time it takes to have a 50% drop in bound height" (Fig. 6 legend). Hawkins, Forstmann et al. (2015) implement urgency as a three-parameter logistic gain "applied as a gain (i.e., multiplicative) function on drift rates" (Fig. 2 caption; Eq. 2, following Ditterich 2006a) and state that rapid and delayed urgency are "functionally similar to early and late collapsing boundaries"; their collapsing bound is a Weibull cumulative function (Eq. 1; shape k, scale λ, asymptote a′). Stine et al. 2020 use a logistic collapse "B(t) = ±B0(1 + e^{a(t−d)})^{-1}" (Eq. 3). — [Murphy et al. 2016, Nat Commun, DOI 10.1038/ncomms13526](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms13526); [Okazawa et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y); [Hawkins, Forstmann et al. 2015, J Neurosci, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2410-14.2015](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2410-14.2015); [Stine et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365)
- Pulse-onset signature: Zhou et al.'s TD model (drift b0·t, pulse not scaled by the gain) behaves like CD for early pulses ("CD and TD show near-constant changes for T/τ0 ≤ 0.5", Fig. 2A–B) with smaller magnitude, and ZEP λ* = 1 "for all time-dependent drift rates, including those used in Smith et al., 2004; Ditterich, 2006b; Churchland et al., 2008" (Eq. 16); the SD-of-DT change has the opposite sign from CD for early pulses (Fig. 3B). If the pulse passes through the same gain as the stimulus (Appendix B, Eq. 28: "dX_t = (b0 + p(t))t dt + σ dW_t"), the effects are "smaller ... near the beginning of the trial (quite similar to a SOU model)" (Fig. 9). — [Zhou et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)
- Kernel signature: collapsing bound "inflates the psychophysical kernel over time" (Okazawa Fig. 6a–c); and because non-decision time pulls kernels down, "The gradual reduction of the kernel due to non-decision time can cancel out the increase of the kernel due to urgency" (Fig. 7b–d: decreasing, increasing or flat kernels from the same static weights). — [Okazawa et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)
- Wong et al. 2007 (Discussion): a bound that "decreases in time, presumably as a result of an urgency signal ... Qualitatively this would be equivalent to acceleration of the ramping activity (while the decision threshold is fixed), which naturally results from recurrent excitatory dynamics"; they propose the double-pulse protocol "might help to differentiate the model proposed here from alternative models such as the diffusion model with a collapsing bound or urgency." Zhou 2009 (Discussion) claim the opposite: "both perturbation protocols can distinguish linear and nonlinear UOU-type models ... from drift-diffusion models with an 'urgent' time-dependent increase in drift rate" (via λ* = 1 vs λ* < 1 and the onset profiles of Fig. 2B vs 2D). — [Wong et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007); [Zhou et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)
- Normative origin of collapse (abstract only): Drugowitsch et al. 2012 find that the cost of accumulating evidence "features a brief period in which it is constant but increases thereafter" and "predicts that urgency signals in the brain should be independent of the difficulty". — [Drugowitsch et al. 2012, J Neurosci, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4010-11.2012](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4010-11.2012) (abstract only; full text blocked)

**(e) Urgency-gating model with a short low-pass filter (Cisek/Thura UGM)**
- Cisek, Puskas & El-Murr (2009): integrator models "x_i(t)" grow at a rate set by evidence until threshold (Eq. 1); UGM: neural activity is "the product of the momentary evidence E_i(t) and a signal u(t)" (Eq. 2); model 5 sets u(t) to elapsed time; model 6 low-pass filters the evidence "using a linear differential equation with a time constant of τ = 200 ms" before multiplying by urgency; "With constant evidence and u(t) = t, the two models are equivalent." On pulses: Huk & Shadlen "presented similar motion pulses during the RT task and obtained a much longer window" than the ~300–350 ms window inferred from Kiani et al. 2008; "It remains unclear why such different results were obtained in these two studies" (Discussion). — [Cisek et al. 2009, J Neurosci, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1844-09.2009](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1844-09.2009)
- Carland, Thura & Cisek (2015): leaky integrator "dx/dt = −Lx + gE" (Eq. 1) rewritten as "τ dx/dt = −x + (g/L)E" with "τ = 1/L" (Eq. 2); the zero-time-constant limit "x = hE" (Eq. 3) is Winkel et al.'s filter-less UGM; decision variable "y(t) = x(t)·U(t)" with "U(t) = (b + mt)η" (Eq. 4; η = inter-trial noise on urgency), threshold T, RT = t + t0; fitted "leak (L = 0.004, corresponding to a time constant of 250 ms)", T = 16300, NDD = 481 ms, g = 0.03. Prediction for early evidence: "when the urgency signal is steep, decisions will sometimes be made before the early information has fully leaked out, resulting in a shift in the RT only for those trials. When the urgency signal is shallower, the total decision-related activity will not reach threshold until after the early information has fully leaked out"; "individual response times are only affected by early evidence in a subset of trials in which the decision was made within a certain time window of the change in evidence"; "With a 250-ms time constant the filter equilibrates to within 15% of its asymptote in about 500 ms"; "nearly all of the activity build-up over time can be attributed to the growth of the urgency signal, rather than the integration of evidence". — [Carland et al. 2015, Psychon Bull Rev, DOI 10.3758/s13423-015-0851-2](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0851-2)
- Evans, Hawkins, Boehm, Wagenmakers & Brown (2017), simulation equations: DDM "x_{t+Δ} ↦ x_t + vCΔ + s w √Δ" (Eq. 1); UGM "x_{t+Δ} ↦ (τ/(τ+Δ)) x_t + (Δ/(τ+Δ))(vCΔ + s w √Δ)" (Eq. 2), then the accumulator is "multiplied by the time measured in milliseconds since the onset of evidence accumulation (t − t0)" with threshold at ±a; τ fixed at 100 ms, or free: fitted τ had mean 4105 ms (SD 2625) across subjects (Table 1). Qualitative prediction: "as the time between early evidence and the decision increases ... the UGM predicts that early evidence will have a decreasing effect on decisions, whereas the diffusion model predicts the effect of early evidence to be constant" (Data Analysis). — [Evans et al. 2017, Sci Rep, DOI 10.1038/s41598-017-16694-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-16694-7)
- Hawkins, Wagenmakers, Ratcliff & Brown (2015): UGM uses urgency "μ(t) = βt", β = 1, filter time constant 100 ms (footnote 3: Cisek et al. 2009 used 200 ms); the discriminating terms are "∫0^t θ" for the accumulator and "t × ∫0^t θ" for the UGM; the accumulator's standard deviation grows with √t, the UGM's with t^{3/2}; "the models are not identical, even when both models are augmented with realistic extra assumptions"; the UGM's low-pass filtering "leads to a gradual decrease in information over time". — [Hawkins et al. 2015, J Neurophysiol, DOI 10.1152/jn.00088.2015](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00088.2015)
- Carland, Marcos, Thura & Cisek (2016) (abstract only): "In the blocked condition, early pulses had a strong effect on RTs but late pulses did not, consistent with both models. However, when subjects slowed their decision policy in the interleaved condition, later pulses now became effective while early pulses lost their efficacy. This last result contradicts models based on perfect integration of sensory evidence and implies that motion signals are processed with a strong leak, equivalent to a low-pass filter with a short time constant." — [Carland et al. 2016, J Neurophysiol, DOI 10.1152/jn.00264.2015](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00264.2015)
- Thura, Beauregard-Racine, Fradet & Cisek (2012) (abstract only): "the policy that maximizes reward rate is to estimate evidence by accumulating only novel information and then compare the result to a decreasing accuracy criterion. We suggest that the brain approximates this policy by multiplying an estimate of sensory evidence with a motor-related urgency signal". — [Thura et al. 2012, J Neurophysiol, DOI 10.1152/jn.01071.2011](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.01071.2011)

### Inferences
- Decision-variable response to a pulse of height p and duration Δ at onset T (from the cited equations; "t" is time after pulse offset): perfect integrator δ = pΔ, permanent (Wong 2007; Zhou Eq. 16); stable OU δ(t) = (p/|k|)(1 − e^{−|k|Δ}) e^{−|k|(t−T−Δ)} (Wong 2007's ΔS with τ = 1/|k|; Zhou Eqs. 12–13); unstable OU δ(t) grows as e^{k(t−T−Δ)} (Wong 2007); UGM (Carland Eq. 1–4 / Evans Eq. 2): filtered-evidence displacement x ≈ p(1 − e^{−Δ/τ}) e^{−(t−T−Δ)/τ} multiplied by urgency U(t) = b + mt, so the DV displacement is ∝ (b + mt)·e^{−(t−T−Δ)/τ}; collapsing bound a(t) with perfect integration: δ = pΔ permanent, but the effective displacement relative to the remaining bound is δ/a(t), which grows as the bound collapses (consistent with Okazawa's kernel inflation, Fig. 6a–c).
- Pooled choice effect vs pulse ONSET (i): all classes show the thresholding decline ∝ fraction of trials still undecided when the pulse arrives (Zhou Sec. 3.1; Wong 2007; Kiani 2008). On top of this: perfect integration and urgency-on-drift are flat (TSI) before the decline (Zhou Fig. 2A–B); stable OU rises then falls, giving an interior maximum (Zhou Fig. 2C; Wong 2007); unstable OU/attractor falls monotonically (Zhou Fig. 2D; Wong Fig. 8); UGM rises with onset in proportion to U(T) for decisions made within ~τ of the pulse, and otherwise follows the thresholding decline (Carland 2015 Fig. 5 logic). A collapsing bound with perfect integration should increase the per-undecided-trial effect with onset (δ/a(T) grows), i.e., the opposite sign of onset dependence from leak-before-thresholding is not what distinguishes them; rather, leak and collapsing bound both can make later pulses relatively more effective per undecided trial, so onset profiles alone are weakly diagnostic unless the thresholding decline is modelled (this is my reading of the cited results; no paper directly contrasts SOU and collapsing-bound onset profiles).
- Choice effect vs TIME-TO-DECISION (ii): my derivation for the zero-drift symmetric DDM with absorbing bounds ±a and no leak (standard eigenfunction expansion of the first-passage problem, φ_n(x) = sin(nπ(x+a)/2a), decay rates μ_n = n²π²σ²/(8a²)): a small displacement δ changes the antisymmetric (even-n) modes, so the choice bias among trials deciding at time t_d after the pulse decays as e^{−(μ_2 − μ_1)(t_d − T)} = e^{−3π²σ²(t_d − T)/(8a²)}, i.e., with time constant 8a²/(3π²σ²) ≈ 0.27 × (mean zero-coherence decision time a²/σ²), while the stimulus-aligned, undecided-pooled effect stays flat (Okazawa Eq. 2). So even a perfect integrator predicts a decaying conditional effect ("bound depletion" in the conditional sense), with a time constant set by bound height; leak adds e^{−|k|(t_d−T)} to this, urgency/collapse shortens the effective a(t) and thus accelerates the decay late in the trial while inflating the early per-unit effect. This should be verified by simulation before use; it is not stated in any source read.
- RT distribution (iii): in a perfect integrator the pulse shifts the undecided-trial first-passage density toward (away from) the pulse-direction bound; Huk & Shadlen's observation that pulses "simply scaled and shifted the RT distributions" is what a displacement of the DV predicts; Zhou's Figs. 2–3 give the model-specific mean and SD changes; with errors present, "the change in mean error RTs can reverse sign for late perturbation onsets" because "perturbation of long RT trials can change an impending error to a correct choice" (Zhou Sec. 3.3, Fig. 5A). A symmetric latency change pooled over pulse signs is predicted to be near zero for small pulses in all linear models (positive and negative pulses give mirror-image mean-DT changes in Zhou Fig. 2 at early onsets); asymmetry arises from errors (Fig. 5) and from nonlinearity (SPB, Fig. 7).
- Base coherence and pulse strength (iv): linear models depend on pulse area (perfect integrator exactly, OU via (1 − e^{−|k|Δ})/|k|); the effect per undecided trial shrinks when the DV is near a bound, which happens earlier at high coherence (Huk & Shadlen Discussion; Kiani Fig. S2), so the onset at which the thresholding decline begins moves earlier with coherence; UGM effects scale with the filtered-evidence displacement times U at decision, independent of base coherence except through decision timing; soft-threshold integrators favour brief strong pulses over long weak ones (Cain 2013).

## Key question 2: What Zhou, Wong-Lin & Holmes (2009) derived and concluded; where separation failed; what they recommended

### Takeaway
Zhou et al. show by simulation (10^6 trials per condition, Euler–Maruyama, step 10^-3) and proofs that single pulses with varying onset and pulse–antipulse pairs separate the pure DDM (and the time-dependent-drift DDM) from stable and unstable OU processes through the dependence of mean and SD of decision time on onset and through the zero-effect amplitude ratio λ* = e^{−kΔT/2}; constant- vs time-dependent-drift DDMs are separable only through small SD effects. Separation fails at low signal-to-noise (error rates of 3–5% already mask the SOU optimum and the CD plateau), and a "dead time" before integration makes CD/TD mimic SOU; they recommend easily discriminable stimuli, early pulses, long response deadlines and analysis restricted to long-RT trials.

## Key question 3: Ditterich (2006a, EJN; 2006b, Neural Networks) on pulses and time-variant gain

### Takeaway
Only the abstracts of both Ditterich papers were accessible (Wiley and Elsevier blocked; no open copy via OpenAlex/Europe PMC). From the abstracts and from secondary descriptions (Zhou 2009; Hawkins et al. 2015; Asadpour et al. 2024; Stine et al. 2020), Ditterich argued that a diffusion model whose sensory gain increases over time explains the psychometric function, mean RTs, the shape of RT distributions and slow errors, fits LIP activity best, is reward-rate optimal under fixation-break risk, and that a large class of models (with and without integration, stationary and time-variant) fit behaviour, with the integration time constant "not well-constrained". No accessible source states what Ditterich concluded about pulses specifically.

### Cited Findings
- Ditterich 2006a (abstract only): "While a simple diffusion model can explain the psychometric function and the mean response times of correct responses, it fails to account for the longer response times observed for errors and for the response time distributions. Here I demonstrate that a time-variant version of the diffusion model can explain the psychometric function, the mean response times and the shape of the response time distributions. Such a time-variant mechanism could be implemented in different ways, but the best match between the physiological data and model predictions is provided by a diffusion process with a gain of the sensory signals, which increases over time. It can be shown that such a time-variant decision process allows the monkey to perform optimally (in the sense of maximizing reward rate) given the risk of aborting a trial by breaking fixation". — [Ditterich 2006a, Eur J Neurosci, DOI 10.1111/j.1460-9568.2006.05221.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-9568.2006.05221.x)
- Ditterich 2006b (abstract only): "I will demonstrate that a relatively large class of models, both with and without temporal integration and both stationary and time-variant could account for the behavioral data. Both the single cell recordings from the parietal cortex and previously published data from the extrastriate visual cortex provide additional constraints. Overall, the data favor a diffusion model with time-variant gain and leaky integrators. The integration time constant, however, turns out not to be well-constrained by the data." — [Ditterich 2006b, Neural Netw, DOI 10.1016/j.neunet.2006.05.042](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neunet.2006.05.042)

Ditterich, 2006b" (Sec. 3.2). — [Zhou et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)

### Inferences
- Under a gain on drift and noise that increases with time (Ditterich's model 4 as described by Asadpour et al.), a pulse delivered through the sensory pathway at onset T is amplified by γ(T), so later pulses produce larger DV displacements per undecided trial; combined with the thresholding decline this yields a non-monotonic onset profile that can mimic SOU (as Zhou's Appendix B, Eq. 28, shows for a linear gain). On choice vs time-to-decision, a permanent displacement with a fixed bound gives the same conditional decay as the pure DDM (Key question 1 inference), scaled by γ(T).

## Key question 4: Hawkins et al. 2015 (J Neurophysiol; J Neurosci) and the relation between urgency gating, collapsing bounds and perfect integration; identifiability

### Takeaway
"Integrating accumulated evidence into urgency" does not exist as a title (searched Google-indexed sources, Europe PMC, OpenAlex); the relevant Hawkins papers are the 2015 J Neurophysiol ("Discriminating evidence accumulation from urgency signals ...") and 2015 J Neurosci ("Revisiting the evidence for collapsing boundaries ...") papers, plus Evans et al. 2017 and Trueblood et al. 2021. The theory they establish: an additive or multiplicative urgency on the accumulated evidence with fixed bounds is mathematically identical to a DDM with time-varying bounds (Murphy 2016; Hawkins 2015 Fig. 2), whereas the UGM is not identical to an accumulator even with constant evidence because its noise variance grows as t³ rather than t (Hawkins J Neurophysiol 2015); leak (filter time constant) is identifiable only in changing-evidence designs and becomes unidentifiable once it exceeds typical decision times (Stine 2020; Evans 2017 free-τ fits; Trueblood 2021 abstract).

## Key question 5: Bound depletion / time-shift invariance conditional on RT, and how bounds, leak, urgency and delays distort time-resolved effects

### Takeaway
The published theory separates two statistics: (1) the stimulus-aligned effect pooled over trials still undecided when the pulse arrives, which is flat (time-shift invariant) for a perfect integrator with fixed bounds (Okazawa Eq. 2; Wong 2007) and declines in the full trial pool only because decided trials no longer respond (Zhou "thresholding"; Kiani 2008; Wong 2007 "truncation"); and (2) response-aligned effects, which in a bounded integrator rise toward the decision (Okazawa Fig. 3f), are zero within the non-decision time before the response, and peak at roughly the non-decision time when that time is variable (Fig. 3k). Huk & Shadlen's RT-conditioned analysis (Fig. 4) found choice effects only for responses 300–900 ms after pulse onset. Leak removes early evidence (kernel underestimates early weights), urgency/collapse inflates late weights, non-decision time pulls late weights down, and mixtures can produce flat, rising or falling profiles (Okazawa Fig. 7). No paper derives a reversal of the conditional effect at long RTs.

### Cited Findings
- Huk & Shadlen 2005, Fig. 4 / Eq. 8: trials binned into 75-ms relative-RT bins (RT minus pulse onset); choices "∼300 ms after pulse onset" were affected; earlier responses "showed no significant effect of the pulse"; "Choices made up to 900 ms after a pulse were affected in a direction-selective manner" (Fig. 4 legend); nothing reported for responses beyond 900 ms; "the choice effects lagged the LIP changes by about 75–100 ms"; LIP pulse effects "became evident ∼225 ms after the onset of the pulse and persisted until ∼800 ms after the pulse"; no behavioural regression on onset time was reported (Eq. 10 applies to LIP only). The pulse shifted the psychometric function by "±1.6% coherent motion" (γ = 1.60%, 95% CI 1.4–1.7%; Table 2) and "Positive-direction pulses sped up RTs for preferred-target choices and slowed down RTs for null-target choices" (Fig. 3B). — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005)
- Wong et al. 2007: in their simulations "Trials were not taken into account if the decision threshold was crossed before motion pulse onset" (Data analysis); "pulses shown at different time during motion stimulus may induce nonlinear effects as a result of truncation of the firing rate at the decision bound" (motivating the double-pulse protocol); "To differentiate a linear diffusion-like model with a fixed decision bound and a nonlinear recurrent circuit model, the single-pulse protocol is not ideal because the truncation of firing rate by a decision threshold inevitably introduces nonlinear effects." — [Wong et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007)
- Kiani et al. 2008: pulses (±3.2%, 200 ms) biased choices by 1.3 ± 0.1% coherence overall; for trials ≥ 700 ms split by pulse-time quartile, "the early pulses caused significantly larger biases than the late ones" (p = 0.018; Eq. 5 α4 = −0.40 ± 0.19); third-quartile shift 0.2 ± 0.4% (p = 0.26), fourth −0.1 ± 0.4% (p = 0.63) (Fig. 5B–F); interpretation: "information is simply ignored once the decision bound is attained"; the leaky alternative, predicting that "the information acquired later in a trial should exert a larger influence", was rejected (BIC 59.5; Bayes factor 8.35 × 10^12). — [Kiani et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4761-07.2008)
- Zhou et al. 2009 (Sec. 3.3): with two thresholds and 5% errors, correct and error trials "respond to the perturbations in different manners"; "the change in mean error RTs can reverse sign for late perturbation onsets ... perturbation of long RT trials can change an impending error to a correct choice". — [Zhou et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)

- Carland et al. 2015 (UGM): "individual response times are only affected by early evidence in a subset of trials in which the decision was made within a certain time window of the change in evidence. Other trials, in which the decision was made later, will otherwise show no difference in response time because any early evidence will have leaked out by the time the decision is made. Crucially, however, the mean RT for all trials will still undergo a significant shift" (Interpreting model fits; Fig. 5). — [Carland et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0851-2)

### Inferences
- "Bound depletion" has two distinct theoretical faces. (A) Pooled-over-trials, stimulus-aligned: the effect declines with onset because decided trials are inert (all classes; Zhou, Kiani, Wong). (B) Conditional on decision time: for a fixed-bound perfect integrator the effect of a displacement on the choice made at t_d decays with (t_d − T) at rate (μ_2 − μ_1) = 3π²σ²/(8a²) (my eigenmode derivation, Key question 1), consistent with Okazawa's response-aligned kernel rising toward the decision (Fig. 3f) and with Huk & Shadlen's finite 300–900 ms window. A late *reversal* is not predicted by the leading modes in the symmetric zero-drift case; reversals in Zhou's simulations arise for error RTs (pulses converting impending errors into correct choices) and would arise in choice-conditional analyses when pulse and no-pulse trials are compared at matched RT with non-zero drift (sign depends on the DV distribution of late-deciding trials; not derived in any source).
- Non-decision time predicts the absence of effects for responses within t_nd + sensory latency of the pulse (Okazawa Fig. 3j–k; Huk & Shadlen: effects begin ~300 ms after pulse onset), so the earliest usable time-to-decision bin is set by the non-decision time distribution, and its variability smears the onset of the conditional effect.
- Under leak, the conditional decay rate is 3π²σ²/(8a²) + |k| (sum of depletion and leak); under urgency/collapse the effective a(t) shrinks so the depletion rate increases with time while the per-unit effect is inflated — so an observed cut-off of the conditional effect at ~1.3 s (as in our data) is compatible with several mechanisms and must be calibrated by simulation of each fitted model with the free pulse gain.

### Gaps
- No analytic treatment of the choice effect conditional on RT exists in the sources; Huk & Shadlen report only the 300–900 ms window with no data beyond; the model-based prediction for this window is not shown in their paper (only LIP time courses).
- Kiani et al. 2008 is a fixed-duration task; its quantitative bound-depletion profile does not transfer directly to RT tasks.

## Key question 6: Closed-form or simulation results for the pulse-induced choice shift versus time since the pulse under OU leak vs collapsing bound

### Takeaway
Closed forms exist only for the decision-variable displacement (Wong 2007 for stable/unstable OU; Zhou 2009 Eqs. 12–16 for the early-pulse ZEP condition λ* = e^{−kΔT/2}) and for the proportionality of stimulus-aligned kernels to sensory weights in a fixed-bound DDM (Okazawa Eq. 2). Bogacz et al. 2006 give OU error-rate formulas (Eq. 12 interrogation; Appendix A55–A56 free response) and the recency/primacy logic but no pulse formula. Collapsing-bound effects on time-resolved weights are available only as simulations (Okazawa Fig. 6a–c; Fig. 7). No source gives the choice shift as a function of time since the pulse for either leak or collapse in free-response tasks; Drugowitsch 2012 (abstract only) and Evans et al. 2020 do not address pulses.

### Cited Findings
- Wong et al. 2007: ΔS = pτ e^{(T−t)/τ}(e^{Tp/τ} − 1) (stable) and ΔS = pτ e^{(−T+t)/τ}(1 − e^{−Tp/τ}) (unstable), perfect-integrator limit ΔS ≈ pTp (Results, "Reverberation, not leak"). — [Wong et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007)
- Zhou et al. 2009: Eqs. 12–16 and λ* = e^{−kΔT/2} (Sec. 3.2); "Asymptotic methods can be used to approximate passage time moments for small perturbations (Lindner, 2004), but the lack of compact formulae for passage time distributions (PDFs) in most cases, including OU processes, and the need for double integrations (Lindner, 2004) makes this approach generally intractable"; "general analytical expressions for systems with finite noise appear elusive" (Discussion). — [Zhou et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)
- Bogacz et al. 2006: OU Eqs. 10–12; free-response ER and DT "derived by Busemeyer and Townsend (1992); we give them as Equations A55 and A56 in Appendix A, along with asymptotic approximations A64 and A65"; recency/primacy by sign of λ. — [Bogacz et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.113.4.700)
- Okazawa et al. 2018: Eq. 2 K(t) = (2σ_s²/B)w(t) (fixed bound, no non-decision time); unbounded DDM analogue (Eq. 14, scaling 4σ_s²/(√(2π)σ_tot)); for dynamic bounds "we used the average bound height from the stimulus onset to the median RT to calculate the scaling factor" (Methods); hyperbolic collapse Eq. 15; leak/inhibition model equation (Methods). — [Okazawa et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)
- Stine et al. 2020: numerical Fokker–Planck (Chang–Cooper) propagation for leaky integration with the leak applied as a per-step contraction "x′ = x e^{−Δt/λ}" [sic; their notation] and collapsing bounds Eq. 3 — a usable recipe for computing choice-conditional pulse effects numerically; FSD kernels from 100-ms pulses via logistic regression (Eq. 16; Fig. 2B shows that integration, extrema detection and snapshot all yield flat kernels in a 1-s fixed-duration task). — [Stine et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365)
- Usher & McClelland 2001 (abstract only): "Special cases of the model match a classical diffusion process, but leakage and competition work together to address several challenges to existing diffusion, random walk, and accumulator models ... A new paradigm that controls the time of arrival of information supporting different choice alternatives provides further support." — [Usher & McClelland 2001, Psychol Rev, DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.108.3.550](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.108.3.550)
- Drugowitsch et al. 2012 (abstract only): no pulse or time-varying-evidence analysis is mentioned. — [Drugowitsch et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4010-11.2012)

### Inferences
- A calibrated test should therefore be simulation-based: for each fitted model (fixed bound; OU with λ; collapsing bound a/(1 + u·t)), with a free pulse gain, compute (1) the pooled effect vs onset, (2) the effect vs time-to-decision (bins of RT − pulse onset, excluding the non-decision time), and (3) the pulse–antipulse cancellation ratio λ*, and compare the three profiles; Zhou's λ* and Okazawa's kernel results give the sanity checks (λ* = 1 and flat stimulus-aligned effect for fixed-bound perfect integration; λ* > 1 and early-weight loss for leak; inflated late weights for collapse).
- The eigenmode decay of the conditional effect in a fixed-bound DDM (rate 3π²σ²/8a², my derivation) should be reproduced by such simulations as a check that conditional decay is not mistaken for leak.

### Gaps
- No closed-form expression for choice shift vs time since pulse under OU or collapsing bounds was found in any source; the Lindner (2004) perturbation approach cited by Zhou is noted as "generally intractable".
- Usher & McClelland 2001 and Bogacz Appendix A (A55–A65) were not read in full; the latter's free-response OU formulas are available in the PDF linked above but were not transcribed.
