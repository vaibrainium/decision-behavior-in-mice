# Identifiability, parameter/model recovery and experimental design for separating leaky integration from urgency / collapsing bounds (methods literature)

Scope: methods, identifiability and design literature only. Theory of pulse signatures, primate/human empirical pulse studies and rodent work are covered by other researchers; those papers appear here only where they state a design or identifiability point. Reading status is marked per source: "full text" (read locally or via full HTML), "partial" (sections read), or "abstract only". Quotes are verbatim from the text read. "Not reported" means the quantity was absent from the portions read.

Conventions used below: leak = Ornstein-Uhlenbeck decay of accumulated evidence (rate L, time constant 1/L); urgency = multiplicative time-varying gain (equivalently a collapsing bound); CT = collapsing threshold; UGM = urgency-gating model; NDT = non-decision time; PDA = probability density approximation (simulation-based kernel likelihood); DE-MCMC = differential-evolution MCMC.

## Q1. Evans, Trueblood & Holmes (2020, Behav Res Methods): which time-variant parameters are recoverable, at what trial counts, and what trades off?

### Takeaway
With 1,000 trials per condition and Bayesian PDA fitting, only the DDM with a *linearly* collapsing threshold recovered all of its parameters (drift, initial threshold, collapse rate); the leakage-formulated UGM recovered drift, threshold and leak (leak only when 1/L is shorter than decision times), but its urgency-strength parameter is mathematically unidentifiable; Weibull-collapse DDM, collapsing LBA and the filter-formulated UGM could not recover their threshold/urgency parameters. A single evidence switch at 250 ms helped only marginally. Trial-count sweeps were not run.

### Cited Findings
- Models assessed: "two versions of a collapse threshold DDM (CT DDM), a collapse threshold variant of the LBA model (CT LBA), and two variants of the UGM", DDM collapse forms linear (a_upper(t) = a0 − a_slope·t, Eq. 2) and Weibull; UGM variants "formulated in terms of a leakage process" vs "a low-pass filtering process"; bias fixed at z = 0 and no between-trial variabilities because those "are known to have recovery issues (Lerche & Voss, 2016)" (Models section, Fig. 1). Full text. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)
- Simulation design: Latin hypercube, "4000 parameter sets" per model; "a single synthetic data set was generated, with both a fixed- and changing-evidence condition, each with 1000 trials"; change of evidence "occurred after 250 ms, corresponding to ... a swap of the positive/negative sign in the DDM/UGM"; fits to constant evidence alone and to constant+changing jointly "with all parameters constrained over conditions"; Bayesian estimation with DE-MCMC and PDA using 10,000 simulated trials per likelihood evaluation (Methods; Appendix D). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)
- Inclusion filter (Appendix A: 0.4 < mean and median RT < 2.5 s, 0.1 < IQR < 2 s, min RT < 1.5 s, max RT > 1.5 s, plus condition-specific criteria) retained 2166/4000 sets for CT LBA (54.2%), 1239 for linear CT DDM (31%), 1030 for Weibull CT DDM (25.8%), 892 for filter UGM (22.3%), 437 for leakage UGM (11%); the authors note "it is possible that our choice of exclusion criteria could have influenced our recovery findings" (Methods; Appendix A). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)
- Three general trends (Results, Figs. 2–6): (i) "the difference in drift rate between alternatives ... recovers extremely well, regardless of the time-variant function"; (ii) "a clear link between the recovery of the initial threshold and the recovery of the time-variant parameters (i.e., the threshold collapse, or urgency signal), where time-variant and initial threshold parameters are either both recovered well, or both recovered poorly ... This suggests that a key tradeoff exists between the initial threshold and these time-variant parameters"; (iii) "the addition of a changing evidence condition appears to aide the recovery of the parameters for all models, though in most cases the improvement is fairly minor". — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)
- Model-specific: CT LBA "cannot recover the values of the drift rates themselves, the initial threshold, or the rate of threshold collapse"; Weibull CT DDM "cannot recover any of the threshold parameters adequately, with either fixed or changing evidence"; linear CT DDM "precisely recovers all parameters including the drift rate, the initial threshold, and the rate of threshold collapse ... when either fixed or changing evidence is considered, though the addition of changing evidence does somewhat improve the quality of recovery" (Results, Figs. 2–4). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)
- UGM: drift "precise in both cases"; "Small time constants and large leakage rates both recover well, while large time constants/small leakage rates do not ... large time constants/small leakage rates correspond to times that are longer than decision times themselves"; "while the threshold is recoverable in the leakage formulation, neither the threshold nor the urgency value (u) are recoverable in the filter version, with either fixed or changing information" (Results, Figs. 5–6). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)
- Appendix C ("Unidentifiability of urgency parameter in the leakage formulated UGM"): combining the two UGM equations gives dx = [−(L + 1/t)x + t(uv)]dt + t(uσ)dw (Eq. 8); "the parameter u effectively serves only to rescale the strength of evidence"; the change of variables x = uy "completely removes u from the equation, indicating it is not identifiable". — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)
- Recommendations (Discussion): "if parameter estimation is the goal, then researchers use either the CT DDM with a linear collapse, or the UGM with a leakage process. If the relative strength of time-varying caution/urgency are of primary importance, then the CT DDM should be used"; for other time-variant models "stick to the goal of model inference, that is contrasting different models based on how well they fit data, rather than basing conclusions on parameters"; "we strongly recommend that proper parameter recovery assessments are performed before using the parameters of any time-varying model for inference, as ... small changes to the formulation of a model can result in a lack of robustness of the parameter estimates". — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)
- Fit vs recovery: "the ability of a model to accurately fit a data set, and the ability of that model to precisely recover parameters are different. In the case of the three models that do not recover parameters, the 'best fit' parameters do still fit the generated data very well" (Discussion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)
- Changing evidence: "no model parameters qualitatively shifted from 'non-recoverable' to 'recoverable' due to the addition of changing evidence" (Discussion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)
- Secondary readings of this paper: Trueblood et al. (2021) cite it as showing that "many of the models of time varying urgency (see Hawkins, Forstmann, et al., 2015; Ditterich, 2006a, for example) are not identifiable" and that "nonlinearities typically increase the parameter indeterminacy or 'sloppiness'" (Introduction). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)

### Inferences
- The paper's 1,000-trial-per-condition design is comparable to or larger than the per-onset pulse-trial counts in the mouse data set (80–1,400 per onset), so its negative results for Weibull/filter-UGM parameters are not attributable to small samples; its positive result for the linear-collapse DDM is the relevant benchmark for a "gain (1 + u·t)" urgency parameterization (linear gain is the multiplicative counterpart of a hyperbolically collapsing bound, see Q4/Smith & Ratcliff).
- The threshold–collapse trade-off (trend ii) is the mechanism most likely behind the project's 35–45% recovery hit rate for leak/urgency from control trials: bound, urgency and leak all reshape the right tail.

### Gaps
- No trial-count sweep; recovery as a function of N is not reported. No NDT variability, no bias, no mixture/lapse components were included.
- The single evidence change was a sustained sign reversal at 250 ms, not a brief pulse; no pulse-duration or pulse-timing manipulations were tested in this paper.

## Q2. Trueblood, Heathcote, Evans & Holmes (2021, Psychol Rev): how were changing-evidence experiments designed to identify leak and urgency separately, what was identifiable, and what was found?

### Takeaway
They nondimensionalized a generalized UGM into a two-parameter family (urgency ratio k = m/b, leak L), showed by simulation that with fixed evidence "parameter recovery is not possible on the basis of choice and RT data alone" whereas a single within-trial evidence reversal at 0.2 s makes k and L recoverable (L only when 1/L is shorter than a trial), and then estimated k ≈ 1 and a leak time constant ≈ 200–250 ms in four flashing-grid experiments (~1,080 main trials/participant, half with a switch). Smith & Ratcliff (2022) read the same recoveries as "highly variable".

### Cited Findings
- Model (Eqs. 1–5): ỹ = x(t)U(t), dx = (−Lx + E0(t))dt + σdw, U(t) = b + mt; nondimensionalized to dy = [−(L + k/(1+kt))y + E0(t)(1+kt)]dt + σ(1+kt)dw with "urgency ratio" k = m/b; "k < 1 represents an integration dominated system while k > 1 is urgency dominated"; k = 0 "is precisely an evidence integrator, where the value of L determines the presence/importance of leakage"; k → ∞ gives the "pure UGM" of Cisek et al. (2009). Accepted-manuscript full text (UTAS repository). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)
- Recovery design: 3,000 Latin-hypercube parameter sets with v ∈ [0.01, 0.6], a ∈ [0.01, 0.6], k ∈ [0.1, 10], L ∈ [0.1, 10] (k, L log-sampled); NDT fixed at 0.3 s, s = 0.1; fixed-evidence data sets of 1,000 simulated trials; switching data sets with two trial types, T1 constant and T2 with "the stimulus changes at 0.2s after initiation (e.g. E0 = v for t ∈ [0, 0.2] and E0 = −v for t ∈ [0.2, end])"; censoring to "typical" RT distributions left 926 (constant) and 1,058 (changing) sets; fits by DE-MCMC (3N chains, 1,000 burn-in, 2,000 samples) with PDA likelihoods; "the switch times were assumed to be known inputs to the model" (Parameter recovery section). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)
- Recovery result (Figs. 3–4): "When evidence is fixed, parameter recovery is not possible on the basis of choice and RT data alone, and it appears to be essentially impossible to distinguish between integration versus urgency dominated strategies. When evidence changes over time, however, estimates are much more precise, and although appreciable uncertainty remains, high, medium, and low urgency or leakage can be distinguished. There is one caveat to this. Leakage is only identifiable when the leakage rate is above L = 1 ... If the time constant associated with leakage is longer than the typical length of a trial, for all intents and purposes leakage would be approximately absent and therefore not identifiable." — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)
- Design rationale: "it is crucially important when investigating the role of urgency or evidence leakage to consider experimental paradigms where information changes over time. In the absence of such changes, it is difficult to constrain these cognitive parameters with choice and RT data alone" (Parameter recovery section); "Time varying urgency and leakage are dynamic properties of the decision process ... dynamically changing stimuli are more effective at probing the dynamic properties of decisions than static stimuli" (General Discussion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)
- Experiment 1 design (Method): flashing grid of 400 orange/blue elements re-randomized every 3 frames at 60 Hz; up to 2 s to respond; 34 participants; blocks 5–19 of 72 trials each (15 × 72 = 1,080 main trials), "half were non-switch trials and half were switch trials"; evidence strengths 57% ("strong") and 53% ("weak"); on switch trials "the largest proportion color at the start of the trial became the smallest proportion color after the switch"; participants "were not told that the proportion of colored elements could possibly change". Experiments 2–3 varied initial-stimulus duration (including 150 ms) and strength; Experiment 4 used value-based gambles (27 participants analyzed). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)
- Fitting: hierarchical Bayesian DE-MCMC with PDA (Appendix A); priors log(k), log(L) ~ truncated normal on [−1, 1] in log10 units (i.e., 0.1–10), "log normal priors were used for leakage and urgency ratios since ... values of the urgency ratio of 0.1 and 10 represent symmetric extremes" (Appendix A, Eqs. 9–18). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)
- Findings: Exp. 1 "the urgency ratio is at moderate levels (k ∼ 1) while the leakage rate is relatively quick (the leakage timescale is faster than 250ms)" (Fig. 7c); Exp. 2 "urgency ratio takes on a mean value of ∼ 1.6 while the leakage timescale is again ∼ 250ms" and "even a quick 150ms initial stimulus duration affects the post switch drifts" (Fig. 9); Exp. 3 "k = 0.95 along with a leakage timescale of ∼ 250ms" (Fig. 10); the standard UGM (k = ∞) "provides a significantly worse accounting" (Table 2). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)
- Interpretation: "k ∼ 1 is right at the dividing line of urgency and accumulation dominated scenarios, which may be one reason that prior studies focusing only on the two extreme ends of this spectrum have led to ambiguous and contradictory results"; leakage "time constant of 200 − 250ms, which is significant, but on the slower (and hence weaker) end of the range that has typically been assumed" (General Discussion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)
- Relative processing confound: "when information changes over time, early evidence alters how subsequent evidence is processed" (citing Holmes et al. 2016); they fit separate pre- and post-switch drift rates, and "longer duration and stronger early information has a larger influence on subsequent drift rates" (General Discussion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)
- Why parameter inference rather than model comparison: model comparison "forces a researcher to explicitly define the models to be compared ... Taking a parameter inference based approach ... allows us to determine which portion of that model space best accounts for data, rather than focusing only on the extremes" (General Discussion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)
- Qualitative-signature pitfalls: Appendix B shows the Cisek et al. (2009) data "do not distinguish between integration and urgency hypotheses when evidence is defined in a conventional manner" (Fig. 12); Appendix C shows RT skew "is not always an effective differentiator of the two theories. In particular, while strong positive skew cannot be produced by pure urgency gating, weaker skew can be produced by both theories" (Fig. 13; 100,000 parameter sets). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)
- Holmes et al. (2016)'s rate delay "can mimic the effects of leakage" (General Discussion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)
- Contrasting reading by Smith & Ratcliff (2022): Trueblood et al.'s recovery simulations showed that "the recovered parameters were highly variable for both constant and changing-stimulus conditions" (Explicit Predictions section), and in their own fits of the Trueblood flashing-grid data "the variability in the estimates of urgency and decay and the correlations between them suggests that these parameters are not well identified in data" (γ–m correlations r = −.366, −.347, −.291; Fig. 8). Partial (PMC text to 200k chars). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000301)

### Inferences
- The identifiability result rests on a *sustained* reversal of evidence (from 0.2 s to response); whether a 50–67 ms pulse provides the same constraint is not established by this paper (see Gaps). The L > 1 s⁻¹ identifiability floor implies that in the mouse data (median RT 0.6–1.3 s) only leak time constants below roughly a second are in principle detectable from choice–RT data.
- Their estimate that urgency and integration are "of roughly equal importance" (k ≈ 1) in humans suggests that a pre-registered test of "leak vs urgency" should allow both to be present, rather than contrasting pure extremes.

### Gaps
- Number of simulated trials per trial type in the switching recovery (T1/T2) was not captured from the text read; the recovery figures' numerical summaries (e.g., correlation coefficients) are not given in prose.
- No pulse-timing manipulation (multiple onsets) in the recovery study; the switch time was known and fixed at 0.2 s.
- Whether recovery was validated with the switch unknown to the model, or with NDT free, is not reported in the portions read.

## Q3. Holmes, Trueblood & Heathcote (2016, piecewise LBA) and Holmes & Trueblood (2018, piecewise DDM): how do evidence-change designs identify leak/urgency, and what recovery was reported?

### Takeaway
Both papers model a single within-trial evidence change with a free "delay" between the change and its effect on accumulation; the delay (≈0.45 s in Holmes 2016) is the dominant, most uncertain parameter, trades off against post-change drift, and is acknowledged to mimic leakage. Formal recovery was reported only for the pDDM (1,000 + 1,000 trials; drifts, threshold, bias and delay recovered, variabilities not; ~98% of posterior variance along the delay axis).

### Cited Findings
- Holmes et al. (2016) design: RDM with one direction reversal per switch trial; per-participant coherence (10/15/20%); the switch time was individualized to "the median RT of earlier blocks minus 150 ms", i.e., "calibrated to take around half of the average accumulation period"; blocks 4–18 × 72 trials, half switch (≈1,080 main trials); 31 of 47 participants retained; participants were not told switches could occur (Secs. 1.3, 2.1–2.2). Partial (PMC text, both halves summarized). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2015.11.002)
- Behavioral signature: "the effect of the change on accuracy was substantially delayed"; after the switch, switch-trial accuracy rose only in the slowest RT quartile (q4 = 0.49 vs ≈0.20 in q1–q3) (Sec. 2.2, Fig. 2). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2015.11.002)
- Model-based: pLBA with a "rate delay" t_rate estimated at >0.4 s (≈0.45 s) was strongly supported over a threshold change ("a change of threshold does not provide a good accounting of the data", Sec. 3.7); NDT t_er ≈ 0.12 s; post-switch drift separation larger than pre-switch (Sec. 4). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2015.11.002)
- Trade-offs and sloppiness: participant-level parameters "strongly correlated", described as "sloppy models" (Sec. 3.8, Fig. 5); forcing the rate delay to zero compresses post-switch drifts, "an artefact of removing the rate delay" (Sec. 3.7); the full model "was quite unstable, indicating that it was over-parameterised for our data" (Footnote 3). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2015.11.002)
- Recovery: "we verified that it performs well on parameter recovery for synthetic data" (Appendix B.1.2); no trial counts or per-parameter numbers are given. Fitting: PDA with 10,000 simulated samples per block, 50 ms kernel bandwidth, DE-MCMC with 18 chains; DIC "is known to asymptotically prefer overly complex models" so the authors recommend converging evidence such as "plausibility and coherence of posterior parameter estimates" (Discussion; Appendix B). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2015.11.002)
- Holmes & Trueblood (2018) pDDM: parameters a, z, s_z, μ_before, μ_after, s_μ, t_ND and "a delay parameter t_delay ... to account for any lag between presentation and accumulation of new information" (Fig. 1 caption); the PDA "is effective at estimating the values of parameter a, z, t_delay, μ_before, μ_after, though it performs poorly at estimating the trial to trial variabilities" (Results, Fig. 4); recovery data set "1000 non-switch and 1000 switch trials" with the switch "coded to occur at Ts = 0.5 s" (and a 5,000 + 5,000 version, Fig. 4b). Full PDF, partially read. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-017-0901-y)
- Posterior geometry: PCA of the MCMC chains found "a single principle component is responsible for ∼ 98% of the chain variance" aligned with "the t_delay axis. Thus, a vast majority of the chain variability results from imprecision in the estimate of the delay parameter, and this is an artifact of the model more so than the method" (Results). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-017-0901-y)
- Sloppiness statement: "most models containing more than a few parameters exhibit 'sloppiness', which describes models where a wide range of parameter values can nearly equally account for observed data ... The issue of sloppiness is somewhat exacerbated when approximations to a model must be made (as is the case with pDDM)" (General discussion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-017-0901-y)
- Neither paper includes a leak or urgency parameter; Trueblood et al. (2021) state the 2016 model "did not allow for leakage, but it did allow for a delay in the onset of the effect of the change, which can mimic the effects of leakage" (General Discussion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)

### Inferences
- A free delay (or a sensory filter) is observationally close to leak for a single evidence change; any pulse-based test for leak must fix or independently constrain the sensory/encoding delay (motion-energy filter and NDT), otherwise the leak estimate will absorb it.
- Placing the change at about half the typical accumulation time (Holmes 2016) is a practical design heuristic consistent with the "change early relative to RT" recommendation of Murrow & Holmes (Q5).

### Gaps
- Holmes 2016 reports no quantitative recovery; Holmes & Trueblood 2018's recovery is a single parameter set (two sample sizes), not a parameter-space sweep.

## Q4. Voskuilen, Ratcliff & Smith (2016), Hawkins et al. (2015, J Neurosci), Palestro et al. (2018), Malhotra et al. (2017): which RT-data features identify collapsing bounds/urgency, and how do they interact with leak?

### Takeaway
In constant-evidence RT data the signatures of collapsing bounds/urgency are small and concentrated in the slow tail (reduced skew, truncated 0.9 quantiles, error-RT ordering), they are mimicked by across-trial variability, fast guesses or timed responding, and the collapse parameters trade off with bound, drift variability and NDT. Collapsing bounds are detected mainly when the task induces them (deadlines, mixed interrogation/free-response, mixed difficulty, extensive practice). Leak is rarely modelled in this literature; where it is (UGM filter), it is fixed or traded against urgency.

### Cited Findings
- Voskuilen et al.: hyperbolic-ratio collapse (κ = collapse amount, t_0.5 = semi-saturation time) with across-trial variabilities; six experiments (four numerosity, N = 21/11/22/9; two dot-motion with six coherences and a speed/accuracy manipulation); no deadlines. BIC favored fixed bounds in all six experiments; parametric-bootstrap cross-fitting (40 simulated sets per experiment) favored fixed in Exps 1, 3, 5, 6 and collapsing in 2 and 4 (κ = 0.59 in Exp 2; κ ≈ 0.002 in Exp 4); "data from 5 of 6 experiments favored either fixed boundaries or boundaries with only negligible collapse" (Secs. 4–5, Table 4). Partial (PMC text, main sections + appendix). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmp.2016.04.008)
- Voskuilen identifiability: recovery on Exp 5 simulations shows κ overestimated and t_0.5 underestimated; fixing κ at 0.3 or 0.8 "gave nearly identical boundaries" (Figs. 9–10); "there is an identifiability issue"; κ correlated significantly with drift rate, boundary separation and NDT, and t_0.5 with drift and boundary; these correlations "undermine the usefulness of the model in the sense that parameter interpretation as a whole becomes less straightforward" (Appendix A, Fig. A.1). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmp.2016.04.008)
- Voskuilen data features: quantile-probability plots "approximately linear" for both models; differences are "the 0.9 quantiles (about 10–20 ms) and error RT slowing"; collapse without across-trial variability yields only a small error slowdown, whereas fixed bounds with drift variability fit slow errors better (Fig. 11); the fixed model fits most collapsing-generated data except the 0.9 quantile in the largest-collapse/no-variability case (Fig. 12); "substantial mimicry between the two models" (Secs. 5–7). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmp.2016.04.008)
- Hawkins et al. (2015): fixed-bound DDM (with η, s_z, s_t), Weibull collapsing bound (shape, scale, asymptote; "the Weibull can closely mimic various forms of time variant boundaries", Fig. 3) and a logistic multiplicative urgency signal after Ditterich (2006a) (Eq. 2); 2% uniform contaminant mixture; QMPE with 10,000 simulated replicates per condition during estimation and 50,000 for final evaluation; differential evolution (120 particles × 500 iterations), each fit run three times (Materials and Methods). Full text (UvA repository PDF). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2410-14.2015)
- Hawkins data sizes: humans ≈483–960 trials per participant (e.g., ~560 for the Palmer et al. replication, ~960 for Ratcliff & McKoon 2008); monkeys 2,614–10,762 trials (e.g., 10,212 and 10,762 in the Middlebrooks & Schall data) (Materials and Methods). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2410-14.2015)
- Hawkins results: across the nine primary data sets "data from nine experiments provided most support for the conventional, fixed bound model"; the urgency model was preferred for "3 of 93 subjects"; in the 91 additional replication participants (74 with delayed feedback, 17 from Ratcliff et al. 2001 with speed instructions), 64 showed very strong evidence for fixed bounds (P_BIC ≥ .99) and 12 strong (.95–.99), and of the remaining 15 one favored collapsing bounds and 14 showed no strong preference; "Of the eight macaques that received very extensive practice, six exhibited strong support for dynamic bounds"; datasets supporting dynamic models "had response time distributions that were less skewed" because "the collapsing bounds partially truncate the slow right tails" (Results, Fig. 5); "Collapsing boundaries and urgency signals do not replace between-trial variability in parameters" (Results heading). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2410-14.2015)
- Hawkins model recovery: "reliably discriminating between synthetic data generated by fixed bound versus collapsing bound models was not always easy"; procedure after Navarro et al. (2004) and Wagenmakers et al. (2004): 100 synthetic data sets per model per experiment from subject-averaged best-fit parameters "with sample size equal to the sample size in the real datasets (with very large simulated sample sizes, model recovery was close to perfect)", ~10,000 estimation exercises; "for many parameter settings, model recovery was excellent even for the sample sizes matching the real data"; "Model recovery was perfect when the data-generating parameters—taken from the fits to data—differed substantially between the fixed and collapsing models"; for data sets modelled after the experiments that supported collapsing bounds "the data-generating model was correctly identified 100% of the time"; recovery was not run for the urgency model (Materials and Methods; Results, Fig. 5 bottom row). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2410-14.2015)
- Hawkins conclusion: "evidence for static or dynamic response boundaries may depend on specific paradigms or procedures, such as the extent of task practice"; "the difficulty of selecting between collapsing and fixed bounds models has received insufficient attention in previous research" (Abstract). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2410-14.2015)
- Palestro et al. (2018): RDM with a within-subject mixture of interrogation (response-signal) and free response, with an "opt-out" so that endogenous and exogenous terminations are separable ("perhaps the strongest constraint was the addition of an 'opt-out' policy embedded within the interrogation paradigm", General discussion); viewing durations up to 900 ms; 14 subjects; 16 models (fixed vs collapsing variants) compared by BIC, approximate posterior model probabilities and BIC-approximated Bayes factors; "a variant with collapsing decision boundaries was preferred across all 14 subjects"; BIC-approximated Bayes factors for the best collapsing-bound model (Model 11) over the best fixed-bound model (Model 4) exceeded 100:1 for many subjects (Fig. 8; exact count not captured); results held when data were split into halves to check practice; conclusion: "subjects may adopt a dynamic decision policy due to task characteristics, specifically to account for the mixture of response time paradigms and motion strengths across trials" (Abstract; Results). Full PDF, partially read. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-018-1479-9)
- Palestro on prior evidence: in Hawkins et al. (2015) "speeded judgments are better captured by time-invariant decision policies, whereas expanded judgments are better captured by time-variant decision policies"; expanded-judgment stimuli may "induce a bias toward a time-variant policy, severely limiting this mechanism's generalizability" (Introduction; General discussion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-018-1479-9)
- Malhotra et al. (2017): expanded-judgment task with discrete cues every 200 ms (Exp 1) or 50 ms (Exp 2b), so "the experimenter can observe the exact sequence of samples used by the participant"; the boundary slope was inferred model-free from a logistic regression of wait/go decisions on accumulated evidence X and time T (line of indifference, Eqs. 2–3); mixed-difficulty games produced more negative slopes than easy games (Exp 1: t(21) = 5.24, p < .001; Exp 2a: t(21) = 3.92; Exp 2b: t(23) = 2.12, p = .044) (Exp 1 Method/Results). Full XML. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000286)
- Malhotra validation and leak: Appendix B simulations found "a systematic relationship between this inferred slope and the true slope"; Appendix C: "the estimated difference in slopes between single- and mixed-difficulty conditions reflected the true difference, irrespective of the noise in drift rate or loss in integration of evidence" (Discussion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000286)
- Malhotra on alternative mechanisms: the pattern "is compatible with a number of different mechanistic accounts", including urgency gating (later cues weighted more) and "a constant threshold but also ... a stochastic deadline"; "Both these mechanisms will lead to decision boundaries that appear to decrease with time"; participants who did not decrease boundaries in Exp 3 "provide a good test for teasing apart these models"; in signal-detection-style paradigms "one can distinguish between constant or variable decision boundaries only indirectly, by fitting the two models to the data and comparing them" (Discussion; Introduction). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000286)
- Formal equivalence (Smith & Ratcliff 2022): a collapsing-bound model with a_i(t) = a_i/U(t) "will therefore be equivalent to an urgency-gating model with fixed boundaries", for "any boundary/urgency function" (Equivalence section, after Eqs. 19–20); "the number of identifiable parameters in the model is one fewer than the number of parameters in the function U(t)"; decay γ and urgency m "allow these two processes to be traded off against one another" (Explicit Predictions); diagnostic features: "a quantile probability function that is canted upwards towards the left indicates a slow-error pattern", "deadlines result in more symmetrical RT distributions", drift variability produces "an ordering of correct and error RTs that is like the one predicted by models with urgency", and urgency gating "lacks a mechanism to predict fast errors" (Results; Discussion). Partial. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000301)
- Smith & Ratcliff empirical: diffusion best for constant-information tasks; for the changing-information flashing-grid data "Using the BIC, 24 of 44 participants favored urgency gating. Using the parametric bootstrap, 26 of 44 favored the diffusion model"; urgency gating without decay was preferred to with decay (8/12, 14/16, 13/16 by BIC); hierarchical models "cannot overcome ... the problem of underidentified parameters at the individual participant level"; "terminating trials at 2000 ms is a data-censoring process that can bias estimation" (Discussion; Method). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000301)
- Mimicry by additive urgency: "The predictions of the additive urgency model are very similar to those of the collapsing thresholds model" (Boehm et al. 2016, main text; PMC XML, grep-read). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0958-5)
- Mimicry by fast guesses: a fixed-bound diffusion mixed with fast guesses (proportion ≈0.75 at the highest speed stress; guess RT mean 262 ms) reproduces errors faster than corrects, U-shaped SD/mean and skewness across speed stress, with accuracy below 300 ms at chance (t(19) = 1.07, p = .30) (Ratcliff & Kang 2021, Results, Fig. 4, Table 1; full XML). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-94451-7)
- Mimicry by timed responding: in the Timed Racing Diffusion Model "as the timing rate increases the predicted RT distributions become increasingly Gaussian. This is a feature of the TRDM that allows it to explain Gaussian-like RT distributions that have, to date, been most effectively explained with collapsing threshold models" (Hawkins & Heathcote 2021, preprint text). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000259)
- Behavior alone cannot separate gain-urgency from collapsing bounds: the urgency DDM "is mathematically identical to a model in which the standard DDM is coupled with time-varying decision bounds"; the deadline-induced conditional accuracy slope (β = −2.03 vs −0.35 under free response) was explained by urgency, and the argument against bound adjustment rested on EEG μ-band invariance (Murphy et al. 2016, Results; partial XML). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms13526)
- Ditterich (2006b): "a relatively large class of models, both with and without temporal integration and both stationary and time-variant could account for the behavioral data" of Roitman & Shadlen (abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neunet.2006.05.042)

### Inferences
- For a control-trial-only test, the leak/urgency/bound trade-off is structural (Smith & Ratcliff's one-fewer-identifiable-parameter rule and Voskuilen's κ–a–NDT correlations), so the project's weak control-trial recovery is expected, not a fitting failure.
- Any claim of urgency from the mouse RT distributions should be checked against fast-guess and timed-response mimicry (Ratcliff & Kang; Hawkins & Heathcote) and against an explicit "late slow responses" censoring effect (Smith & Ratcliff), since the task has a 5 s response window and responses after ~1.3 s show no pulse effect.

### Gaps
- Voskuilen's per-model identification percentages were not in the portions read. Palestro's text as read contains no model-recovery simulation. None of these four papers manipulated within-trial evidence, so they do not speak to pulse designs directly.

## Q5. Lüken et al. (2025) and other recent (2018–2026) identifiability/recovery work relevant to leak/urgency

### Takeaway
Recent full-likelihood and amortized-inference studies converge: leak and urgency parameters are nearly unidentifiable from constant-evidence choice–RT data but become recoverable when evidence changes within the trial (best when the change is early relative to RT and across more than one condition); collapse parameters of Weibull-type bounds are unrecoverable at any N although the bound *shape* is; starting threshold, decay rate and NDT trade off and are rescued by an external NDT constraint; and low error rates (<5–15%) create drift–threshold trade-offs that even 1,200 trials do not remove.

### Cited Findings
- Lüken et al. (2025): DDM and LBA; Study 1 error rates 0, 1, 5, 15, 25, 35, 50% (plus 7.5, 10, 12.5%), 150 vs 1,200 trials, 100 data sets per cell; data-generating values drawn from truncated multivariate normals fit to 580 DDM and 765 LBA empirical parameter sets; Studies 2–3 added difficulty and speed/accuracy manipulations (Method). Full text. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-024-02621-1)
- Results: "increasing bias as error rates decreased for v and a in the DDM"; biases "stronger for small than large trial numbers (150 vs. 1200)"; "strong positive relationships between v and a ... at low error rates. These high correlations occur because, at low error rates, the models are almost exclusively constrained by RTs"; "strong negative dependencies between DDM a and t0 ... However, the dependencies did not compromise parameter recovery across error rates since t0 only affects the leading edge"; a speed/accuracy manipulation "reduced the bias at low error rates for the DDM but not for the LBA. The difficulty manipulation did not reduce the bias"; "the mean group difference in drift rates was largely overestimated at low error rates" (Discussion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-024-02621-1)
- Recommendation: "we recommend tasks with error rates between 15% and 35% for small, and between 5% and 35% for large trial numbers"; "it is likely that the absolute number of error trials is what matters most for parameter identifiability" (Abstract; Discussion). - Murrow & Holmes (2024, PsyArXiv; NSF PAR copy read): full-likelihood Bayesian recovery (PyBEAM) for sDDM, leaky integrator (v = μ(t) − Lx), Weibull/reduced-Weibull/linear/exponential collapsing thresholds, the UGM in Trueblood et al.'s nondimensional form (drift E(t)(1+kt) + [k/(1+kt) − L]x, diffusion σ(1+kt), Eqs. 11–12), and a conflict model; N = 100, 250, 500, 1,000, 10,000; 1,000 Latin-hypercube parameter sets per N; ranges in Table 1 (Leakage: L 1–10, switch time t0 0.2–2 s; UGM: L 0.1–10, k 0.1–10, t0 0.2–2 s). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/3ca6y)

- UGM: "as with the leakage model, recovery is poor when information is fixed. ### Inferences
- The project's "leak" (OU decay) and "urgency" (gain 1 + u·t) are exactly the L and k of the Trueblood/Murrow nondimensional UGM; Murrow & Holmes's result that both become "excellent[ly]" recoverable with an early evidence change across several conditions is the strongest methods-level support for a joint control + pulse fit, provided the change is a sustained reversal; whether 50–67 ms pulses carry enough information is untested (Gap).
- The Rasanan NDT trade-off suggests using the 100% coherence trials (and motion-energy latency) to pin NDT before fitting collapse/urgency.
- Lüken's error-rate recommendation implies that in the mouse task the 0–18% coherence conditions (which produce errors) carry most of the information about threshold vs drift; the 100% condition mainly constrains NDT.

### Gaps
- No 2018–2026 recovery study used brief (≤100 ms) pulses or multiple pulse onsets; all "changing information" designs use a single sustained switch at a known time.
- Murrow & Holmes (2024) is a preprint; its R² values for the leak CI condition were in figure labels not reliably extractable from the PDF text.

## Q6. Hawkins, Wagenmakers, Ratcliff & Brown (2015, J Neurophysiol): what simulation design showed accumulation vs urgency gating are distinguishable, and which observable did the work?

### Takeaway
With constant evidence, four difficulty levels, 200 trials per level (800 per synthetic participant) and 100 synthetic participants per model, QMPE model recovery between an evidence-accumulation model and a UGM (urgency βt with a 100 ms low-pass filter) was "accurate for every single synthetic data set"; the discriminating observable was RT distribution *shape* (positively skewed under accumulation vs near-Gaussian with slower errors under urgency gating), not mean RT or accuracy.

### Cited Findings
- Models: Stone (1960) accumulator (drifts 0.03–0.23, bounds 0–130, start aU/2, NDT 300 ms) vs UGM with μ(t) = βt (β = 1, zero intercept) and a 100 ms low-pass filter (Table 1); within-trial noise s = 3.16; trial-to-trial drift variability η = 0.1 (EAM) and 0.13 (UGM) (Methods). Full text (PMC). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00088.2015)
- Design: constant stimuli, four difficulty levels, "200 trials per condition (800 total)", 100 synthetic participants per model; targets mean RT 667 ms and accuracies 60/75/87/95%; real data: Ratcliff & McKoon (2008) 15 humans (~960 trials each) and Roitman & Shadlen (2002) two macaques (2,614 and 3,534 trials). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00088.2015)
- Observable: the EAM "predicts positively skewed response time distributions"; the UGM predicts near-normal RTs with slower errors; "Model recovery was accurate for every single synthetic data set", with goodness-of-fit differences "perfectly separated" (Fig. 2B), also with trial-to-trial drift variability (Fig. 2D). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00088.2015)
- With a free time constant and urgency scaling, the UGM "makes highly similar predictions to the EAM" for mean RT and accuracy, but "the models once again make different predictions for the shape of the response time distributions"; results "remain the same whether one assumes a shorter (100 ms) or slightly longer (200 ms) time constant" (footnote 3). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00088.2015)
- Real data: "the evidence accumulation model provided the best account for one data set [Ratcliff & McKoon 2008], and the urgency gating model for the other [Roitman & Shadlen 2002]". — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00088.2015)
- Caveat from later work: in a generalized UGM allowing intermediate urgency ratios and leak, "weaker skew can be produced by both theories", so skew "is not always an effective differentiator" (Trueblood et al. 2021, Appendix C, Fig. 13). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)
- Caveat from timed responding: Gaussian-like RT distributions are also produced by a race between evidence and a timing process (Hawkins & Heathcote 2021). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000259)

### Inferences
- The perfect separation holds for the *extreme* contrast (pure integrator vs pure UGM with fixed 100 ms filter and zero intercept). For a model family with graded urgency (k ≈ 1) and weak leak, as in the mouse fits, this shape-based discrimination is expected to be weak, consistent with the project's poor control-trial recovery.

### Gaps
- No pulses or changing evidence in this simulation; the authors only suggest "briefly presented stimuli and time-invariant and time-variant sensory information" as future directions.

## Q7. Stine, Zylberberg, Ditterich & Shadlen (2020) on pulse-based tests, and Okazawa et al. (2018) on RT-conditioned kernels

### Takeaway
Stine et al. show that choice accuracy, chronometric functions, RT distribution shapes and flat psychophysical kernels (including with random brief pulses) do not discriminate integration from non-integration unless non-decision time and SNR are constrained from independent tasks; leak time constants are identifiable only when shorter than the longest decision times and are biased by NDT misestimation. Okazawa et al. show that response-aligned kernels carry bound-termination and NDT artifacts, that urgency inflates kernels while NDT and leak depress them (so mixtures can be flat), and recommend fitting choice–RT first and then simulating kernels.

### Cited Findings
- Stine et al. models: perfect integration (κ, B, C0, tND), extrema detection (first sample beyond ±B), snapshot, leaky integration ("Perfect integration and extrema detection can be thought of as special cases of the leaky integration model"); with collapsing bounds used in the elaborated fits ("the decision-bounds can symmetrically collapse toward zero as a function of time", Results). Full HTML. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365)
- Non-discriminating analyses: "the models are indistinguishable on the basis of either the RT fits or the choice-accuracy predictions"; "integration and extrema detection are not distinguishable on the basis of the shapes of the RT distributions" (Results, Free response tasks); for fixed-duration kernels, the simulations "included a small, 100 ms stimulus pulse whose sign and timing were random on each trial" (±0.1 strength), yet non-integration kernels "misleadingly suggest that evidence presented throughout" the trial contributed; high accuracy and a flat kernel "are not, on their own, evidence for or against any particular decision-making strategy" (Results, Fixed stimulus duration tasks; Methods). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365)
- Role of NDT: "extrema detection mimics integration in a FR task in part because the non-decision time is unconstrained"; "the models can evade punishment for inaccurate estimates of the non-decision time"; "Importantly, the difference in non-decision time is robust and typically ranges from 50 to 150 ms. Therefore, empirical constraints on the non-decision time should, in theory, disentangle the models"; NDT was constrained with "a supplementary block of 300 to 400 FR trials at 100% coherence" (Results; Methods). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365)
- Cross-task constraint: "a model that accurately estimates SNR should be able to parsimoniously explain data from both trial-types"; "We demonstrate that the SNR can be adequately constrained if its estimate is derived from a separate task-design" (Discussion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365)
- Leak identifiability: time constants of 0.80 s (S4) and 0.84 s (S5) "substantially improved the quality of the model fit"; "the time-constant becomes less identifiable as its value approaches and exceeds the longest decision times"; "the time-constants are likely underestimated"; "an overestimate of the non-decision time would induce an underestimate of the integration time-constant"; "we suspect some other models that do not posit leaky integration could do so as well" (Results; Discussion; Methods). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365)
- Power: with 10 trials per stimulus strength "the large majority of ΔBICs did not offer strong support for either model"; with 1,000 per strength "all but one dataset ... yielded strong support for the data-generating model"; human data ~1,800 FR and ~3,000 VSD trials per subject, six subjects (Results, Fig. 5; Methods). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365)
- General rule: "a given behavioral observation should be adduced as conclusive evidence for an integration strategy only if it is not reproduced by alternative strategies"; "non-integration models mimicked integration for reasons that were often counterintuitive" (Discussion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365)
- Okazawa et al.: "the kernel is inversely proportional to bound height" (Par25); "Urgency is effectively a reduction of decision bound over time" and "leads to inflation of the psychophysical kernel" (Fig. 6a–c); "non-decision times pull down the psychophysical kernel" (Fig. 3j); response-aligned kernels "peak and then drop down to zero before the response ... because the non-decision time causes later fluctuations in the stimulus not to bear on the choice" (Fig. 3k); the response-aligned "kernel is guaranteed to show a steep rise close to the decision time" which "does not indicate an increase of sensory weights" (Par16); when leak dominates, "psychophysical kernels systematically underestimate the sensory weights" (Par31); NDT and urgency have opposing effects and their balance "can yield a flat kernel" (Fig. 7). Partial (first 100k chars of XML). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)
- Okazawa recommendations: "psychophysical kernels on their own are inadequate to determine the nuances of sensory and decision-making processes"; "One should always assess psychophysical kernels in conjunction with the choice and RT distributions"; "fit them to some aspect of behavior, and then generate predictions about another aspect of behavior"; a sensitivity change alters kernel magnitude in the opposite direction from a bound or urgency change (Par37–38). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)
- Bound-depletion basis: "monkeys ... commit to a choice when the accumulated evidence reaches a threshold level (or bound), sometimes long before the end of stimulus" (Kiani, Hanks & Shadlen 2008, abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.4761-07.2008)

### Inferences
- The project's observation that the pulse effect is absent for responses slower than ~1.3 s is the kind of RT-conditioned statistic that Okazawa/Stine warn is shaped by termination and NDT, so its interpretation must come from simulating the fitted model under the same selection (the project already does this for the primary model).
- Stine's "constrain NDT from 100% trials" is directly applicable: the mouse data set has a 100% coherence condition.

### Gaps
- Stine et al. do not analyze pulse effects on RT, pulse timing, or a pulse-gain statistic; Okazawa et al. do not discuss discrete pulses. Neither paper gives power estimates for pulse designs.

## Q8. Zhou, Wong-Lin & Holmes (2009): which perturbation statistics and conditions give separability (design points only)

### Takeaway
Single pulses delivered early (small T/τ0) and pulse–antipulse pairs separate stable-OU (leaky), unstable-OU, pure-drift and time-varying-drift integrators through the *onset-time dependence* of the change in mean and SD of decision time; the signatures vanish when pulses arrive after most trials have terminated, are masked at low SNR/high error rates and by a dead time before integration, and CD-vs-TD SD differences "may not be detectable experimentally".

### Cited Findings
- Models: constant-drift (CD), time-varying drift b(t) = b0·t (TD), stable and unstable Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (SOU k < 0, UOU k > 0), and a nonlinear pitchfork model (SPB) (Secs. 2.2–2.3, Table 1). Full text (PMC). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)
- Perturbations: a single step pulse of amplitude p and duration τ0/10 at onset T (Eq. 9), and a pulse–antipulse "zero-effect perturbation" with amplitude ratio λ (Eq. 10); statistics: normalized change in mean and SD of decision time, for correct and error trials separately (Secs. 2.4, 3.1–3.4, Figs. 2–8). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)
- Separability: "The protocols can distinguish stable from unstable Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes"; mean-DT changes are near-constant for early onsets under CD and TD, peak near T/τ0 ≈ 0.5 under SOU and decrease monotonically under UOU (Fig. 2); SD changes under TD have the opposite sign to CD (Fig. 3); ZEPs occur at λ* = 1 for CD and TD and at λ* = e^(−kΔT/2) for OU models (Eq. 15, Table 2). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)
- Conditions: results hold "provided that perturbations are delivered sufficiently early in the integration process"; effects fade as T approaches τ0 "because more trials have already crossed threshold"; low SNR and high error rates mask the SOU optimum and the CD/TD differences (Figs. 5–6); "if there is a 'dead time' between stimulus onset and the start of evidence integration, the CD and TD models can display similar signatures to the SOU model"; differences in SD between CD and TD "are small and may not be detectable experimentally"; the authors suggest to "prolong the response deadline" and analyze long-RT trials (Secs. 3–4). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)

### Inferences
- The project's three onsets (≈55, 237, 417 ms) relative to median RTs of 0.6–1.3 s span roughly T/τ0 ≈ 0.1–0.7 if τ0 is taken as the decision time, which is the regime Zhou et al. identify as informative for a stable-OU (leak) signature; the dead-time caveat maps onto the motion-energy latency and NDT in the mouse model.

### Gaps
- No prescription for the number of onsets, pulse amplitudes or trials; no treatment of collapsing bounds or multiplicative gain, and no choice-based statistics.

## Q9. Design classes in the methods/design literature: two-pulse, step/ramp, early-evidence, deadline and speed–accuracy manipulations

### Takeaway
Two-pulse-with-gap designs test leak through gap-invariance of accuracy (and are confounded by bounded early termination and order effects); single sustained reversals and ramps test accumulation vs urgency but are only diagnostic once the UGM's low-pass filter is modelled; deadlines (more than instructions, reward-rate goals or practice) are the manipulations most reliably shown to induce collapsing bounds/urgency, and deadline-induced symmetry of RT distributions is itself a confound for time-based responding.

### Cited Findings
- Two-pulse (humans): "performance was largely invariant to the interpulse intervals up to at least 1 s"; expected double-pulse accuracy under perfect integration was computed from single-pulse accuracy as the sign of the sum of two independent samples; weak–strong sequences were more accurate than strong–weak (Kiani, Churchland & Shadlen 2013, Results; PDF grep-read). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2094-13.2013)
- Two-pulse modelling: two-pulse accuracy "is superior to what a perfect accumulator can predict"; DDM variants in which "the accumulated information in the first pulse plays the role of a starting point" and the rate depends on pulse order fit best (Tohidi-Moghaddam et al. 2019, abstract and model section). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-019-01810-8)
- Two-pulse with EEG (2025): 200 ms pulses with gaps of 0–1.08 s, ~700–933 trials per participant; no gap main effect on accuracy (Exp 1 β = 0.05 ± 0.02, p = .22; Exp 2 β = −0.03 ± 0.05, p = .51), read as "an absence of leakage of information from the first pulse"; first-pulse weights exceeded second-pulse weights (0.27 vs 0.18; 0.30 vs 0.06), explained by "a bounded process where participants sometimes terminated evidence accumulation early"; the fitted bounded model assumed "no accumulation during the gap intervals, and ... no leak" and "has no way to produce systematic gap-dependent effects" (Parés-Pujolràs et al. 2025, eLife reviewed preprint, Secs. 2.1–2.2, Fig. 4; partial). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.107980.1)
- Long-timescale two-sample designs: integration at prolonged timescales with brief samples separated by gaps (Waskom & Kiani 2018, abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2018.10.021)
- Leak adapted to signal statistics: "performance can be improved by means of leaky (forgetful) integration, if the integration timescale is adapted toward the predominant signal duration", tested by varying signal duration/onset within noise streams (Ossmy et al. 2013, abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2013.04.039)
- Discrete-pulse volatility designs: "Using discrete evidence pulses, we exactly compute the optimal inference process ... by changing the volatility of the environment, we demonstrate experimental control over the rats' accumulation timescale" (Piet et al. 2018, abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-06561-y)
- Early-evidence step designs: Winkel et al. (2014) manipulated "the presence, duration, and valence of early evidence"; the DDM predicted later effects, the UGM none, and "The behavioral data showed clear effects" (abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0551-8) Carland et al. (2015) reply that with "a low-pass filter with a 250 ms time constant" the UGM "can successfully reproduce the results of the Winkel et al. experiment" (abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0851-2)
- Ramp design: early 15% coherence (7 frames) then ramps to 40% at four rates × congruent/incongruent, 65 participants, 7 analyzed blocks × 60 trials; no congruency × ramp-rate interaction for RT (BF 19:1 for the null) or accuracy (39:1); the DDM fit better than the UGM (UGM underestimated late-evidence median RT by 445 ms and accuracy by 16%); conditional accuracy functions "did not discriminate" because both models predict decreasing CAFs with between-trial variability; a free filter time constant made the UGM "more like the diffusion model"; "for short decisions, the UGM's rapid leak means early evidence has not fully dissipated, which makes the test inconclusive" (Evans et al. 2017, Results/Discussion; full XML). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-16694-7)
- Constant evidence is non-diagnostic: UGM and accumulation "are difficult to distinguish when, as in previous studies, subjects are presented with constant sensory evidence throughout each trial" (Cisek et al. 2009, abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1844-09.2009) The tokens/changing-coherence design "in which motion coherence changes during each trial" (Thura et al. 2012, abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.01071.2011)
- Deadlines vs instructions vs reward-rate goals: "An emphasis on decision speed encouraged the adoption of collapsing thresholds—most strongly through the use of response deadlines but also through instruction to a lesser extent—but setting an explicit goal of reward rate optimality through both instructions and task design did not" (Evans, Hawkins & Brown 2020, abstract; preprint text partially read). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0000725)
- Deadline manipulation and CAF: 1.4 s deadline with a miss penalty "ten times that for an incorrect decision" produced a conditional-accuracy slope of −2.03 vs −0.35 under free response and near-chance accuracy at the deadline (50.4%); an urgency DDM fit better than a fixed-bound DDM (P < 0.001) (Murphy et al. 2016, Results; partial). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms13526)
- Instruction vs deadline affect different components: "instructional cues affected the response threshold, and deadlines affected the rate of decr[ease]" (Katsimpokis, Hawkins & van Maanen 2020, abstract only; retrieved abstract truncated). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42113-020-00074-y)
- Deadline and timing ability: "although we find evidence that participants collapse decision bounds, we found no relation between" collapse speed and timing precision (Miletić & van Maanen 2019, abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2019.01.002)
- Practice and time-based penalties: humans with "extensive task practice, or time-based penalties" become efficient like monkeys (Evans & Hawkins 2019, abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2018.11.014)
- Mixed difficulty with observable samples: decreasing boundaries inferred model-free in mixed-difficulty games (Malhotra et al. 2017, see Q4). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000286)
- Deadlines as a confound for inference: "deadlines result in more symmetrical RT distributions" (Smith & Ratcliff 2022, Introduction); the TRDM explains deadline effects without collapsing thresholds or urgency (Hawkins & Heathcote 2021, abstract/preprint). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000301)

### Inferences
- A two-pulse gap manipulation is the cleanest *design* for leak (gap-invariance), but it is an interrogation-style design; in a reaction-time task with bounded termination (as in the mouse task) the first-pulse primacy artifact described by Parés-Pujolràs et al. would appear even without leak, so leak must be inferred by model fitting rather than by raw gap effects.
- Speed–accuracy policy or deadline manipulations change the bound/urgency regime rather than identifying it, and the author's rule of existing data only rules them out for this project.

### Gaps
- No methods paper was found that compares the identifiability afforded by brief pulses at multiple onsets against a single sustained switch.

## Q10. Good-practice references for simulation-based calibration, model recovery and pre-registered decision rules

### Takeaway
The practice literature converges on: simulate before fitting; check parameter recovery in the regime of the real data; report a confusion matrix and the inversion matrix (p(true model | selected model)); prefer qualitative, falsifiable predictions that only one model reproduces; treat relative model comparison as insufficient without ex-post simulation; use simulation-based calibration for Bayesian fits; and pre-register analysis plans because modeler's degrees of freedom demonstrably change inferences.

### Cited Findings
- Wilson & Collins (2019): "If there is only a weak correlation between the simulated and recovered parameters and/or a significant bias" there may be a bug or an underpowered design; correlated recovered parameters mean "the parameters in the model are trading off against one another", fixed by reparameterizing or redesigning; "it is not necessarily problematic if a model's parameters are not recoverable in a full parameter space, as long as they are recoverable in the range that matters for real data"; the confusion matrix is "the probability that data simulated by one model is best fit by another"; "Models that are identifiable in one parameter regime may be impossible to distinguish in another!"; the inversion matrix p(simulated | fit) "can be computed from the confusion matrix using Bayes rule" (Appendix 3) and in Box 5 a model recovered only 30% of the time still gave 97% confidence when selected; "distinguishing between models on the basis of qualitative patterns in the data is always preferable to quantitative model comparison"; "computational modeling is rarely informative when there is no evidence of an effect in model-independent analyses"; power can be estimated by simulating the proposed effect, recovering parameters and running the planned tests (Rules/Boxes 4–5; partial, first half). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.49547)
- Palminteri, Wyart & Koechlin (2017): "the importance of falsifying candidate models in light of the observed data has been largely underestimated"; guidelines: (i) design a task "built to reveal at least one behavioral effect of interest, allowing discrimination between the different hypothetical models"; (ii) "Simulate ex ante the two (or more) competing computational models"; ... (vi) "Simulate the models ex post with their best-fitting parameter values to verify that ... only the behavior of the best-fitting model, and ideally not the rival one(s), can reproduce the behavioral effect of interest"; the model space "must contain (i) at least one model that is able to, and (ii) one model that cannot produce the effect of interest"; "relative model comparison results alone should not be viewed as definitive evidence" (Proposed Guidelines; Concluding Remarks; full PDF). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2017.03.011)
- Heathcote, Brown & Wagenmakers (2015): guidelines on "parameter recovery, testing selective influence, quantifying parameter uncertainty, testing and displaying model fit, and selecting among model parameterizations and types" (chapter abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4939-2236-9_2)
- Model mimicry tools: "landscaping" (fit model A to data generated by model B and vice versa) to "evaluate the distinguishability of models" and "the informativeness of data in distinguishing between models" (Navarro, Pitt & Myung 2004, abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2003.11.001) The parametric bootstrap cross-fitting method (Wagenmakers et al. 2004, title only; used by Voskuilen 2016 and Smith & Ratcliff 2022). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmp.2003.11.004)
- Falsifiability of the DDM: "simulated patterns of plausible data are presented that the model cannot fit" (Ratcliff 2002, abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03196283) "When a good fit can be bad" (Pitt & Myung 2002, title only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(02)01964-2)
- Blinded inference: 17 teams analyzed 14 manipulated RDM data sets; best planned accuracy 84% (EZ2) with an average of 71%; "these 'modeler's degrees of freedom' did affect their inferences"; "without a preregistered analysis plan" choices form a "garden of forking paths" (Dutilh et al. 2019, Results/Discussion; partial). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-017-1417-2)
- Pre-registration in modeling: "we propose a number of techniques and practices aimed at making psychological modeling more transparent, trusted, and robust" (Lee et al. 2019, abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42113-019-00029-y)
- Simulation-based calibration: SBC "identifies inaccurate computation and inconsistencies in model implementations" for any algorithm producing posterior samples (Talts et al. 2018, arXiv abstract only). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06788) Principled Bayesian workflow for cognitive science: "prior predictive checks, computational faithfulness, model sensitivity, and posterior predictive checks" (Schad, Betancourt & Vasishth 2021, abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000275)
- Hierarchical estimation tooling and best practice (DMC): "the importance of parameter-recovery" studies emphasized (Heathcote et al. 2019, abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-018-1067-y)
- Simulation-based likelihoods: PDA guide (Holmes 2015, title only; used throughout Q1–Q3). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmp.2015.08.006) Parallel PDA (Lin, Heathcote & Holmes 2019, title only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-018-1153-1) Efficient simulation framework for complex EAMs including "time-varying rates of evidence accumulation or decision thresholds" (Evans 2019, abstract only). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01219-z)
- Likelihood-based fitting of time-varying models: PyDDM supports time- and position-dependent drift (pulsed/changing evidence; leaky/unstable integration), bounds that collapse "according to an arbitrary function", and "a parameterizable mixture model of any distribution and a fittable ratio"; it does not support across-trial drift variability; "trial-wise trajectory simulation is very slow and provides a coarse-grained representation of the model"; "Individual GDDMs must be tested for model and parameter recovery before claims can be made about model mechanisms" (Shinn, Lam & Murray 2020; partial). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.56938)
- Model recovery recommended for every proposed EAM; both AIC and BIC reported to identify "a set of likely models" rather than a single winner (ChaRTr, see Q5). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jneumeth.2019.108432)
- Evans, Trueblood & Holmes (2020): "proper parameter recovery assessments are performed before using the parameters of any time-varying model for inference" (Q1). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)
- Lee et al.'s point on single-number comparisons as cited by Trueblood et al.: information criteria "depending critically on the balance they strike between complexity penalties and quality of fit ... a single number summary fails to fully capture the full richness of the data" (Trueblood et al. 2021, Introduction). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)

### Inferences
- A pre-registered decision rule for "leak vs urgency" that follows these sources would (a) define in advance a qualitative pulse statistic that one model class reproduces and the other cannot (Palminteri's model-space criterion), (b) report the confusion and inversion matrices at the project's actual per-mouse trial counts and in the fitted parameter regime (Wilson & Collins), and (c) use held-out likelihood only as a secondary statistic.

### Gaps
- No published example of a pre-registered decision rule specifically for leak vs urgency in pulse data was found.

## Q11. Synthesis across sources for the four objectives (cross-references to Q1–Q10)

### Takeaway
(1) Trade-offs: initial bound ↔ collapse/urgency (Q1), leak ↔ urgency (Q4 Smith & Ratcliff; Q5 Rasanan), bound/collapse ↔ NDT (Q4 Voskuilen; Q5 Rasanan, Lüken), drift ↔ bound at low error rates (Q5 Lüken), delay/filter ↔ leak (Q3). (2) Power to separate leak from urgency comes from within-trial evidence change placed early relative to RT, across several conditions, with NDT independently constrained (Q2, Q5, Q7, Q8). (3) Practice: joint constant + changing fits with parameters shared across trial types, hierarchical where individual data are thin, recovery and confusion matrices in the real regime, falsification by ex-post simulation (Q1, Q2, Q10). (4) Pitfalls: RT-conditioned selection and bound termination, NDT masking early pulses, fast-guess/timed-response and across-trial-variability mimicry, deadline censoring, kernel artifacts (Q4, Q7).

### Cited Findings
- Trade-off list with sources: bound ↔ collapse (Evans 2020, Results trend ii) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0); leak γ ↔ urgency m (Smith & Ratcliff 2022, Explicit Predictions; Fig. 8 correlations −.29 to −.37) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000301); collapse κ ↔ drift, boundary, NDT (Voskuilen 2016, Appendix A) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmp.2016.04.008); NDT ↔ starting threshold ↔ decay (Rasanan 2026) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.109730.1); drift ↔ threshold at low error rates (Lüken 2025, Discussion) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-024-02621-1); delay ↔ post-change drift and delay mimicking leak (Holmes 2016 Sec. 3.7; Trueblood 2021 General Discussion) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2015.11.002); leak identifiable only if 1/L < decision time (Trueblood 2021; Evans 2020; Stine 2020) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255).
- Designs with demonstrated recovery power: single sustained reversal at 0.2–0.25 s with constant trials fitted jointly (Trueblood 2021 Figs. 3–4; Murrow & Holmes 2024 Fig. 3 and UGM results, "best when the time of evidence change is early relative to typical response times"; multiple drift conditions help) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/3ca6y); early pulses and pulse–antipulse pairs with mean and SD of decision time by onset (Zhou 2009) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817); NDT constrained from 100%-coherence trials (Stine 2020) or external measurements (Rasanan) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365).
- Statistics shown to lack power or to be confounded: RT skew/shape for graded urgency (Trueblood 2021 App. C) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255); conditional accuracy functions with across-trial variability (Evans 2017) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-16694-7); flat kernels and choice accuracy (Stine 2020) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365); response-aligned kernels near the response (Okazawa 2018) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y); 0.9-quantile and error-RT differences of 10–20 ms (Voskuilen 2016) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmp.2016.04.008).
- Mimicry sources to include as competitors: fast guesses (Ratcliff & Kang 2021) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-94451-7); timed responding (Hawkins & Heathcote 2021) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000259); additive urgency vs collapsing bounds (Boehm 2016; ChaRTr) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0958-5); fixed bounds with across-trial variability vs collapse (Voskuilen 2016; Hawkins 2015; Rasanan cross-fit) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2410-14.2015); stochastic deadline vs decreasing bound (Malhotra 2017) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000286); extrema detection/non-integration with free NDT (Stine 2020) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365).
- Fitting/comparison practice: parameter inference over a continuous (k, L) family rather than comparing only the extremes (Trueblood 2021) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255); hierarchical estimation does not fix individual-level under-identification (Smith & Ratcliff 2022) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000301); recovery assessed in the regime of real data and reported as confusion + inversion matrices (Wilson & Collins 2019) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.49547); ex-post simulation to show only one model reproduces the effect (Palminteri 2017) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2017.03.011); fit multiple threshold forms and check consistency; prefer linear collapse or reduced Weibull for recoverability (Murrow & Holmes 2024; Evans 2020) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/3ca6y); censoring at a response window biases estimation (Smith & Ratcliff 2022) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000301).

### Inferences (project-specific, not from sources)
- The mouse pulse design is closer to Zhou's brief-pulse protocol than to the sustained-switch designs in which leak/urgency recovery has been demonstrated; a feasibility illustration should therefore test whether 50–67 ms pulses at the three onsets reproduce the Murrow/Trueblood recovery gains, with the pulse gain free as the project already requires.
- Given the L > 1 s⁻¹ floor and the "early relative to RT" rule, the earliest onset (≈55 ms) is likely masked by motion-energy latency plus NDT (Zhou's dead time; Holmes's delay), the middle onset (≈237 ms) is the most informative, and the latest (≈417 ms) is affected by bound depletion for fast mice.
- A pre-specified statistic that the literature does not already show to be confounded is the onset-dependence of the pulse effect on mean/SD decision time and on choice within RT bins, generated by the fitted leak and urgency models with the pulse gain matched to the observed effect, compared by confusion/inversion matrices at the actual per-mouse trial counts.

### Gaps
- No source provides a power analysis or recovery study for brief pulses at multiple onsets in a reaction-time task; the closest results are the single-switch recoveries (Q1, Q2, Q5) and the analytical pulse protocols (Q8).
- No source provides a worked, pre-registered decision rule for leak vs urgency; the practice sources give only general criteria.
