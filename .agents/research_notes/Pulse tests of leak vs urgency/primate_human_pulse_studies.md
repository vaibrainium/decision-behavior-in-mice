# Primate and human pulse / changing-evidence tests of leak vs urgency vs perfect integration in RT decisions

Scope note: empirical monkey and human studies only. "Read" status is given per study. Unless marked "abstract only", the full text (PMC, publisher, eLife, Frontiers, Cisek-lab PDF, or Springer PDF) was read directly or extracted by a text-summarizer from the full text; numbers quoted from a summarizer are marked "(extracted)". Our design, for reference: 50-67 ms pulses of +/-100% coherence at ~50-60, ~233-241 or 417 ms after onset; RTs mostly 0.5-2 s; pulse shifts choice ~7.5 pp (~0.3 log-odds) with no detectable dependence on onset; effect present for RT < ~1.3 s and absent later; choices toward the pulse are faster.

## Huk & Shadlen 2005 (J Neurosci): how did pulse effects depend on onset time and on RT, and how was the decline interpreted?

### Takeaway
Behaviourally, 100-ms background-motion pulses shifted both choice and RT by the equivalent of +/-1.6% coherence (same magnitude for choice and RT) and affected choices made 300-900 ms after the pulse; the paper reports no behavioural breakdown by pulse onset time. In LIP the pulse effect lasted ~575 ms (225-800 ms after the pulse) but was weaker for later pulses (-0.6 sp/s per 100 ms of onset time); the authors attribute the temporal decline to the decision bound, say the data "do not furnish a measurement of the integration time constant", and argue that leak cannot explain the time-shift-invariance violation because leak "should not preferentially affect the strength of pulses that occur later"; a collapsing bound / urgency signal is offered as the most attractive explanation.

### Cited Findings
- Design: two rhesus monkeys, RT direction discrimination, coherences 0, +/-3.2, 6.4, 12.8, 25.6, 51.2%; on two-thirds of trials a 100-ms motion pulse was inserted into the dim random-pixel texture behind the dots (not into the dots), at one of five onset times; monkeys were rewarded on dot direction, so pulses were task-irrelevant (Results, "Motion pulses affect decisions and reaction times") — [Huk & Shadlen 2005, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/)
- Pulse onset times were 100, 150, 211, 287 and 392 ms after dot onset (extracted from Methods; also restated in Wong et al. 2007, Methods) — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/); [Wong et al. 2007](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007/full)
- Choice effect: "The motion pulses caused the psychometric function to shift along the abscissa by an amount equivalent to +/-1.6% coherent motion, as if the strength of the random dots were altered by this amount for the entire duration of the trial" (Results; Fig. 3A). Joint diffusion fit to choice and RT: pulse = +/-1.6% coherence (95% CI 1.4-1.7%); a separate RT-specific pulse term was not needed (delta = -0.05%, likelihood-ratio p = 0.70), i.e. the pulse shifted the chronometric function by the same 1.6% equivalent (Fig. 3B; Eqs. 6-7) — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/)
- Per-monkey effect (Table 2, extracted): gamma = 1.60% (SE 0.08) combined; monkey I 2.15% (0.10); monkey S 0.89% (0.12); other fit parameters k = 0.426, A = 22.811, nondecision time 417 ms — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/)
- RT-conditioned choice effect (Fig. 4): trials binned in 75-ms "relative-RT" bins (RT minus pulse onset); "pulses affected the choices that occurred ~300 ms after pulse onset; trials in which saccadic responses were initiated earlier relative to the pulse showed no significant effect of the pulse... Trials in which responses were initiated from 300 to 900 ms after pulse onset showed a clear effect of the pulse on choice behavior. Thus, responses made up to 900 ms after a pulse still reflected effects of the pulse direction" (Results; Fig. 4 legend: "Choices made up to 900 ms after a pulse were affected in a direction-selective manner") — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/)
- Pulses did not precipitate decisions at a particular time: "Positive and negative pulses simply scaled and shifted the RT distributions seen on no-pulse trials (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, p > 0.6 for all pairwise comparisons...)" (Results) — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/)
- No behavioural analysis of pulse effect as a function of pulse onset time (100-392 ms) is reported in the Results text that was read; the onset-time analysis is neural only (Fig. 10B) — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/)
- LIP: "The pulses first affected LIP activity ~225 ms after onset and caused detectable changes in firing rate for another ~575 ms (Fig. 6). The pulses affected choices over a similar range of times but lagged the LIP changes by ~75-100 ms (Fig. 4)" (Discussion). Population effect 225-800 ms after pulse: +1.00 +/- 0.21 sp/s (positive pulses), -0.89 +/- 0.22 sp/s (negative), ANOVA F(2,159) = 22.28, p < 1e-8; 18 of 54 neurons individually significant (Fig. 7; extracted) — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/)
- Additivity (Fig. 10A, 250-350 ms after the earliest, 100-ms pulse): pulse-induced firing-rate change fell with dot coherence, slope -2.5 sp/s per 100% coherence (95% CI -4.6 to -0.3, p < 0.05); the bounded-integrator model gave -1.5 (CI -3.7 to 0.8) (extracted) — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/)
- Time-shift invariance (Fig. 10B; effect averaged 250-350 ms after each pulse, 0 and 3.2% coherence only): "Pulses delivered later in the trial appear to influence the LIP response to a lesser degree than pulses delivered early in the trial... Pulse effects declined at a rate of -0.6 spikes/s per 100 ms of pulse onset time (95% CI, -0.9 to -0.3; p < 0.001, t test)." The simulated perfect bounded integrator also declined, but less: "-0.1 spikes/s per 100 ms of pulse onset time; 95% CI, -0.4 to 0.1; p = 0.29" (Results, "Additivity and time-shift invariance of integration") — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/)
- Interpretation of persistence: "our measurement of the temporal persistence of pulse effects does not furnish an estimate of the leak of the integration process. The presence of the decision bound explains the dramatic decline of pulse effects with time... the relatively small remaining difference between the pulse effects seen in the LIP data and in the simulated integrator does not necessarily implicate a leaky integrator" (Results) — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/)
- Interpretation of the onset-time decline: "It appears that bounded integration is not sufficient to explain the weak impact of later pulses on the LIP responses. An attractive possibility is that the decision bound is not stationary but decreases over time (Ditterich, 2006). Equivalently, an 'urgency' signal might be added to the accumulated evidence (Janssen and Shadlen, 2005). Time-varying effects of attention... or neuronal responsivity (adaptation) might also play a role. Alternatively... nonlinearities (e.g., attractor dynamics)." And: "The observed departure from time-shift invariance is less easily accounted for by leak of the integration process. Although leak can decrease the temporal persistence of pulse effects, it should not preferentially affect the strength of pulses that occur later in time" (Results) — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/)
- Discussion (extracted): "Computer simulation of LIP as a perfect, bounded integrator suggests that the temporal decline of pulse effects is not primarily caused by a short integration time constant (or leak)"; "Our experiments do not furnish a measurement of the integration time constant"; the persistence supports "neural integration on a timescale of at least ~0.5 s" — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/)
- Simulation model (Table 1, extracted): MT pools -> LIP perfect integrators with 125-ms latency and 100-ms smoothing -> bound 68 sp/s; the only pulse-related adjustment was to set the MT change "to match the observed effect on behavior (a horizontal shift of the curves equivalent to ~1.6% motion coherence)" (Results) — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/)

### Inferences
- The behavioural evidence for "persistence" is RT-conditioned (Fig. 4: effect present for responses 300-900 ms after the pulse), not onset-conditioned; the onset-time decline is a neural (LIP) result for pulses at 100-392 ms. Our observation of a flat onset dependence over 50-417 ms is therefore not in conflict with Huk & Shadlen's behaviour, and the LIP decline they report concerns the same onset range as ours.
- Their own stated logic cuts against leak as the explanation for a decline with onset time (leak shortens persistence after each pulse but does not single out later pulses) and favours bound/collapsing-bound explanations; by the same logic, a flat onset dependence (ours) is what a perfect integrator with a bound that is rarely reached before ~420 ms predicts.
- Their Fig. 4 analysis (pulse shift as a function of RT minus pulse onset, 75-ms bins, with no-pulse trials assigned virtual onsets) transfers directly to our data and is the closest published analogue to our "effect present for RT < 1.3 s" result; they found effects out to 900 ms after the pulse with no reversal, and RT distributions that were only shifted/scaled.
- Note the model-fitting approach: pulse gain was set by hand to match the behavioural 1.6% equivalent rather than derived from the pulse's physical strength, so the paper gives no precedent for predicting pulse magnitude from control-trial DDM parameters.

### Gaps
- Table 2's RT shift in ms is not reported (only the coherence-equivalent shift); no per-onset-time behavioural numbers exist in the paper.
- The supplementary/figure data for the magnitude of the choice shift in each relative-RT bin were not available in text form; Fig. 4 values would need to be read off the figure.

## Wong, Huk, Shadlen & Wang 2007 (Front Comput Neurosci): the "violation of time-shift invariance" and attractor vs leaky-integrator accounts

### Takeaway
Wong et al. reproduce the Huk & Shadlen pulse protocol in a biophysical attractor network and show that the observed weakening of later pulses (violation of time-shift invariance, TSI) arises from attractor dynamics: once the network state has moved away from the unstable fixed point, later pulses have less leverage. Their linear analysis states that a stable leaky integrator predicts the opposite (later pulses more effective), a perfect integrator predicts invariance, and only an unstable (reverberating) integrator or a bound/collapsing bound predicts a decline.

### Cited Findings
- Protocol modelled: "a 100 ms motion pulse was applied after a time delay from motion stimulus onset"; onsets 100, 150, 211, 287, 392 ms; model pulse strength p = 11% coherence (vs 10% in the Huk & Shadlen LIP model); sensory-to-LIP latency 225 ms (Methods; extracted) — [Wong et al. 2007, DOI 10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007/full)
- Averaged over the five onsets the model's psychometric shift is "~1.6%", matching Huk & Shadlen (Results; extracted) — [Wong et al. 2007](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007/full)
- Violation of TSI (Fig. 8): "the effect of the pulse is weaker when its onset time is later"; the neural measure is the firing-rate change 250-350 ms after pulse onset on 0% and 3.2% trials (Fig. 8A, reproducing Huk & Shadlen's analysis), and the psychometric shift decreases with onset time (Fig. 8B) (extracted; no numeric values given in the text) — [Wong et al. 2007](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007/full)
- Mechanism (Fig. 9, potential-well picture): the network state "has time to accelerate away from the unstable point", so later pulses have less leverage; the linear analysis states that for a stable leaky integrator the pulse effect is "a monotonically increasing function of the pulse onset time" (opposite to the data), for a perfect integrator it is onset-independent, and for an unstable integrator "the change in the activity decreases with pulse onset time" (extracted) — [Wong et al. 2007](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007/full)
- Effective time constants near the saddle point: tau_stable = 79 ms, tau_unstable = 175 ms at c' = 0%; 77 and 159 ms at c' = 12.8% (Fig. 3 captions); NMDA tau_S = 60 ms; bound 55 Hz (extracted) — [Wong et al. 2007](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007/full)
- Bound effect: pulse-induced differences "converge over time" because firing rates are "truncated at the same decision threshold"; the pulse effect "persists longer than the duration of the pulse itself" but no decay constant is reported (extracted) — [Wong et al. 2007](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007/full)
- Model RT predictions for a single pulse at c' = 12.8%: positive pulse changes accuracy/mean RT from 95.2% / 746 ms to 97.1% / 714 ms; negative pulse to 88.7% / 783 ms (extracted) — [Wong et al. 2007](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007/full)
- Double-pulse prediction (onsets 150 and 250 ms): "The second pulse in the paired-pulse protocol has a weaker effect than its standalone counterpart"; the authors say this non-additivity is "experimentally testable" and could distinguish the attractor model from a diffusion model with a collapsing bound or urgency signal (Abstract/Discussion; extracted) — [Wong et al. 2007](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007/full)

### Inferences
- Wong et al.'s linear analysis is the clearest published statement that an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck leak (stable integrator) predicts later pulses to be MORE effective (because less of their trace decays before the decision), whereas urgency/collapsing bound or attractor nonlinearity predicts later pulses to be LESS effective; a flat onset dependence (ours) sits between the two and is the perfect-integrator prediction.
- Whether Fig. 8B's declining behavioural shift is the monkeys' data or the model's output is not clear from the extracted text; treat the behavioural onset-time decline as a model prediction unless verified in the figure.

### Gaps
- Numerical values of the TSI curves (data and model) are not given in the text.

## Kiani, Hanks & Shadlen 2008 (J Neurosci): late-pulse inefficacy explained by bound crossing rather than leak

### Takeaway
In a fixed-duration task (80-1500 ms), 200-ms pulses of +/-3.2% coherence shifted choices by 1.3% coherence overall but, among trials > 700 ms, only pulses in the first two timing quartiles were effective (quartiles 3-4: 0.2 +/- 0.4% and -0.1 +/- 0.4%). The quantitative argument against leak came from accuracy-vs-duration fits (bounded accumulator beats leaky accumulator with tau = 298.5 ms by a Bayes factor of 8.35e12) and from motion-energy kernels showing early, not late, evidence drives choice; the pulse experiment confirmed that "the brain ignores the later information, presumably because it has already terminated the decision process", and the authors state they "did not appear to lose, or leak away, information acquired at the beginning of the trial".

### Cited Findings
- Design: two monkeys, fixed viewing durations drawn from a truncated exponential (80-1500 ms, mean 330 ms), coherences 0-51.2%, delay or no delay before Go; 51 LIP neurons; 42,647 recorded trials plus 60,921 psychophysics-only trials (Methods; extracted) — [Kiani et al. 2008, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4761-07.2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670720/)
- Pulse experiment (one monkey, 18,017 trials, extracted): "the motion coherence was increased or decreased by 3.2% for 200 ms in every trial... the minimum stimulus duration was increased to 280 ms (truncated exponential distribution; range, 280-1500 ms; mean, 650 ms). The pulse began at a random time, with uniform probability density, between the beginning of the stimulus and 200 ms before the end of the stimulus" (Methods) — [Kiani et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670720/)
- Results: "The motion pulses significantly biased the monkey's choice toward the pulse direction (Fig. 5A) (psychometric function shift across all trials, 1.3 +/- 0.1% coherence; p < 1e-8)... these pulses were more effective when they were presented earlier in the trial... we examined all the trials lasting 700 ms or longer... the early pulses caused significantly larger biases than the late ones (Fig. 5B) (p = 0.018) (Eq. 5). In fact, the bias caused by the late pulses was indistinguishable from 0 (third quartile: shift, 0.2 +/- 0.4%, p = 0.26; fourth quartile: shift, -0.1 +/- 0.4%, p = 0.63)... the brain ignores the later information, presumably because it has already terminated the decision process" (Results) — [Kiani et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670720/)
- Regression (Eq. 5): alpha2 = 1.51 +/- 0.52 (p = 0.002), alpha4 = -0.40 +/- 0.19 per quartile ("diminishing effect for later pulses"), alpha3 = -0.12 +/- 0.19 (p = 0.26, no pulse-time effect independent of direction) (Methods) — [Kiani et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670720/)
- Model comparison on accuracy vs duration (Methods "Motion energy analysis"; extracted): bounded accumulator k = 0.46, B = 19.9; leaky accumulator k = 0.45, tau = 298.5 ms; BIC difference 59.5, Bayes factor 8.35e12 favouring bounded accumulation; the motion-energy kernels (Fig. 4C-D) "clearly favor the termination hypothesis" (leaky accumulation predicts late evidence matters more; bounded predicts early matters more) — [Kiani et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670720/)
- Discrimination threshold stopped improving at a breakpoint of 403-443 ms (mean 420 ms) (Fig. 3B; extracted) — [Kiani et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670720/)
- Discussion: "They did not appear to lose, or leak away, information acquired at the beginning of the trial (Figs. 4, 5)... A leaky accumulation process, although theoretically capable of describing the observed curtailed improvement of accuracy... is not supported in this task." Also: "By ruling out the leaky accumulator, we do not disregard the presence of all types of leak in the brain." — [Kiani et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670720/)
- Prediction used against time-limited or time-weighted alternatives: "a pulse delivered at a particular time should affect the decision process identically for all motion strengths. In contrast, the bounded accumulation model predicts that the effectiveness of a pulse depends on both the time it is delivered and the strength of the motion that it perturbed... the pulse should remain effective at later times for weaker motion than for stronger motion. This is exactly what we observed (Fig. S2)" (Discussion; Fig. S2 not read) — [Kiani et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670720/)
- LIP bound-crossing evidence: sustained delay-period activity ~35 sp/s independent of duration and coherence (34.9 +/- 3.1 vs 35.3 +/- 3.2 sp/s); bound-crossing time correlated with RT for short no-delay trials (r = 0.16, p = 1.4e-4 at 0% coherence) (Results; extracted) — [Kiani et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670720/)

### Inferences
- Kiani et al. did not fit a leaky vs bounded model to the pulse data themselves; the pulse result is a qualitative confirmation (early-only efficacy) of the kernel/threshold analyses. The leak rejection rests on the accuracy-vs-duration fit (tau = 298.5 ms leaky model loses).
- The pulse-efficacy decline occurred for pulses in the later half of >700-ms trials, i.e. well after ~420 ms where thresholds stopped improving; our latest pulse (417 ms) is at or before that point, so a Kiani-type decline is not expected within our onset range.
- Their Fig. S2 logic gives a concrete test for our data: under bounded integration the pulse effect should fall off with onset earlier for stronger base coherences (bound reached sooner) than for 0-9% coherence; under leak the onset dependence should be the same for all coherences.

### Gaps
- Fig. S2 (pulse efficacy by time x coherence) values are in the supplement, which was not accessible.
- No RT-conditioned pulse analysis (the task was fixed-duration; RT from the Go cue was not analysed against pulse timing).

## Cisek-lab studies (Cisek, Puskas & El-Murr 2009; Thura et al. 2012; Thura & Cisek 2014; Carland, Thura & Cisek 2015; Carland, Marcos, Thura & Cisek 2016): manipulations, conclusions, fitted time constants

### Takeaway
Across tokens-task (humans 2009, 2012; monkeys 2014) and random-dot studies (humans 2016), the Cisek lab argues that evidence is low-pass filtered with a time constant of ~100-250 ms and multiplied by an urgency signal. The tokens-task argument is that "bias-for" and "bias-against" trials (identical evidence after the sixth token/step) produce identical decision times and success probabilities, even for decisions made within 400-450 ms of the sixth token. The random-dot pulse study (Carland et al. 2016: 100-ms pulses doubling 3% to 6% coherence at 100/200/400 ms) found that slowing subjects' decision policy made the 100-ms pulse lose its RT-speeding effect while the 400-ms pulse gained it; a UGM with tau = 167 ms reproduced this, a 250-ms tau "consistently predicts a significant effect of the 100 ms pulse even in the interleaved sessions", and no perfect-integrator parameterisation reproduced the loss of early-pulse efficacy. Note: Carland, Thura & Cisek 2015 is in Psychon Bull Rev (22:1830-1838), not J Neurophysiol.

### Cited Findings
- Cisek 2009, design: 22 humans; 15 tokens jump every 200 ms from centre to one of two targets; subjects decide when they like; trial types 50% random, 15% easy, 15% ambiguous, 10% bias-for (first 3 tokens to correct target, next 3 to the other), 10% bias-against (first six movements reversed); fast vs slow blocks (post-decision interval 20 vs 170 ms) (Methods; extracted) — [Cisek et al. 2009, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1844-09.2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6665752/)
- Cisek 2009, key statistic: decision times in bias-for vs bias-against did not differ (example subject 1885 +/- 145 vs 1957 +/- 191 ms, KS p > 0.05; not significant for 21 of 22 subjects); success probability at decision 0.86 +/- 0.06 vs 0.88 +/- 0.06 (Fig. 5; extracted). Fast decisions: "we looked at decision times from a subset of bias-for and bias-against trials in which a subject made the decision within 400 ms after the sixth token movement... we did not see any significant differences... in any of the 16 subjects who made enough of these fast choices... (mean difference, -9 ms; SD, 68 ms)"; within 200 ms (6 subjects) -3 ms (SD 23) (Results) — [Cisek et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6665752/)
- Cisek 2009, models: diffusion (model 1) predicted faster bias-for responses (KS p < 0.01), contradicted by data; leaky diffusion L = 0.0005 (model 2) and leaky novel-information integrator L = 0.0003 (model 4; larger leaks prevented threshold crossing) did not resolve it; urgency-gating with low-pass filter tau = 200 ms, gain 3, noise SD 0.7 (model 6) reproduced the results, and "the addition of a low-pass filter does not appreciably change the behavioral results" (Results, Fig. 7; extracted) — [Cisek et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6665752/)
- Cisek 2009, Discussion on the motion-pulse literature: "Kiani et al. (2008) presented brief motion pulses to monkeys performing the FD task, finding a short window for motion integration (~300-350 ms). However, Huk and Shadlen (2005) presented similar motion pulses during the RT task and obtained a much longer window. It remains unclear why such different results were obtained in these two studies." They also cite ~100-ms integration windows (Ludwig et al. 2005; Ghose 2006) — [Cisek et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6665752/)
- Thura 2012, design/results: modified random-dot task with coherence steps; bias-for/bias-against trials (first three steps toward correct, next three opposite, or reversed); example subject mean DT 1,715 vs 1,810 ms (p > 0.05); "no significant difference (ns) between DTs in bias-for and bias-against trials for 24 of 30" subjects (Fig. 7); success probabilities similar in 13 of 14 (time pressure) and 15 of 16 (no time pressure) subjects (Fig. 8C); for decisions "within 450 ms after the sixth coherence step" (11 + 1 subjects with >= 5 such trials) "there were no significant differences between DTs for bias-for versus bias-against trials (Fig. 8E)"; the two subjects with a difference were faster in bias-against; bias-for-ambiguous vs bias-against-ambiguous accuracy did not differ (p = 0.3, t test) — [Thura et al. 2012, DOI 10.1152/jn.01071.2011](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/ThuraCisek2012.pdf)
- Thura 2012, attention check: decisions before 1,125 ms were correct 84.3% (time pressure) / 96.7% (no time pressure) of the time in bias-for trials but only 9.7% / 1.9% in bias-against trials, "confirm[ing] that they attended to the motion signal during those first 1,125 ms" — [Thura et al. 2012](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/ThuraCisek2012.pdf)
- Thura 2012, time constant: model low-pass filter "tau is a time constant (100 ms)"; "In a constant evidence task, integration of novel information will indeed be brief (<200 ms)"; "our data suggests that the time constant of integration is very short (e.g., 100 ms), much shorter than what is normally assumed by bounded integration models"; equivalent to "what Ditterich (2006a, 2006b) described as a leaky integrator with time-varying gain" (Methods; Discussion) — [Thura et al. 2012](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/ThuraCisek2012.pdf)
- Thura 2012 on the pulse literature: "In a fixed-duration task, Kiani et al. (2008) found that brief motion pulses had an effect on monkeys' performance only if they occurred within the first 400 ms of motion viewing. In a RT version of the same task, motion pulses had a long-lasting effect on neural activity, but this effect was much weaker for pulses that occurred >200 ms after the motion onset (Huk and Shadlen 2005), as if the temporal integration used to quantify evidence was completed quickly" (Discussion) — [Thura et al. 2012](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/ThuraCisek2012.pdf)
- Thura & Cisek 2014 (monkeys, PMd/M1, tokens task): "we saw no effects of within-trial history differences (Figures 4 and 5), as if the sensory information was not integrated but instead simply low-pass filtered with a short time constant (less than 200 ms) (Figure 5B). Although a leaky integrator... could potentially explain this result, the leak would have to be so strong that the time constant of the system would be very short" (Discussion) — [Thura & Cisek 2014, DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2014.01.031](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/ThuraCisek2014.pdf)
- Carland 2015 (commentary on Winkel 2014): UGM re-implemented with a low-pass filter, leak L = 0.004 ("corresponding to a time constant of 250 ms"), gain g = 0.03, threshold T = 16300, inter-trial (urgency-slope) noise log-normal mu = 0.7, sigma = 0.2, signal noise sigma = 6 (SNR 1:6) at 60 Hz, non-decision delay 481 ms; it reproduced Winkel's nine conditions (Fig. 4a,b) with "sensitivity to early evidence... weaker in our model than in the behavioral data" (Results) — [Carland et al. 2015, DOI 10.3758/s13423-015-0851-2](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0851-2)
- Carland 2015, decay claim: "With a 250-ms time constant the filter equilibrates to within 15% of its asymptote in about 500 ms... Allowing for a ~450-ms non-decision delay, this would mean that all RTs longer than 950 ms (97% of the data of Winkel et al.) would be determined purely by random fluctuations" unless urgency drives threshold crossing; "individual response times are only affected by early evidence in a subset of trials in which the decision was made within a certain time window of the change in evidence... the mean RT for all trials will still undergo a significant shift" (sections "Interpreting model fits to data") — [Carland et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0851-2)
- Carland 2015 on time-constant value: "The specific setting of the time constant is difficult to estimate in behavioral experiments, although prior studies and our recent neural data suggest that in some conditions it can be as short as 100 ms (Ludwig et al., 2005; Ghose, 2006; Thura & Cisek, 2014). It could also be task dependent and adjustable (Ossmy et al., 2013)" — [Carland et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0851-2)
- Carland 2016, design: 44 humans, reaching to targets, 3-s limit; "blocked" sessions all 3% coherence ("CMD" trials), 40% no-pulse and 60% with "brief motion 'pulses' during which the coherence of the motion stimulus was doubled (to 6%) for 100 ms... These pulses could occur 100, 200, or 400 ms after stimulus onset" (20% each); pulses "not consciously detectable" (post-experiment interview); "interleaved" sessions mixed 20% identical CMD trials with variable-motion (VMD) trials (Methods) — [Carland et al. 2016, DOI 10.1152/jn.00264.2015](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/CarlandMarcosThuraCisek2016.pdf)
- Carland 2016, policy manipulation: no-pulse CMD RTs were slower in interleaved than blocked sessions for 42/44 subjects (pooled KS P = 3.3e-41); subject JM medians 727 vs 1,057 ms (Results "Effects of sessions") — [Carland et al. 2016](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/CarlandMarcosThuraCisek2016.pdf)
- Carland 2016, pulse x policy result ("fast" subgroup, Fig. 4A): blocked - 100-ms and 200-ms pulses sped RT (KS P = 6.7e-16 and 1.8e-9), 400-ms pulse not (P = 0.21); interleaved - "the 100 ms pulses lost their efficacy (P = 0.052, K-S test), while the 400 ms pulses became effective (P = 2.1e-5, K-S test). The 200 ms pulses remained effective (P = 0.002)". Subject JM: blocked 100/200-ms P = 6.98e-12 / 2.42e-6, 400 ms P = 0.21; interleaved 400 ms P = 0.03, 100/200 ms P = 0.75 / 0.98 (Fig. 5A). Of 9 subjects with > 10 sessions, 6 showed the shift of the most effective pulse to later timings (significant in 4/6), two slowest subjects showed no pulse effects, one fast subject showed similar effects in both contexts (Results) — [Carland et al. 2016](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/CarlandMarcosThuraCisek2016.pdf)
- Carland 2016, window: "there is a 'window' of RTs in which the pulses influence behavior, and that window shifts as subjects slow down their decision policy. The 100 ms pulse is most effective on RTs between 650 and 750 ms (blocked sessions), while the 400 ms pulse is most effective (in interleaved sessions) ~300 ms later" (Results, Fig. 6); JM's mean estimated non-decision time was 451 ms (Fig. 5 legend) — [Carland et al. 2016](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/CarlandMarcosThuraCisek2016.pdf)
- Carland 2016, accuracy: fast subgroup no-pulse accuracy 83.0% (blocked) vs 86.4% (interleaved); blocked 100-ms pulse 87.9% and 200-ms 86.2% (significant), 400-ms 84.7% (n.s.); interleaved pulses no significant accuracy effect; "These trends can be captured by both the DDM and UGM... and therefore do not help to distinguish between the models" (Results) — [Carland et al. 2016](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/CarlandMarcosThuraCisek2016.pdf)
- Carland 2016, model fits: UGM with filter tau = 167 ms and context-dependent urgency slopes (log-normal mu = -0.50, sigma = 0.65 blocked; mu = -1.65, sigma = 0.7 interleaved; fit to no-pulse RTs only) reproduced the pattern (blocked: 100/200-ms pulses effective, P = 7.2e-22 and 3.7e-4, 400 ms P = 0.31; interleaved: 200/400 ms effective, 100 ms P = 0.07; Fig. 9A). DDM (best T = 550, theta = 16 blocked; T = 1,550, theta = 35 interleaved) reproduced blocked results but "also predicted that the 100 ms pulses had a significant effect (P = 4.4e-11)" in interleaved sessions, "directly from the assumption of perfect integration"; a DDM with a 150 +/- 100 ms pre-integration delay removed the 100-ms effect but produced only 0.7% of decision times < 200 ms and 9.1% < 400 ms vs 11.6% and 27.6% observed (those early decisions were 78.9% and 78.2% correct) (Results "Modeling results", Fig. 10) — [Carland et al. 2016](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/CarlandMarcosThuraCisek2016.pdf)
- Carland 2016, time-constant bounds: "Our previous analyses... suggest that the time constant is between 100 and 250 ms"; "while results similar to Fig. 9 could be generated with a 200-ms time constant, a 250-ms time constant consistently predicts a significant effect of the 100 ms pulse even in the interleaved sessions, in contrast to our data... once the time constant is set below 250 ms small variations of it can be traded off against changes in intertrial variability parameters... allow us to eliminate models with long time constants (>250 ms), including perfect integrators such as the DDM" (Discussion "Narrowing down the value of the time constant") — [Carland et al. 2016](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/CarlandMarcosThuraCisek2016.pdf)
- Carland 2016 on Ossmy et al. 2013's LCA time constants (29-1,995 ms across subjects, means 78 and 493 ms in two conditions): "because the LCA assumes a fixed bound, it forces the use of a long time constant for explaining any late decisions" (Discussion) — [Carland et al. 2016](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/CarlandMarcosThuraCisek2016.pdf)
- Carland 2016 on Winkel 2014: "in their implementation of the UGM they did not include the low-pass filter. If a low-pass filter is added to the model, it can easily reproduce those data (Carland et al. 2015)... a low-pass filter with a short time constant is capable of simulating the effects of early motion signals, even on RT distributions whose means are ~1,000 ms" (Discussion "Noise") — [Carland et al. 2016](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/CarlandMarcosThuraCisek2016.pdf)
- Carland 2016 on attractor models: strong recurrent feedback "effectively implement[s] temporal integration with almost no leak", so such models "would also predict a persistent effect of the 100 ms pulses even in the interleaved condition, in contrast to our data" (Discussion) — [Carland et al. 2016](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/CarlandMarcosThuraCisek2016.pdf)

### Inferences
- The decisive Cisek-lab statistic is a within-subject "policy x pulse-timing" interaction: slowing the decision policy should (under leak) remove the effect of the earliest pulse and reveal effects of later pulses, whereas under bounded perfect integration early pulses must remain at least as effective as late ones. This transfers to our design only if we can manipulate the decision policy (e.g., by session context or by splitting on RT within subject); their RT-split version (Fig. 6: effect of each pulse vs the subject's median RT) is directly applicable to our 10 mice.
- Their window estimates imply very short persistence: the 100-ms pulse was most effective for RTs 650-750 ms with ~450-480 ms non-decision time, i.e. decisions made ~100-300 ms after the pulse. Our pulses affect choices for RTs up to ~1.3 s (~0.9-1.2 s after pulse onset), an order of magnitude longer than Carland et al.'s window and comparable to Huk & Shadlen's 900 ms, which under the UGM would require tau far above 250 ms (the pulse trace in a filter decays as exp(-t/tau) and urgency only rescales it).
- Carland et al. measured pulse effects on RT (speeding by a congruent pulse) rather than on choice; their accuracy effects were small and non-diagnostic. The Cisek-lab bias-for/bias-against tests require evidence that is identical after a given time, which our single-pulse design does not provide; the nearest analogue is comparing late-RT trials with vs without an early pulse (Carland 2015's argument predicts no difference for decisions made well after the pulse).
- Their estimate of tau is not a fit but an elimination (tau >= 250 ms rejected because the 100-ms pulse would stay effective in slow sessions); they also note that tau below 250 ms trades off against inter-trial variability parameters, so the value is weakly identified even in their own design.

### Gaps
- Carland 2016 reports significance tests, not effect sizes in ms for the pulse-induced RT speeding (medians are only in figure insets).
- The Thura 2012 pulse-related passage characterises Huk & Shadlen's result as "much weaker for pulses >200 ms after motion onset"; Huk & Shadlen's own number is -0.6 sp/s per 100 ms of onset time in LIP and no behavioural onset analysis, so the characterisation is an interpretation.

## Winkel et al. 2014 and Evans et al. 2017: how early evidence's lasting influence argued against urgency gating; what statistic was decisive

### Takeaway
Winkel et al. (19 humans) showed that 67-117 ms of early evidence (within the first 200 ms) changed mean correct RT and error rate ~1.1-1.3 s later in a graded way (RT linear contrast F(1,1) = 164.6, p < .001; error rate F = 50.6), which an unfiltered UGM cannot produce (DDM RMSE 15 ms vs UGM 23 ms) and which the authors took to make "a large leak... unlikely". Evans et al. (65 humans) found that a 467-ms early evidence ramp had a "lasting and relatively constant influence" on later decisions (congruency x late-evidence-rate interaction absent, BF 19:1 for the null on RT and 39:1 on accuracy), and that a leak-free diffusion model fit better than a UGM with tau = 100 ms (UGM underestimated median RT by 445 ms and accuracy by 16%), with the best free tau averaging 4,105 ms; they also showed that the declining conditional-accuracy function (congruency advantage gone by ~2 s) does NOT discriminate the models because drift variability produces it in both.

### Cited Findings
- Winkel design: 22 recruited, 19 analysed; coherence set to 80% accuracy; early evidence "lasted either 67 ms (short) or 117 ms (long), followed by a period with 0% coherence... such that the early evidence and the 0%-coherence sections always summed to 200 ms"; late evidence (200-2000 ms) absent, opposite or same direction; nine trial types; 1,000 trials; stimulus up to 2,000 ms or until response; RT feedback "too slow" > 1,750 ms (Methods, Table 1) — [Winkel et al. 2014, DOI 10.3758/s13423-013-0551-8](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0551-8)
- Winkel results (Table 1, correct RT (SD) / error rate): no late evidence - 0 ms early 1,283 (97) / .50; 67 ms 1,268 (88) / .45; 117 ms 1,257 (75) / .43; late opposite - 117 ms 1,231 / .19; 67 ms 1,228 / .17; late present only 1,220 / .16; late same - 67 ms 1,184 / .12; 117 ms 1,187 / .12; 200 ms 1,156 / .09 — [Winkel et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0551-8)
- Winkel statistics: with late evidence, RTs decreased with early evidence [F(1, 2.7) = 35.7, p < .001; linear contrast F(1, 1) = 164.6, p < .001] and error rates decreased [F(1, 3.4) = 20.0; linear F(1,1) = 50.6, p < .001]; without late evidence, RT not significant (p = .15; linear p = .07) but error rates significant (p = .027; linear p = .022) (Results "Behavioral data") — [Winkel et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0551-8)
- Winkel models: "pure DDM" vs UGM "without temporal filtering (Cisek et al., 2009; see Eq. 2)" fit to mean RTs per trial type: "The DDM provided a better fit to the data (RMSE = 15 ms) than the UGM did (RMSE = 23 ms)... the DDM predicted effects of the presence, duration, and direction of the early evidence that the UGM did not" (Results "Modeling"; Fig. 3) — [Winkel et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0551-8)
- Winkel on leak: "Our data do not allow us to rule out the presence of a small leak. However, a large leak seems unlikely, considering that the first 67 ms of evidence have pronounced effects 1,100 ms later" (Discussion) — [Winkel et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0551-8)
- Evans design: 70 participants, 65 analysed; free-response RDM; early evidence rose from 0 to 15% (6 dots) and back to 0 over 7 frames (467 ms), congruent or incongruent with late evidence; late evidence ramped linearly to 40% at one dot step per 66.7, 133, 200 or 267 ms, or absent ("none") (Methods; extracted) — [Evans et al. 2017, DOI 10.1038/s41598-017-16694-7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5703954/)
- Evans decisive statistics: Bayesian ANOVA on mean RT - ramp-rate main effect BF > 1e80; congruency x ramp-rate interaction BF = 19:1 in favour of the null; on accuracy the interaction BF = 39:1 for the null; "none" condition: responses consistent with early evidence more frequent and faster (BF > 1e8); interpreted as early information having "a lasting and relatively constant influence on the decision outcome" (Results; extracted) — [Evans et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5703954/)
- Evans CAF: the congruent-minus-incongruent accuracy difference declined with RT and "disappears at about 2 s" (500-ms bins), so "the two methods of qualitative analysis support opposite conclusions"; the authors then show both models predict a declining CAF through between-trial drift variability, so the CAF decline "does not discriminate them"; simulations matched to sample size show CAF predictions "too noisy to be decisive for the slowest bins" (Results/Discussion; extracted) — [Evans et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5703954/)
- Evans fits (Table 1; extracted): diffusion v = 0.03, eta = 0.01, a = 0.42, t0 = 0.28 s; UGM (tau fixed 100 ms, urgency gain 1/ms) v = 7.05, a = 53,499, t0 = 0.39; UGM free tau: tau = 4,105 ms (SD 2,625); fit quality in late-evidence conditions - diffusion underestimates median correct RT by 27 ms and accuracy by 0.4%, UGM by 445 ms and 16%; the free-tau UGM "was still worse than the diffusion model's fit"; a long tau makes the UGM "less leaky, and more like the diffusion model" — [Evans et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5703954/)
- Evans RT dependence: the UGM fit the fastest congruent quantiles (10th-20th percentile) better than the diffusion model; in the "none" condition the UGM better predicted the initial CAF decay but the 2-s bin is noisy (Results; extracted) — [Evans et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5703954/)
- Ferrucci et al. 2021 (human tokens task with memory) reinterpret both: "when such filter, with a short time constant (250 ms), was added to the model, the UGM could correctly fit the data" of Winkel; for Evans "a possible reason for the low performance of the UGM... might be the use of a non-optimal value of slope for the urgency signal, which was a fixed parameter during data fitting"; Carland 2016's policy effect "is... consistent with the lack of effect observed in Evans et al. for responses longer than 2 s" (Discussion) — [Ferrucci, Genovesio & Marcos 2021, DOI 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1009455](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8516247/)

### Inferences
- Winkel's decisive statistic was a graded effect of early-evidence duration/valence on mean correct RT and on error rate for trials with identical late evidence; Carland 2015 showed a filtered UGM (tau 250 ms, steep urgency) reproduces the mean shift because fast trials carry the effect. Winkel's data therefore do not by themselves bound the leak; the distribution-level analysis (does the early-evidence effect survive in slow trials?) is what would, and Evans's Bayesian interaction test is the cleanest published version of that: lasting effect = no interaction between early-evidence congruency and later-evidence rate (which varies RT).
- Evans's warning is the most relevant to our "effect absent for RT > 1.3 s" result: a declining pulse effect across RT bins is predicted by a leak-free DDM with drift-rate variability as well as by a leaky model, so the RT-binned decline alone cannot discriminate leak from perfect integration; a model-based comparison (as in Evans) or a manipulation that changes RT without changing evidence (Carland 2016) is needed.
- Evans's free-tau estimate (~4 s) is the only RT-task fit in this set that estimates leak freely from pulse-like data; it is consistent with "effectively no leak" over the 0.5-2 s decision times relevant to us, but comes from a model whose urgency slope was fixed.

### Gaps
- Winkel's supplementary material (model code, full distributions) was not read; no RT-distribution analysis of early-evidence effects is in the main text.
- Evans's per-condition CAF values and the formal model-comparison statistic for the fits are only in figures (not quoted numerically in the text that was extracted).

## Hawkins, Wagenmakers, Ratcliff & Brown 2015 (J Neurophysiol): which designs/datasets discriminated accumulation from urgency and which model won

### Takeaway
Hawkins et al. showed by simulation that constant-evidence RT distributions already discriminate the Stone-type evidence accumulation model (EAM) from the UGM (because the UGM's RT variance grows as t^3 vs t for the EAM, giving near-normal RT distributions and slow errors), with perfect model recovery in 100 synthetic subjects x 800 trials. On real data the EAM won for Ratcliff & McKoon's 15 humans, while the UGM fit Roitman & Shadlen's two monkeys "much better". The UGM's low-pass tau was fixed at 100 ms (200 ms gave the same result).

### Cited Findings
- Models: EAM (Stone 1960) vs UGM with urgency mu(t) = beta*t (beta = 1) and a low-pass filter; fitted by quantile maximum product estimation on 10th-90th RT percentiles for correct and error responses; no BIC/DIC; the comparison statistic is the maximised QMP, which "approximates log-likelihood" (Methods; extracted) — [Hawkins et al. 2015, DOI 10.1152/jn.00088.2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4495756/)
- Theory: Thura et al. 2012's equivalence proof is called "incorrect"; the discriminating terms are the integral of noise for the EAM vs t times that integral for the UGM, whose variances grow as t and t^3 respectively (Fig. 1); the UGM predicts slow errors, near-normal RT distributions and monotonic quantile-probability plots (Fig. 3) (extracted) — [Hawkins et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4495756/)
- Recovery: 100 synthetic participants per model, 200 trials x 4 difficulties, accuracy 60-95%, mean RT 667 ms; "Model recovery was accurate for every single synthetic data set", also with drift variability (Fig. 2B, 2D) (extracted) — [Hawkins et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4495756/)
- Real data: Ratcliff & McKoon 2008 (15 humans, ~960 trials each, coherences 5-50%) - EAM fit better; Roitman & Shadlen 2002 (2 macaques, 2,614 and 3,534 trials) - UGM fit "much better"; QMP values are given only in Fig. 5; the EAM over-predicted the humans' 90th percentile, the UGM under-estimated monkey accuracy and over-predicted the 10th percentile of monkey errors (extracted) — [Hawkins et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4495756/)
- Time constant: fixed at 100 ms; footnote 3: 200 ms gives the same results; a free-UGM simulation (beta in [0,5], tau in [0,2] s) still produced RT shapes differing from the EAM (extracted) — [Hawkins et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4495756/)
- Recommendation: data from briefly presented, masked and time-varying stimuli (Ratcliff & Rouder 2000) favoured a constant drift rate and "might therefore provide a critical test of the UGM", because low-pass filtering implies a gradual decline of information after stimulus offset (Discussion; extracted) — [Hawkins et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4495756/)
- Thura's reply ("How to discriminate conclusively among different models of decision making?", J Neurophysiol 2016, DOI 10.1152/jn.00911.2015) exists as a commentary on this paper (title and DOI verified; content not read) — [Thura 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4922450/)

### Inferences
- For our purposes the paper's relevance is methodological: it argues that fits to constant-evidence choice/RT distributions can distinguish UGM-style urgency from pure accumulation, and that the winner can differ by species/dataset (monkeys favoured UGM). It does not contain a pulse analysis.
- Because the UGM here has a fixed urgency gain and a fixed tau, its "win" on monkey data mostly reflects RT-distribution shape (slow errors, near-normal distributions), the same features that a collapsing-bound DDM produces; this is why Carland 2016 says "even sophisticated comparisons of model fits (Hawkins et al. 2015a) do not yield consistent conclusions across tasks, subjects, or modeling assumptions".

### Gaps
- Numeric QMP differences for the real datasets are only in figure panels.

## Stine, Trautmann, Jeurissen & Shadlen 2023 (Neuron) and Steinemann et al. 2024 (eLife): what weak random-time pulses showed about persistence and how pulse effects were conditioned on time

### Takeaway
Both studies (same two monkeys) used 100-ms pulses of weak motion (+/-3.2% for monkey J, +/-4.0% for monkey M) at a random time 0.1-0.8 s after motion onset on ~half of RT trials as a check on integration. Stine et al. report that "pulses shown early in the trial affected choices made nearly a second later" (Fig. S1) and that the pulse's effect on LIP firing persisted with a Gaussian-fit sigma of 0.11 s versus 0.03 s in the superior colliculus (p < .001); Steinemann et al. cite the pulse effects on choice and RT (Figure 1-figure supplement 1) as support for bounded integration. Neither paper reports pulse effects as a function of pulse onset time, nor numeric effect sizes in the main text.

### Cited Findings
- Stine 2023 pulse design: "On approximately half of the trials, a 100 ms pulse of motion was added to the RDM stimulus. The pulse took the form of an increment or decrement of motion coherence (similar to Kiani et al.)... if the motion stimulus had a signed coherence of +12.8%, a leftward motion pulse of strength 4.0% would change this coherence to +8.8% for 100 ms. The strength of the pulse was calibrated to induce a weak but reliable effect on choices and RTs. The pulse strength was 4.0% coherence for Monkey M and 3.2% for Monkey J. The sign of the pulse was chosen randomly" (STAR Methods) — [Stine et al. 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2023.05.028](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10565788/)
- Stine 2023 behaviour: "Figure S1 shows that the pulses affected choices and RTs. Moreover, pulses shown early in the trial affected choices made nearly a second later. This persistent influence suggests that the monkeys' decisions arise through the temporal integration of motion evidence and is inconsistent with non-integration strategies" (Results) — [Stine et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10565788/)
- Stine 2023 neural persistence: pulse effect estimated by time-resolved regression of firing rate on signed pulse direction and choice (Eq. 9), beta1(t) fit with a Gaussian whose SD defines persistence: "pulses affect LIP activity for several hundred milliseconds (sigma = 0.11 s, Gaussian fit). In contrast, the pulses affect SC activity more transiently (sigma = 0.03 s; p < .001, likelihood ratio test)" (Results, Fig. 2C-D; STAR Methods) — [Stine et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10565788/)
- Steinemann 2024 pulse design: "On approximately half of the trials, a 100 ms pulse of weak motion (+/-3.2% or 4.0% coherence for monkeys J and M, respectively) is added to the random dot motion stimulus at a random time (0.1-0.8 s, lambda = 0.4) relative to motion onset (similar to Kiani et al., 2008)"; monkey M 9,684 trials, monkey J 8,142 trials (Methods; Fig. 1 legend) — [Steinemann et al. 2024, DOI 10.7554/eLife.90859](https://elifesciences.org/articles/90859)
- Steinemann 2024 use of pulses: "The influence of these pulses on choice and RT further supports the assertion that the choices and RTs arose through a process of integration of noisy samples of evidence to a stopping bound (Figure 1-figure supplement 1; Stine et al., 2020; Stine et al., 2023; Hyafil et al., 2023)" (Results) — [Steinemann et al. 2024](https://elifesciences.org/articles/90859)
- Related (Stine et al. 2020, eLife): pulses were used only in simulations to compute psychophysical kernels ("a small, 100 ms stimulus pulse whose sign and timing were random on each trial"); the human experiments tested integration vs non-integration without pulse data (Results/Methods) — [Stine et al. 2020, DOI 10.7554/eLife.55365](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7217695/)

### Inferences
- The Shadlen-lab convention since 2008 is a weak (3-4%) 100-ms pulse at a random time with a flat hazard, and the diagnostic is persistence of the choice effect as a function of time from pulse to response (Huk 2005 Fig. 4 style), not the onset-time dependence. The reported persistence ("nearly a second") is consistent with our ~0.9-1.2 s window.
- The LIP persistence sigma of 0.11 s is a neural, not behavioural, time constant and is shaped by bound crossing, so it should not be read as a leak estimate (cf. Huk & Shadlen 2005).

### Gaps
- Stine 2023 Fig. S1 and Steinemann 2024 Figure 1-figure supplement 1 (the actual choice/RT pulse effects and their time dependence) were not accessible as text; effect sizes and the shape of the persistence curve are not reported in the main texts.

## Zylberberg et al. 2016, Kiani et al. 2013, Levi et al. 2018, Yates et al. 2017: what they say about leak time constants measured with time-varying evidence

### Takeaway
None of these four estimates a decision-stage leak from pulses in an RT task. Kiani 2013 (humans, two 120-ms pulses, delayed response) found accuracy invariant to gaps of 0-1,080 ms, i.e. no measurable decay over ~1 s and a larger weight on the second pulse. Zylberberg 2016 fit RT-task data (1 monkey, 3 humans) with a bounded accumulator whose bound collapsed logistically with time and no leak term; it manipulated evidence variance, not pulses. Levi 2018 and Yates 2017 used fixed-duration seven-pulse stimuli (humans and macaques): temporal weights shifted with the stimulus statistics (Levi) and MT responses to pulses decayed within the trial while LIP pulse kernels were sustained (Yates); neither reports a leak time constant.

### Cited Findings
- Kiani 2013 design: 5 humans; "one or two 120-ms-long motion pulses"; double-pulse coherences 3.2, 6.4, 12.8%; "The two pulses were separated by 0, 120, 360, or 1080 ms"; delayed response (400-1000 ms delay then Go, respond within 1 s); 2,400-3,300 trials per subject (Methods; extracted) — [Kiani, Churchland & Shadlen 2013, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2094-13.2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3797371/)
- Kiani 2013 results: accuracy "largely unaffected by interpulse interval" (Table 1 beta3 per subject: -0.52 +/- 0.40, -0.59 +/- 0.38, -0.75 +/- 0.38 (p = 0.05), 0.27 +/- 0.44, 0.39 +/- 0.41); the first pulse's evidence was "maintained without any significant decay or loss during the interpulse interval"; observed accuracy exceeded the perfect-integrator prediction (beta = 0.13 +/- 0.04, p = 3e-4 equal strength; 0.16 +/- 0.03, p = 2.4e-10 unequal); second pulse carried more weight (motion-energy beta3 = -12.9 +/- 3.6, p = 3.4e-4); "a leaky process... is incompatible with the invariance to interpulse intervals"; the mechanism must be "capable of maintaining the fidelity of accumulated evidence for at least 1 s" (Results/Discussion; extracted) — [Kiani et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3797371/)
- Zylberberg 2016: RT task with one monkey and three humans; the model is bounded accumulation with "the bound height... change[d] as a function of time... parameterized by a logistic function of time" (parameters B0, a, d, Table 1; e.g. monkey M1 B0 = 1.96, a = 0.64, d = -0.02; humans a = -2.17 to -2.97); only the momentary-evidence variance differed between volatility conditions; no leak parameter appears in the model (Methods "Bounded accumulation model", Table 1) — [Zylberberg, Fetsch & Shadlen 2016, DOI 10.7554/eLife.17688](https://elifesciences.org/articles/17688)
- Zylberberg 2016: in the 0.2-s human confidence task "the presence of bounds did not improve the quality of the fits. This implies that subjects used all the stimulus information" (Methods) — [Zylberberg et al. 2016](https://elifesciences.org/articles/17688)
- Levi 2018 design: "seven consecutive motion pulses lasting 150 ms each" (100 ms for two humans); choice after fixation-point offset ("go"), i.e. fixed duration; > 145,000 trials across humans and macaques; temporal weights estimated by logistic regression on the seven pulses (Methods/Results) — [Levi, Yates, Huk & Katz 2018, DOI 10.1523/ENEURO.0169-18.2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6220584/)
- Levi 2018 results: weights shifted to the pulses with highest expected motion strength in early- and late-stimulus sessions; in flat sessions macaques showed early weighting while humans were flatter; the authors frame weighting as adaptable "regardless of downstream impacts, such as a bound or a leak" and acknowledge their "conclusions are primarily relevant to fixed duration stimuli" (Results/Discussion; reviewer response) — [Levi et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6220584/)
- Yates 2017: seven pulses of 150 or 166 ms; "both MT and LIP, as well as the behavioral decisions, depended most strongly on motion early in the trial"; a stimulus-to-MT GLM captured 78% +/- 1.56% of PSTH variance and "produced time-varying pulse responses that matched the decay in the MT data"; LIP pulse kernels were sustained but "a small motion integration term rides on top of a large and conventional premotor buildup" (Results/Discussion) — [Yates et al. 2017, DOI 10.1038/nn.4611](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5673485/)

### Inferences
- Kiani 2013 provides the strongest primate/human bound on decay of a pulse's influence: no detectable loss over 1.08 s in a delayed-response design. Under an OU leak with rate lambda, a 1-s gap would attenuate the first pulse by exp(-lambda); the null result implies lambda << 1/s unless leak is specific to continuous evidence.
- Yates 2017's MT decay is a sensory-adaptation effect that would make later pulses weaker at the input even with perfect downstream integration; this is one non-leak, non-urgency reason to expect onset-dependent pulse effects, and our flat onset dependence argues against strong input adaptation within 50-420 ms for our 100% pulses.

### Gaps
- No RT-task leak estimate exists in these four papers.

## 2016-2026 human and monkey studies with pulses or evidence steps in RT tasks (Murphy 2016; Steinemann 2018; Devine 2019; Keung 2019/2020; Bronfman 2016; Pares-Pujolras 2025/26; Kumano & Uka 2024; plus Fetsch 2018, Okazawa 2018, Holmes 2016, Trueblood 2021, Ferrucci 2021, Shinn 2022, Tohidi-Moghaddam 2019)

### Takeaway
Of the suggested studies only Pares-Pujolras et al. (delayed response), Kumano & Uka (monkeys; abstract only) and Bronfman (fixed duration) manipulate evidence within trials; Murphy 2016, Steinemann 2018 and Devine 2019 are constant-evidence RT studies that infer urgency from RT distributions, EEG and pupil. Pares-Pujolras found no leak over gaps up to 1.08 s but a primacy effect explained by bounded accumulation (early termination); Kumano & Uka report larger effects of early than late relevant pulses and a rise-then-fall of irrelevant-pulse effects that they attribute to leaky integration. Additional RT-task studies found: a within-trial decay of MT optogenetic suppression effects beyond ~350 ms of stimulus duration (Fetsch 2018); human RT-task kernels fully explained by a bounded DDM with non-decision time (Okazawa 2018); a > 0.4 s delay before a mid-trial motion reversal changes the accumulation rate (Holmes 2016); and a leak time scale of ~200-250 ms with urgency and accumulation "of roughly equal strength" from a changing-information RDM paradigm (Trueblood 2021; abstract only).

### Cited Findings
- Murphy 2016: 21 humans, RDM under free response vs 1.4-s deadline; an urgency DDM (logistic time-dependent urgency added to anti-correlated accumulators) fit better than the standard DDM (Wilcoxon on BIC differences, P < 0.001 for all pairwise comparisons); mu-band motor preparation showed static and time-dependent urgency; when the LCA network's "effective time constant of accumulation (tau)" was constrained "to be particularly short... the model still provided a reasonable account of behaviour and accumulation dynamics... Thus, evidence accumulation with a long time constant does not appear to be a necessary prerequisite" (Results) — [Murphy, Boonstra & Nieuwenhuis 2016, DOI 10.1038/ncomms13526](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5123079/)
- Steinemann 2018: contrast-comparison RT task under Speed/Accuracy cues (deadline 2.4 s even under Accuracy); pre-response CPP amplitude declined with RT ("The collapsing CPP-amplitude effect observed here clearly points to the presence of" a collapsing bound equivalent to evidence-independent urgency), while urgency at the motor level was mainly a static starting-level shift with "no strong signs of any change to the rate of growth of dynamic urgency"; no within-trial evidence change (Results/Discussion) — [Steinemann, O'Connell & Kelly 2018, DOI 10.1038/s41467-018-06117-0](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6128824/)
- Devine 2019: contrast-change detection/discrimination with uncertain change onset; subjects "commenced decision formation before the target contrast" change on long foreperiods ("premature evidence accumulation"); urgency is discussed only via the literature; no pulses (Results/Discussion) — [Devine et al. 2019, DOI 10.7554/eLife.48526](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6904213/)
- Keung 2019 (abstract only): auditory click accumulation in humans; suboptimalities include an uneven "integration kernel"; pupil-linked arousal relates to kernel and noise — [Keung, Hagen & Wilson 2019, DOI 10.1038/s41562-019-0551-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-019-0551-4). - Bronfman 2016: brightness-fluctuating disks for 1, 2 or 3 s (and 5 s), fixed duration; primacy at 1-2 s (first vs second window t(12) = 3.63, p = 0.003), non-monotonic (U-shaped) at 3 s (ANOVA F(4,48) = 2.58, p = 0.049), recency at 5 s; explained by a dynamic LCA in which leak increases and inhibition decreases with time (Table 1: leak 0.04 + 0.001/step, inhibition 0.1 - 0.0025/step; BIC for the LCA 16,374 and for the absorbing-bound DDM 16,379; the DLCA's BIC value was truncated in the extracted table and is not reported here); integration extends over "30 samples, corresponding to 3 seconds", "almost an order of magnitude" longer than "the moving dot paradigm (about 420 ms; [Kiani 2008])" (Results) — [Bronfman, Brezis & Usher 2016, DOI 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004667](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4750938/)
- Pares-Pujolras et al. (eLife reviewed preprint RP107980, v2 read; v3 exists): two EEG experiments, N = 22 and 21 analysed; one or two 200-ms pulses of coherent motion separated by gaps of [0, 0.5, 1] s (Exp. 1) or [0, 0.12, 0.36, 1.08] s (Exp. 2); responses "after stimulus offset, with no time limit" (delayed, no RT analysis); regression weights pulse 1 beta1 = 0.26 +/- 0.06 and 0.31 +/- 0.05 vs pulse 2 beta2 = 0.16 +/- 0.06 (p = 0.001) and 0.06 +/- 0.04 (p = 0.015); no main effect of gap on accuracy (beta3 = 0.06 +/- 0.08, p = 0.525; -0.003 +/- 0.05, p = 0.579); "there was no evidence of leak in our data"; a three-parameter bounded accumulator (bound, two drifts; no leak, no noise in gaps) was fit, and primacy is attributed to participants who "sometimes terminate decisions early despite a cost in accuracy"; CPP returned to baseline between pulses while motor beta lateralisation sustained a coherence-dependent level through the gap (extracted) — [Pares-Pujolras, Geuzebroek, O'Connell & Kelly, DOI 10.7554/eLife.107980](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/107980v2)
- Kumano & Uka 2024 (abstract only): "We trained two macaque monkeys to switch between direction and depth discrimination tasks in successive trials. Critically, the effects of irrelevant pulses... exhibited an initial minimal effect, followed by an increase and subsequent decrease as a function of pulse timing. Gating mechanisms alone... did not account for the observed time course... the observed increase in the effects of irrelevant pulses with time suggested the involvement of a leaky integration mechanism" (Abstract) — [Kumano & Uka 2024, DOI 10.1097/WNR.0000000000001980](https://doi.org/10.1097/WNR.0000000000001980)

### Inferences
- The only 2018-2026 primate study with pulse timing as the manipulated variable in a choice task is Kumano & Uka 2024; its "early > late relevant pulse" pattern replicates Kiani 2008, and its leaky-integration inference comes from irrelevant pulses' delayed rise, which has no counterpart in our single-rule task.
- Trueblood 2021 is the closest RT-task estimate of leak from changing evidence in humans (~200-250 ms) but is a model-based inference with urgency and relative processing co-estimated; without the full text, the identifiability of leak vs urgency in their design cannot be assessed here.
- Fetsch 2018 shows that an early perturbation's effect on choice can vanish for longer stimulus durations through compensation/readout changes, an alternative to leak or urgency for RT-dependent loss of pulse effects; Holmes 2016's > 0.4 s lag before new evidence is incorporated is a caution about assuming instantaneous pulse incorporation.

### Gaps
- Full texts of Kumano & Uka 2024 (402 at the publisher) and Trueblood 2021 (bioRxiv rate-limited; APA paywall) were not read; pulse durations, onset times and effect sizes for Kumano & Uka are unknown.
- No 2018-2026 human RT study with brief (< 100 ms) pulses at fixed early onsets analogous to ours was found in forward-citation sweeps (2018-2026) of Huk 2005, Kiani 2008, Cisek 2009, Winkel 2014, Carland 2016 and Evans 2017 filtered on pulse/urgency/leak terms (473 citing works screened by title).

## Cross-study synthesis: which analyses transfer to early pulses (50-420 ms) with decisions 0.5-2 s later

### Takeaway
Three analysis families recur. (1) Pulse effect vs time-from-pulse-to-response (Huk 2005 Fig. 4; Stine 2023 Fig. S1): persistence to ~0.9-1 s with no reversal is the published signature of (bounded) integration and matches our RT < 1.3 s result, but Evans 2017 shows that a decline across RT bins is also produced by leak-free models with drift variability, so this cannot by itself exclude leak. (2) Pulse effect vs pulse onset time: a decline is predicted by bound crossing (Kiani 2008; Huk 2005), collapsing bound/urgency and attractor dynamics (Huk 2005; Wong 2007), whereas a stable OU leak predicts later pulses to be more effective (Wong 2007 linear analysis); a flat profile over 50-420 ms (ours) is the perfect-integrator expectation when the bound is rarely reached that early, and Kiani's Fig. S2 logic predicts the onset decline should begin earlier for higher base coherence. (3) Policy manipulation (Carland 2016): slowing decisions should abolish early-pulse effects under tau <= 250 ms leak and should not under perfect integration; this is the only published design that separates leak from bound/urgency with pulses, and it requires a within-subject change in RT independent of the stimulus.

### Cited Findings
- Published pulse persistence windows (time from pulse to response at which choice is still affected): 300-900 ms (monkeys, RT task) — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/); "nearly a second" (monkeys, RT task) — [Stine et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10565788/); first 67 ms of evidence affects mean RT ~1,100 ms later (humans) — [Winkel et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0551-8); congruency advantage gone by ~2 s (humans) — [Evans et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5703954/); 100-ms pulse most effective for RTs 650-750 ms with ~450-480 ms non-decision time (humans) — [Carland et al. 2016](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/CarlandMarcosThuraCisek2016.pdf); no decay across a 1.08-s gap (humans, delayed response) — [Kiani et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3797371/).
- Published onset-time dependence: LIP pulse effect -0.6 sp/s per 100 ms of onset over 100-392 ms (bounded-integrator model -0.1) — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/); choice shift from 1.51 (early quartiles) to ~0 in quartiles 3-4 of > 700-ms fixed-duration trials — [Kiani et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670720/); shift of effective pulse timing from 100 -> 400 ms when RT lengthens by ~300 ms — [Carland et al. 2016](https://www.cisek.org/pavel/Pubs/CarlandMarcosThuraCisek2016.pdf).
- Model-based leak estimates from pulse/evidence-change data in RT tasks: UGM tau fixed at 100-250 ms (Cisek 2009: 200; Thura 2012: 100; Carland 2015: 250; Carland 2016: 167; Hawkins 2015 and Evans 2017: 100) — sources above; free fits: tau = 4,105 ms (SD 2,625) — [Evans et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5703954/); ~200-250 ms (abstract) — [Trueblood et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255); leaky-accumulator tau = 298.5 ms rejected vs bound (fixed duration) — [Kiani et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670720/).
- Pulse gain relative to physical strength: Huk & Shadlen set the model's MT pulse response "to match the observed effect on behavior" rather than deriving it — [Huk & Shadlen 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725829/); Wong et al. used 11% model coherence for a pulse worth 1.6% behaviourally — [Wong et al. 2007](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007/full).

### Inferences
- For our design, the most informative additional analyses suggested by this literature are: (a) pulse effect (log-odds) vs base coherence and onset (Kiani 2008 Fig. S2 and Huk 2005 Fig. 10A additivity: bounded integration predicts smaller/shorter-lived effects on stronger base motion; leak predicts no coherence dependence of the onset profile); (b) an RT-split per mouse of each onset's effect (Carland 2016 Fig. 6 logic) with the caveat that RT splits confound evidence and policy; (c) a model-based comparison in which leak, urgency and pulse gain are all free, fit to pulse-trial choices and RTs jointly, because the RT-binned decline is non-diagnostic by itself (Evans 2017).
- Given that published RT-task persistence windows (0.9-1 s) and gap invariance (1.08 s) both exceed the ~0.5-0.75 s over which a 200-250 ms filter would retain a pulse, a leak time constant in the Cisek-lab range would be hard to reconcile with our persistence to ~1.3 s RT unless urgency is strong and the effect is carried by fast trials; this is exactly the quantitative trade-off Carland 2015/2016 describe, so it should be tested by simulation with our parameters rather than assumed.

### Gaps
- No published study combines (i) very strong brief pulses (100% for 50-67 ms), (ii) fixed early onsets, and (iii) RTs of 0.5-2 s; the literature's pulses are weak (3-6% for 100-200 ms) and either random-time (Shadlen lab) or 100-400 ms fixed onsets (Carland 2016). Nonlinear pulse gain (our 2-7x DDM underprediction) is not addressed in any of the primate/human pulse papers found.
