# Pulse timing alone cannot separate leak from urgency

The literature supports one firm answer: the three descriptive pulse signatures this project already has (a choice shift that is flat across the three onsets, present for responses within about 1.3 s of stimulus onset and absent later without reversal, and faster choices toward the pulse) are each reproduced by a **fixed-bound perfect integrator** once bound depletion, non-decision time and across-trial drift variability are in the model, so on their own they favour neither leak, nor urgency, nor perfect integration ([Zhou et al. 2009, Sec. 3.1 and Fig. 2](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817); [Okazawa et al. 2018, Fig. 3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y); [Evans et al. 2017, Results](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-16694-7)). Leak (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck decay) and urgency (gain (1 + u·t), mathematically identical to a collapsing bound) move a pulse's effect in **opposite directions**, but only per trial still undecided when the pulse arrives, only when their rates exceed roughly **1 /s** for decision times of 0.6–1.3 s, and only after the thresholding decline shared by every model class is removed by simulation ([Wong et al. 2007, Results](https://doi.org/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007); [Trueblood et al. 2021, Figs. 3–4](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255); [Stine et al. 2020, Fig. 7](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365)). The project's control-fitted values (leak 0.07–2.7 /s, urgency −0.08 to 0.14 /s) straddle or sit below that floor, which is why their recovery was poor and why the pulse gain must be free. **No published study in any species has combined brief pulses at several fixed onsets with free-response reaction times and fitted leak and urgency jointly**; recovery of both has been demonstrated only for a single sustained evidence switch placed early relative to RT, fitted jointly with constant-evidence trials and with non-decision time constrained independently ([Murrow & Holmes 2024, Fig. 3 and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/3ca6y); [Evans, Trueblood & Holmes 2020, Results](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)). Section 5 therefore treats the question as parameter inference over a continuous (leak, urgency, pulse gain) family rather than a contest between pure models, pins non-decision time from the 100 % coherence trials, pre-specifies three qualitative statistics that every candidate must reproduce by ex-post simulation, requires confusion and inversion matrices at the real per-mouse trial counts, and opens with a cheap feasibility simulation (already scripted) that can stop the programme before any cluster fit if the candidates' predicted profiles differ by less than the data's uncertainty.

## Every model class shares the thresholding decline; the differences live per undecided trial

The theory is cleanest at the level of the decision variable. Zhou, Wong-Lin and Holmes reduce a three-population circuit to the scalar process dX = (kX + I(t))dt + σ dW, where k < 0 is a stable Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process (leak toward the starting point), k > 0 an unstable one (self-excitation), k = 0 the pure drift-diffusion model, and a drift b(t) = b₀·t stands in for urgency ([Zhou et al. 2009, Eqs. 5–7 and Sec. 2.2](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)). For a step pulse of height p and duration T_p starting at T, the linearised analysis of Wong, Huk, Shadlen and Wang gives the displacement of the accumulated evidence as **ΔS = pτ e^{(T−t)/τ}(e^{T_p/τ} − 1)** for a leaky process with time constant τ, which in the limit of large τ becomes **ΔS ≈ pT_p**, "independent of the pulse onset time T, but dependent on the integral (total area) of the pulse" ([Wong et al. 2007, Results, "Reverberation, not leak"](https://doi.org/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007)). The three classes are then separated by what happens to that displacement between the pulse and the decision: a perfect integrator keeps it permanently; a leak erodes it as e^{−|k|(t−T)}, so "an earlier pulse is gradually 'forgotten'" and "the impact of a brief pulse should be larger with a later onset time" until the bound truncates it ([Wong et al. 2007, Results](https://doi.org/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007)); an unstable integrator amplifies it, so earlier pulses dominate and "the change in the activity decreases with pulse onset time" ([Wong et al. 2007, Results and Fig. 8](https://doi.org/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007)). Under a collapsing bound with perfect integration the displacement is permanent but becomes a growing fraction of the shrinking bound, which is why "urgency is effectively a reduction of decision bound over time and leads to inflation of the psychophysical kernel" ([Okazawa et al. 2018, Fig. 6a–c](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)); that this gain formulation and the collapsing bound are the same model is stated explicitly for additive urgency ("mathematically identical to a model in which the standard DDM is coupled with time-varying decision bounds", [Murphy et al. 2016, Methods](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms13526)) and, for any gain function U(t), by Smith and Ratcliff, who add that "the number of identifiable parameters in the model is one fewer than the number of parameters in the function U(t)" ([Smith & Ratcliff 2022, Equivalence section](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000301)). The urgency-gating model of the Cisek laboratory is a different animal: the evidence is low-pass filtered with a time constant of 100–250 ms and then multiplied by a growing urgency signal, so a pulse's trace decays with the filter but is rescaled by U(t) at the moment of decision ([Carland et al. 2015, Eqs. 1–4](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0851-2); [Cisek et al. 2009, Eq. 2 and model 6](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1844-09.2009)); its noise variance grows as t³ rather than t, so it is "not identical" to an accumulator even with constant evidence ([Hawkins et al. 2015, J Neurophysiol, Fig. 1](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00088.2015)).

Every one of these predictions is stated per undecided trial. Pooled over all trials, all classes show the same decline with onset because "for later perturbations, more trials have already crossed threshold, and so there are relatively fewer trials that are perturbed than unperturbed" ([Zhou et al. 2009, Sec. 3.1](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)); Kiani, Hanks and Shadlen's phrase for the same thing is that "information is simply ignored once the decision bound is attained" ([Kiani et al. 2008, Results](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4761-07.2008)), and Wong et al. warn that "the truncation of firing rate by a decision threshold inevitably introduces nonlinear effects" into single-pulse protocols ([Wong et al. 2007, Discussion](https://doi.org/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007)). On top of that shared decline, Zhou et al.'s 10⁶-trial simulations give the class-specific shapes of the change in mean decision time: **near-constant for T/τ₀ ≤ 0.5 under both constant drift and time-dependent drift** (with the time-dependent drift smaller), **an interior maximum at T/τ₀ ≈ 0.5 for the stable OU** ("SOU is alone in having an onset time for which the perturbative effect is maximized"), and **a monotonic decrease for the unstable OU** ([Zhou et al. 2009, Fig. 2A–D](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)). The standard deviation of decision time distinguishes the urgency-like model from the pure DDM only through a sign reversal for early pulses, and the authors concede these differences "are small and may not be detectable experimentally" ([Zhou et al. 2009, Fig. 3B and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)). Their second protocol, a pulse followed by an antipulse, yields a zero-effect amplitude ratio **λ\* = e^{−kΔT/2}**: exactly 1 for the DDM and for "all time-dependent drift rates, including those used in Smith et al., 2004; Ditterich, 2006b; Churchland et al., 2008", above 1 for leak (1.221 predicted, 1.223–1.227 simulated for k = −1, ΔT = 0.4) and below 1 for self-excitation (0.905 predicted, 0.903–0.907 simulated for k = 0.2, ΔT = 1) ([Zhou et al. 2009, Eqs. 14–16, Table 2 and Fig. 4](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)). The pulse–antipulse ratio is thus the one analytic statistic that is blind to urgency and sensitive to leak; it requires paired opposite pulses within a trial, which the mouse design does not contain.

Two further results fix what a pulse does to choice as a function of time, and both come from the kernel theory of Okazawa and colleagues. In a DDM with constant bound and noise and zero non-decision time, the stimulus-aligned psychophysical kernel conditioned on trials not yet decided is proportional to the sensory weights, **K(t) = (2σ_s²/B)·w(t)**, so "neither the integration process nor the boundedness of the integration per se causes a systematic deviation of psychophysical kernels from true sensory weights" ([Okazawa et al. 2018, Eqs. 1–3 and Fig. 3e](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)). Response-aligned kernels, by contrast, "rise monotonically ... as expected for termination with bound crossing", and that rise "does not indicate an increase of sensory weights" ([Okazawa et al. 2018, Fig. 3f and Par16](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)). Leak makes kernels "systematically underestimate the sensory weights, especially for earlier sensory evidence in the trial", mutual inhibition produces early amplification, and a collapsing bound inflates late weights ([Okazawa et al. 2018, Fig. 6a–c and 6j–l](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)). **Inference** (from the theory note; not stated in any source and not yet verified by simulation): for a symmetric zero-drift DDM with absorbing bounds ±a, an eigenmode expansion of the first-passage problem shows that the choice bias among trials deciding at time t_d after a small displacement decays as e^{−3π²σ²(t_d − T)/(8a²)}, a time constant of about 0.27 times the mean zero-coherence decision time a²/σ², while the undecided-pooled stimulus-aligned effect stays flat. If that derivation holds, a perfect integrator predicts a *conditional* effect that fades with the pulse-to-response interval without any leak, and leak merely adds |k| to the decay rate; this is the quantitative form of "bound depletion" that any RT-conditioned analysis must be calibrated against. A late sign reversal is not produced by the leading modes in that symmetric case; in Zhou et al.'s simulations reversals appear for error RTs because "perturbation of long RT trials can change an impending error to a correct choice" ([Zhou et al. 2009, Sec. 3.3 and Fig. 5A](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)), which is the most likely origin of the late reversal the project's control-fitted model predicts and the data lack (`.agents/reports/2026-10-27-pulse-readout-model-rt-split.md`).

For reaction-time distributions the theory is thinner. In a linear model a positive pulse shifts the undecided-trial first-passage density toward the pulse bound, so choices toward the pulse are faster and choices against it slower; Huk and Shadlen observed exactly this and found that "positive and negative pulses simply scaled and shifted the RT distributions seen on no-pulse trials (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, p > 0.6 for all pairwise comparisons)" ([Huk & Shadlen 2005, Results and Fig. 3B](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005)). Zhou et al.'s mean-decision-time changes for positive and negative pulses are mirror images at early onsets, so a symmetric latency change pooled over pulse signs is predicted to be near zero in every linear class, with asymmetries arising only from errors or from nonlinearity ([Zhou et al. 2009, Figs. 2, 5 and 7](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)). The urgency-gating model makes the sharpest RT prediction of all: "individual response times are only affected by early evidence in a subset of trials in which the decision was made within a certain time window of the change in evidence ... Crucially, however, the mean RT for all trials will still undergo a significant shift" ([Carland et al. 2015, "Interpreting model fits"](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0851-2)), and Evans et al. phrase the same contrast for choice: "as the time between early evidence and the decision increases ... the UGM predicts that early evidence will have a decreasing effect on decisions, whereas the diffusion model predicts the effect of early evidence to be constant" ([Evans et al. 2017, Data Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-16694-7)).

Base-coherence dependence follows from where the decision variable sits when the pulse arrives. Linear models depend on pulse area (exactly for the perfect integrator, through (1 − e^{−|k|Δ})/|k| for leak); Huk and Shadlen found the pulse effect "approximately additive with the effects of dot motion coherence" in LIP, with a slope of −2.5 spikes/s per 100 % coherence (95 % CI −4.6 to −0.3) against −1.5 (−3.7 to 0.8) for their simulated bounded integrator, and explained the weaker effect at high coherence by accumulation "closer to the bound at pulse onset" ([Huk & Shadlen 2005, Fig. 10A and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005); numbers extracted). Kiani et al. derived the complementary timing prediction: under bounded accumulation "the pulse should remain effective at later times for weaker motion than for stronger motion", whereas a time-limited or time-weighted process would be "identical for all motion strengths" ([Kiani et al. 2008, Discussion; Fig. S2 not read](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4761-07.2008)). Cain and colleagues add that a soft-threshold (robust) integrator favours "brief, stronger pulses" over "longer, weaker pulses containing the same total evidence", so a 50–67 ms pulse at 100 % coherence is precisely the regime in which linearity in pulse area should not be assumed ([Cain et al. 2013, Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00976.2012)). One last theoretical trap matters for the project's parameterisation: if the pulse itself passes through a time-varying gain (as in Ditterich's model, where the gain multiplies both drift and noise), its effect is "smaller ... near the beginning of the trial (quite similar to a SOU model)", so an urgency model can mimic the leak signature in the onset profile ([Zhou et al. 2009, Appendix B, Eq. 28 and Fig. 9](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817); Ditterich's model via [Asadpour et al. 2024, Eqs. 2–3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42113-023-00194-1)). The project's gain (1 + u·t) on *accumulated* evidence leaves the pulse unscaled at delivery and re-weights it afterwards; neither of Zhou's variants is exactly this model, so its onset profile has to be simulated rather than read off Fig. 2.

## Published studies measured persistence, not leak, and their time constants span 100 ms to 4 s

### Primate pulse experiments

The reaction-time pulse study that defines the field is Huk and Shadlen's. Two monkeys discriminated motion at coherences 0 to ±51.2 % while, on two-thirds of trials, a 100-ms motion pulse was inserted into the dim background texture (not the dots) at one of five onsets, 100, 150, 211, 287 or 392 ms after dot onset ([Huk & Shadlen 2005, Results and Methods](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005); onsets extracted and restated in [Wong et al. 2007, Methods](https://doi.org/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007)). The behavioural result was a shift of the psychometric function by **±1.6 % coherence (95 % CI 1.4–1.7 %)**, "as if the strength of the random dots were altered by this amount for the entire duration of the trial", and a joint diffusion fit to choices and RTs needed no separate RT-specific pulse term (δ = −0.05 %, likelihood-ratio p = 0.70) ([Huk & Shadlen 2005, Fig. 3 and Eqs. 6–7](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005)). The analysis most relevant to this project is their Fig. 4: trials binned in 75-ms steps of RT minus pulse onset showed "a clear effect of the pulse on choice behavior" for responses initiated **300 to 900 ms after pulse onset**, no effect for earlier responses, and nothing reported beyond 900 ms ([Huk & Shadlen 2005, Fig. 4 and Eq. 8](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005)). The paper reports no behavioural analysis by onset time; the onset dependence is a neural result: LIP pulse effects "declined at a rate of −0.6 spikes/s per 100 ms of pulse onset time (95 % CI −0.9 to −0.3; p < 0.001)", whereas a simulated perfect bounded integrator (bound 68 spikes/s, MT latency 100 ms, LIP latency 125 ms) declined by only −0.1 (−0.4 to 0.1; p = 0.29) ([Huk & Shadlen 2005, Fig. 10B and Table 1](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005); extracted). Their interpretation is the template for everything that followed: "the presence of the decision bound explains the dramatic decline of pulse effects with time", "our experiments do not furnish a measurement of the integration time constant", leak "can decrease the temporal persistence of pulse effects" but "should not preferentially affect the strength of pulses that occur later in time", and "an attractive possibility is that the decision bound is not stationary but decreases over time" or, "equivalently, an 'urgency' signal might be added to the accumulated evidence" ([Huk & Shadlen 2005, Results and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005)). Two details matter for the mouse project: the model's pulse strength was set by hand "to match the observed effect on behavior" rather than derived from the stimulus, and the two monkeys differed by a factor of 2.4 in pulse sensitivity (γ = 2.15 % and 0.89 %) ([Huk & Shadlen 2005, Table 2 and Results](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005); extracted).

Wong et al. reproduced the protocol in a biophysical attractor network and showed that the weakening of later pulses arises because the network state "has time to accelerate away from the unstable point"; their linear analysis is the clearest published statement that a stable leaky integrator predicts the opposite (later pulses more effective) and a perfect integrator predicts invariance, and they propose a double-pulse protocol (150 and 250 ms) whose non-additivity ("the effect is dominated by the first pulse") would distinguish the attractor from "the diffusion model with a collapsing bound or urgency" ([Wong et al. 2007, Figs. 8–10 and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.3389/neuro.10.006.2007)). Whether their Fig. 8B behavioural decline is data or model output is not clear from the text that was read. Kiani, Hanks and Shadlen then used pulses in a fixed-duration task (80–1,500 ms): ±3.2 % for 200 ms at a uniformly random time biased choices by **1.3 ± 0.1 % coherence overall**, but among trials of 700 ms or longer only the first two timing quartiles were effective (quartile 3: 0.2 ± 0.4 %, p = 0.26; quartile 4: −0.1 ± 0.4 %, p = 0.63; onset slope α₄ = −0.40 ± 0.19 per quartile, p = 0.018) ([Kiani et al. 2008, Fig. 5 and Eq. 5](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4761-07.2008)). Their rejection of leak did not come from the pulses: it came from accuracy-versus-duration fits in which a bounded accumulator (k = 0.46, B = 19.9) beat a leaky one (τ = 298.5 ms) by a BIC difference of 59.5, a Bayes factor of 8.35 × 10¹², together with motion-energy kernels that favoured early over late evidence ([Kiani et al. 2008, Methods and Fig. 4C–D](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4761-07.2008); extracted). The Shadlen laboratory's later RT studies used weak (3.2–4.0 %), 100-ms pulses at random times 0.1–0.8 s after onset on about half of trials and report only that "pulses shown early in the trial affected choices made nearly a second later", with LIP pulse effects persisting with a Gaussian σ of 0.11 s against 0.03 s in the superior colliculus (p < .001); the behavioural curves are in supplements that were not accessible, so no effect sizes or persistence shapes can be quoted ([Stine et al. 2023, Results, Fig. S1 and STAR Methods](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2023.05.028); [Steinemann et al. 2024, Methods and Fig. 1—figure supplement 1](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.90859)). Kumano and Uka (abstract only) report that in monkeys switching between direction and depth tasks "early relevant pulses ... had a significantly larger effect compared to late pulses" and that irrelevant pulses showed a rise-then-fall with timing which they attribute to "a leaky integration mechanism" ([Kumano & Uka 2024, Abstract](https://doi.org/10.1097/WNR.0000000000001980)).

The strongest primate and human bounds on decay come from two-pulse designs with a delayed response. Kiani, Churchland and Shadlen separated two 120-ms pulses by 0, 120, 360 or 1,080 ms and found accuracy "largely unaffected by interpulse interval" (per-subject gap coefficients from −0.75 ± 0.38 to 0.39 ± 0.41, one at p = 0.05), the first pulse's evidence "maintained without any significant decay or loss", and the second pulse weighted more (β₃ = −12.9 ± 3.6, p = 3.4 × 10⁻⁴), concluding that the mechanism must be "capable of maintaining the fidelity of accumulated evidence for at least 1 s" ([Kiani et al. 2013, Table 1 and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2094-13.2013); extracted). Parés-Pujolràs and colleagues replicated the gap invariance with EEG (gap coefficients 0.06 ± 0.08, p = 0.525, and −0.003 ± 0.05, p = 0.579), found first-pulse weights above second-pulse weights (0.26 vs 0.16; 0.31 vs 0.06), and explained the primacy by "a bounded process where participants sometimes terminated evidence accumulation early", with a model that "has no way to produce systematic gap-dependent effects" ([Parés-Pujolràs et al. 2025, Secs. 2.1–2.2 and Fig. 4](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.107980); reviewed preprint). **Inference**: under an OU leak a 1.08-s gap attenuates a pulse by e^{−λ·1.08}, so these nulls imply λ ≪ 1 /s in humans in interrogation designs; they say nothing about an RT task in which the bound ends the trial. Two sensory-stage results complete the primate picture: Fetsch et al. found that optogenetic suppression of MT shifted choices only for stimulus durations below about 350 ms (−4.7 %, p = 0.0006, versus −0.6 %, p = 0.50, for long durations) ([Fetsch et al. 2018, Figs. 6–7](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.36523)), and Yates et al. found that MT responses to successive 150-ms pulses decayed within the trial while LIP kernels were sustained ([Yates et al. 2017, Results](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.4611)), two non-leak, non-urgency reasons for onset-dependent pulse effects.

### Human changing-evidence experiments and the urgency-gating debate

The Cisek laboratory's claim that evidence is low-pass filtered with a short time constant rests on identical-evidence comparisons. In the tokens task, "bias-for" and "bias-against" trials have identical evidence after the sixth token yet produced indistinguishable decision times (example subject 1,885 ± 145 vs 1,957 ± 191 ms; not significant for 21 of 22 subjects) even for decisions made within 400 ms of the sixth token (mean difference −9 ms, SD 68, in 16 subjects); a diffusion model predicted faster bias-for responses (KS p < 0.01), leaky diffusion with L = 0.0005 did not resolve this, and an urgency-gating model with a 200-ms filter did ([Cisek et al. 2009, Figs. 5 and 7](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1844-09.2009); extracted). The same paper flags the inconsistency that motivates this report: Kiani et al. found "a short window for motion integration (~300–350 ms)" whereas Huk and Shadlen "obtained a much longer window", and "it remains unclear why such different results were obtained in these two studies" ([Cisek et al. 2009, Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1844-09.2009)). Thura et al. repeated the design with coherence steps (no difference for 24 of 30 subjects; filter τ = 100 ms) ([Thura et al. 2012, Figs. 7–8 and Methods](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.01071.2011)), and Thura and Cisek saw "no effects of within-trial history differences" in monkey PMd/M1, "as if the sensory information was not integrated but instead simply low-pass filtered with a short time constant (less than 200 ms)" ([Thura & Cisek 2014, Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.01.031)).

Carland, Marcos, Thura and Cisek's random-dot study is the only published design that separates leak from bound or urgency with brief pulses, and it does so through a policy manipulation. Forty-four humans received 100-ms pulses that doubled coherence from 3 % to 6 % at 100, 200 or 400 ms; when identical trials were interleaved with variable-motion trials, no-pulse RTs lengthened (42 of 44 subjects; subject JM 727 → 1,057 ms), and in the fast subgroup "the 100 ms pulses lost their efficacy (P = 0.052), while the 400 ms pulses became effective (P = 2.1 × 10⁻⁵)", with 6 of 9 well-sampled subjects showing the shift (significant in 4) ([Carland et al. 2016, Figs. 4A, 5A and Results](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00264.2015)). The window was short: "the 100 ms pulse is most effective on RTs between 650 and 750 ms (blocked sessions), while the 400 ms pulse is most effective (in interleaved sessions) ~300 ms later", with an estimated non-decision time of 451 ms ([Carland et al. 2016, Fig. 6](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00264.2015)). A UGM with **τ = 167 ms** reproduced the pattern, a 250-ms filter "consistently predicts a significant effect of the 100 ms pulse even in the interleaved sessions", and the DDM reproduced the blocked sessions but "also predicted that the 100 ms pulses had a significant effect (P = 4.4 × 10⁻¹¹)" when policy slowed; a DDM with a 150 ± 100 ms pre-integration delay removed that effect but produced only 0.7 % of decisions under 200 ms against 11.6 % observed ([Carland et al. 2016, Figs. 9–10 and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00264.2015)). Accuracy effects were small and "do not help to distinguish between the models", and the authors concede that below 250 ms "small variations of [τ] can be traded off against changes in intertrial variability parameters" ([Carland et al. 2016, Results and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00264.2015)). The companion commentary shows why the window argument has teeth: with a 250-ms filter "all RTs longer than 950 ms (97 % of the data of Winkel et al.) would be determined purely by random fluctuations" unless urgency drives threshold crossing ([Carland et al. 2015, "Interpreting model fits"](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0851-2)).

The opposing camp measured lasting influence. Winkel et al. found that 67 or 117 ms of early evidence changed mean correct RT and error rate 1.1–1.3 s later in a graded way (linear contrast F(1,1) = 164.6, p < .001 for RT; 50.6 for errors), fit by a DDM (RMSE 15 ms) better than an unfiltered UGM (23 ms), and judged "a large leak seems unlikely, considering that the first 67 ms of evidence have pronounced effects 1,100 ms later" ([Winkel et al. 2014, Table 1 and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0551-8)). Evans, Hawkins, Boehm, Wagenmakers and Brown ramped late evidence at four rates after a 467-ms early pulse in 65 participants and found no congruency-by-ramp-rate interaction (Bayes factors of 19:1 for the null on RT and 39:1 on accuracy), a leak-free diffusion model that beat a 100-ms UGM (which underestimated median RT by 445 ms and accuracy by 16 %), and, when the filter time constant was freed, **τ = 4,105 ms (SD 2,625)**, which makes the UGM "less leaky, and more like the diffusion model" ([Evans et al. 2017, Results and Table 1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-16694-7); extracted). Their most important methodological result is negative: the congruent-minus-incongruent accuracy advantage "disappears at about 2 s", yet both models predict a declining conditional-accuracy function through between-trial drift variability, so that decline "does not discriminate them" ([Evans et al. 2017, Results and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-16694-7)). Ferrucci et al. later argued that a UGM with a 250-ms filter fits Winkel's data and that Evans's UGM lost partly because its urgency slope was fixed ([Ferrucci et al. 2021, Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1009455)). Trueblood, Heathcote, Evans and Holmes resolved the dichotomy by fitting a continuous family in which the urgency ratio k = m/b and the leak L are both free: across three flashing-grid experiments with a single evidence reversal, **k ≈ 1 (0.95–1.6) and a leak time scale of about 200–250 ms**, and "these effects can only be identified in a changing information paradigm" ([Trueblood et al. 2021, Figs. 7c, 9, 10 and Abstract](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)).

Constant-evidence RT distributions add a third line. Hawkins, Wagenmakers, Ratcliff and Brown showed that an evidence-accumulation model and a UGM (filter 100 ms, urgency βt) are discriminable from RT shape alone (positively skewed versus near-Gaussian with slow errors), with model recovery "accurate for every single synthetic data set" at 800 trials per subject; the accumulator won in 15 humans and the UGM in two macaques ([Hawkins et al. 2015, J Neurophysiol, Figs. 2–5](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00088.2015); extracted). Their companion survey found fixed bounds favoured in 72 of 85 humans and in 64 (very strong) plus 12 (strong) of 91 replication participants, dynamic bounds in 6 of 8 extensively practised macaques, and the urgency model preferred for only 3 of 93 subjects ([Hawkins, Forstmann et al. 2015, Fig. 5](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2410-14.2015)). Under a 1.4-s deadline an urgency DDM beat the standard DDM (P < 0.001) and the conditional-accuracy slope fell from −0.35 to −2.03, but the case against bound adjustment rested on EEG motor-preparation signals, not behaviour ([Murphy et al. 2016, Results](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms13526)). Holmes, Trueblood and Heathcote's mid-trial motion reversals revealed "a substantial time delay before the changed motion information is integrated", estimated at more than 0.4 s ([Holmes et al. 2016, Abstract and Sec. 3.7](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2015.11.002)), and Okazawa et al. showed that human RT-task kernels are quantitatively predicted (R² = 0.57) by a bounded DDM with non-decision time and static weights ([Okazawa et al. 2018, Fig. 4e–f](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)).

### Rodent accumulation: λ ≈ 0 in static tasks, adjustable in volatile ones, and no mouse RT pulse study

Rodent leak estimates come almost entirely from fixed-duration pulsed-evidence tasks in which the pulse times are known exactly, so λ is identified from choices alone. Brunton, Botvinick and Brody's nine-parameter click model put **|λ| = 0.91 ± 0.15 s⁻¹ in rats and 0.23 ± 0.071 s⁻¹ in humans** (means of magnitudes), "comparable to or greater than the longest stimulus duration used", with λ "statistically indistinguishable from zero" in the example rat and "a range of accumulation strategies" across animals; the constraint came from a flat psychophysical reverse correlation and from accuracy rising with duration ([Brunton et al. 2013, Fig. 2B, E, H and Fig. 3A](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1233912); [DePasquale et al. 2024, Results](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.84955)). Per-rat values with signs in a second cohort span −2.74 to +2.26 s⁻¹, the "meta-rat" is slightly unstable (λ = 1.227, τ ≈ 0.8 s), and bilateral frontal inactivation made accumulation leaky (λ = −4.144, bootstrap 95 % CI −7.91 to −1.31) with the warning that "noise and lapse covary strongly" ([Erlich et al. 2015, Tables 1–2 and Fig. 4](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.05457)). When the environment switches at 1 Hz, rats discount with **λ ≈ −4.3 to −6.6 s⁻¹ (τ ≈ 150–230 ms)**, within 1 % accuracy of the optimum, an exponential fit to the reverse-correlation kernel "reliably recovers the discounting rate λ" in synthetic agents, and halving the hazard roughly halves the leak ([Piet et al. 2018, Figs. 3d, 5a–e and 6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-06561-y); per-rat values from the arXiv Table 1). A replication cohort was leaky in every rat and differed from the stationary-task cohort (p < 0.01) ([Boyd-Meredith et al. 2022, Fig. 1g](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-30736-3)). Rats counting LED flashes had time constants "close to or greater than 2 s" for most animals and flat kernels ([Scott et al. 2015, Fig. 2B and Figure 2—figure supplement 1](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.11308)). In mice the two fixed-duration datasets agree: in a virtual T-maze **λ = 0.03 ± 0.09 m⁻¹ (P = 0.78)**, about 0.02 s⁻¹ at the recorded running speed (the note's arithmetic), with a mild primacy (early/late weight ratio 0.73 ± 0.06, P = 1.3 × 10⁻⁴) ([Pinto et al. 2018, Figs. 3 and 6C](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2018.00036)), and in a flash-rate task "information throughout the 1000 ms trial influenced choice, with early information most influential", read as "minimal accumulation leak" ([Odoemene et al. 2018, Abstract](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.3478-17.2018); abstract only). Joint neural-behavioural fits show that one behavioural λ can hide opposite circuit dynamics: choices alone gave no cohort difference, yet PPC and striatal activity were best fit by leaky and frontal activity by unstable accumulation, with parameter recovery verified on synthetic data over λ ∈ [−5, 5] s⁻¹ ([DePasquale et al. 2024, Fig. 3 and Figure 1—figure supplements 1–2](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.84955)), and population recordings place the autonomous dynamics in an unstable regime followed by a commitment transition ([Luo et al. 2025, Fig. 4 and Extended Data Fig. 10](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09528-4)).

Rodent reaction-time work has never manipulated evidence within a trial, so it bears on the bound and urgency side only. In a free-response flash task with 95 mice, 21 rats and 18 adolescent humans, a collapsing-boundary DDM beat fixed-bound and linear-urgency DDMs in rodents (**mice p < 0.0005, F = 22.12; rats p = 0.0014**; humans p = 0.13), mice had the fastest collapse (lowest semi-saturation constant, p = 0.004) and the highest urgency slope and onset delay, yet accuracy still rose with RT in every species, peaking at 0.7–1 s; recovery was reported for the classic DDM only, and reviewers flagged that the urgency model was undefined and that "at least two of these variability parameters can not be recovered" ([Chakravarty et al. 2025, Figs. 2F, 4 and Supplementary Fig. 3; reviews](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.105116.1); reviewed preprint). Rats in RT random-dot tasks show accuracy that rises with RT (slope +0.16 ± 0.14 over 53 epochs from 11 rats) where humans show the opposite (−0.16 ± 0.18), with rat errors faster than correct responses ([Shevinsky & Reinagel 2019, Figs. 3–4](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.01211)); Reinagel concluded that in this task response timing "is governed more by elapsed time than by a criterion for sufficient evidence" ([Reinagel 2013, Abstract](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0068505)), Nguyen and Reinagel reproduced the rat pattern with starting-point variability while stating that "fitting the parameters of this model is problematic" ([Nguyen & Reinagel 2022, Figs. 2–3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.794681)), and Asadpour et al. fit the same rats with Ditterich's time-variant gain applied to the drift only, which produces fast errors ([Asadpour et al. 2024, Abstract](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42113-023-00194-1)). The de la Rocha laboratory's rat RT models carry a leak parameter together with a stimulus-independent urgency process that initiates movement on its own, accounting for express responses in 34.3 ± 12.2 % of trials ([Molano-Mazón et al. 2024, Figs. 1 and 5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-53586-7)), and their abstract-only account of time–intensity scale invariance argues for "bounded exact accumulation" ([Pardo-Vazquez et al. 2019, Abstract](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-019-0439-7)). In mice the nearest RT precedents are a change-detection task explained by integration "on the timescale of ∼1 s" plus temporal expectation ([Orsolic et al. 2021, Fig. 2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2021.03.031)) and Chakravarty's flash task; Europe PMC searches for mouse reaction-time studies with motion pulses or fitted leak/urgency returned nothing else, so the project's dataset has no direct precedent (rodent note, Q8 search record).

## Leak and urgency are recoverable only from early evidence changes with non-decision time pinned

The recovery literature explains the project's control-trial failure before any pulse is considered. Evans, Trueblood and Holmes simulated 4,000 parameter sets per model with 1,000 trials each in fixed- and changing-evidence conditions and found that "the difference in drift rate between alternatives ... recovers extremely well, regardless of the time-variant function", but that "time-variant and initial threshold parameters are either both recovered well, or both recovered poorly ... a key tradeoff exists between the initial threshold and these time-variant parameters"; only the **linearly collapsing DDM "precisely recovers all parameters"**, the Weibull collapse "cannot recover any of the threshold parameters adequately", the leakage-formulated UGM recovers leak only when "large leakage rates" correspond to times shorter than the decision times, and its urgency parameter is "not identifiable" because a change of variables removes it from the equation ([Evans et al. 2020, Results, Figs. 2–6 and Appendix C](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)). Adding a single evidence reversal at 250 ms helped only marginally ("no model parameters qualitatively shifted from 'non-recoverable' to 'recoverable'"), and the authors stress that "the ability of a model to accurately fit a data set, and the ability of that model to precisely recover parameters are different" ([Evans et al. 2020, Discussion](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)). Trueblood et al.'s nondimensionalised family makes the same point from the other side: with fixed evidence "parameter recovery is not possible on the basis of choice and RT data alone, and it appears to be essentially impossible to distinguish between integration versus urgency dominated strategies", whereas a switch at 0.2 s makes k and L recoverable, with the caveat that "leakage is only identifiable when the leakage rate is above L = 1" ([Trueblood et al. 2021, Figs. 3–4](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)). Smith and Ratcliff read those same recoveries as "highly variable" and found urgency–decay correlations of −0.29 to −0.37 in their own fits, concluding that "these parameters are not well identified in data" and that hierarchical models "cannot overcome ... the problem of underidentified parameters at the individual participant level" ([Smith & Ratcliff 2022, Fig. 8 and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000301)).

The most recent full-likelihood study is the most encouraging and sets the design rules. Murrow and Holmes (preprint) recovered a leaky integrator, several collapsing-threshold forms and the UGM at N = 100 to 10,000 trials: "in the FI [fixed information] case, it is very difficult to recover the leakage parameter", but with changing information "the leakage parameter is well recovered", "improved even more when multiple drift rate conditions were used", and for the UGM "recovery is excellent ... parameter recovery is best when the time of evidence change is early relative to typical response times" ([Murrow & Holmes 2024, Fig. 3, Results and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/3ca6y)). Weibull thresholds "are not recoverable for any simulation size N; however, the threshold shape is recoverable", linear collapse recovers best, and "under an appropriate coordinate transformation, the UGM with linear urgency becomes a collapsing threshold model", sharing the exponential threshold's identifiability ([Murrow & Holmes 2024, Results and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/3ca6y)). Hadian Rasanan and colleagues studied exactly the project's hyperbolic form θ/(1 + λt): without an external non-decision-time measurement the starting threshold is "unrecoverable" (R² = 0.08) and the decay rate weak (R² = 0.28), with a trial-level NDT measurement both improve (0.88 and 0.75), "more than 250 trials are required" for the decay rate, and cross-fitting a fixed-threshold model with drift variability into the collapsing model returned a decay rate "approximately zero" in more than 75 % of datasets ([Hadian Rasanan et al. 2025, CCN Figs. 1–2](https://2025.ccneuro.org/abstract_pdf/Rasanan_2025_How_reliably_estimate_collapsing-threshold_diffusion_model.pdf); [eLife reviewed preprint, Figs. 4–5 and 7](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.109730.1)). Amortised inference gives the same ordering: an OU leak parameter recovered with R² of 0.12 and 0.03 (MLP) or 0.41 and 0.45 (CNN) at 1,024 and 4,096 trials, against 0.99 for drift ([Fengler et al. 2021, Appendix 1 recovery table](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.65074)). Stine et al. put the floor in words: "the time-constant becomes less identifiable as its value approaches and exceeds the longest decision times", "an overestimate of the non-decision time would induce an underestimate of the integration time-constant", and "the models can evade punishment for inaccurate estimates of the non-decision time" unless NDT is pinned from "a supplementary block of 300 to 400 FR trials at 100 % coherence" ([Stine et al. 2020, Results, Fig. 7 and Methods](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365)).

The trade-off map is therefore: initial bound against collapse or urgency ([Evans et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01218-0)); leak against urgency ([Smith & Ratcliff 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000301)); collapse against drift, bound and NDT, with κ correlated with all three and fixed κ of 0.3 or 0.8 giving "nearly identical boundaries" ([Voskuilen et al. 2016, Appendix A and Figs. 9–10](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmp.2016.04.008)); NDT against starting threshold and decay ([Hadian Rasanan 2026](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.109730.1)); drift against bound at low error rates, which "even 1,200 trials" do not remove, with a recommendation of 15–35 % errors for small samples ([Lüken et al. 2025, Discussion](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-024-02621-1)); and a sensory delay against leak, since a free lag between evidence change and its uptake "can mimic the effects of leakage" and captured about 98 % of posterior variance in the piecewise DDM ([Trueblood et al. 2021, General Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255); [Holmes & Trueblood 2018, Results](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-017-0901-y)). Zhou et al. supply the same warning in pulse language: "if there is a 'dead time' between stimulus onset and the start of evidence integration, the CD and TD models can display similar signatures to the SOU model, even with high signal-to-noise ratios" ([Zhou et al. 2009, Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)). Mimicry extends beyond parameters to whole models: a fixed-bound diffusion mixed with fast guesses reproduces fast errors and the shape statistics usually credited to urgency ([Ratcliff & Kang 2021, Fig. 4 and Table 1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-94451-7)); a race between evidence and a timing process yields "Gaussian-like RT distributions that have, to date, been most effectively explained with collapsing threshold models" ([Hawkins & Heathcote 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000259)); additive urgency and collapsing bounds make "very similar" predictions ([Boehm et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0958-5)); and in a 37-model recovery case study the generating urgency model was top on average but two other families were "the next-best models" ([Chandrasekaran & Hawkins 2019, Sec. 3](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jneumeth.2019.108432)).

Which designs and statistics carry power is now a short list. A sustained evidence switch early relative to RT, fitted jointly with constant-evidence trials under shared parameters, across more than one difficulty, with NDT constrained, recovers both leak above about 1 /s and the urgency ratio ([Trueblood et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255); [Murrow & Holmes 2024](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/3ca6y)); switch placement at roughly half the typical accumulation time (Holmes et al. individualised it to the median RT minus 150 ms) is the practical heuristic ([Holmes et al. 2016, Secs. 2.1–2.2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2015.11.002)). Early pulses and pulse–antipulse pairs, analysed through the onset dependence of mean and SD decision time, separate OU classes from drift-diffusion with or without urgency, provided errors are rare and the pulse precedes most threshold crossings ([Zhou et al. 2009, Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)). A within-subject policy manipulation that changes RT without changing evidence separates a short filter from bounded integration ([Carland et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00264.2015)). Statistics shown to lack power or to be confounded are RT skew for graded urgency ("weaker skew can be produced by both theories", [Trueblood et al. 2021, Appendix C](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)), conditional-accuracy declines ([Evans et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-16694-7)), flat kernels and accuracy ([Stine et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365)), response-aligned kernels near the response ([Okazawa et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)), and 0.9-quantile or error-RT differences of 10–20 ms ([Voskuilen et al. 2016, Secs. 5–7](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmp.2016.04.008)). The practice literature adds the reporting rules: recovery "in the range that matters for real data", a confusion matrix and the inversion matrix p(true model | selected model), since a model recovered only 30 % of the time can still give 97 % confidence when it is selected ([Wilson & Collins 2019, Box 5 and Appendix 3](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.49547)); a model space containing "at least one model that is able to, and one model that cannot produce the effect of interest", with ex-post simulation to show that only the winner reproduces it ([Palminteri et al. 2017, Guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2017.03.011)); and pre-registration, because 17 modelling teams given the same manipulated RDM datasets averaged only 71 % correct inferences and "modeler's degrees of freedom did affect their inferences" ([Dutilh et al. 2019, Results](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-017-1417-2)). The gap is explicit in every note: **no recovery study used brief (≤ 100 ms) pulses or multiple onsets in an RT task, and no rodent or primate study has fitted leak and urgency jointly to pulsed RT data**, so the mouse design must demonstrate its own identifiability.

## Six pitfalls turn naive pulse signatures into artefacts

Selection on reaction time is the first and most pervasive. Any statistic conditioned on when the response occurred inherits the termination process: response-aligned kernels "show a peak, whose time is dependent on the distribution of non-decision times", and "mechanisms that have a similar effect on psychophysical kernels often have contrasting effects on choices and RTs" ([Okazawa et al. 2018, Fig. 3k and Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)). The project's own result that the pulse effect is present for responses within about 1.3 s and absent later is this kind of statistic; Evans et al. showed that an identical decline across RT bins is produced by a leak-free DDM with drift variability, so it "does not discriminate" leak from perfect integration ([Evans et al. 2017, Results](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-16694-7)), and Carland et al. showed that a 250-ms filter plus steep urgency produces an effect carried entirely by fast trials while "the mean RT for all trials will still undergo a significant shift" ([Carland et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0851-2)). Zhou et al.'s advice to "analyze only data from long RT trials" ([Zhou et al. 2009, Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)) is sound for pooled onset profiles but is itself a selection; the earlier project synthesis made the same point that conditioning on future RT can select different trials after the pulse and that the decision can precede the movement (`dissemination/reports/pulse-thesis-research-2026-10-23.md`, section 2).

Bound depletion has two faces that are easily conflated. Pooled over trials, decided trials are inert, so every class shows a decline with onset ([Zhou et al. 2009, Sec. 3.1](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817); [Kiani et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4761-07.2008)); the LIP simulation of Huk and Shadlen quantifies how much of the observed onset decline a bound alone explains (−0.1 of −0.6 spikes/s per 100 ms) and the remainder was attributed to a collapsing bound, not to leak ([Huk & Shadlen 2005, Fig. 10B](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005)). Conditional on decision time, the same bound produces a fading effect (section 1, inference) and, with collapsing bounds, integration becomes "more transient as the velocity of the collapsing bounds increased" ([Prat-Ortega et al. 2021, Supplementary Fig. 1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-21501-z)); even in interrogation designs early termination produces first-pulse primacy with no leak at all ([Parés-Pujolràs et al. 2025, Sec. 2.2](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.107980)). Kernel shape alone therefore cannot identify leak: absorbing bounds give primacy, reflecting bounds recency, a perfect integrator flat kernels, and an attractor crosses from primacy to recency with duration ([Prat-Ortega et al. 2021, Figs. 1–2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-21501-z)).

Non-decision time and sensory delay enter three ways. No pulse can affect a response made within the non-decision time plus sensory latency, which is why Huk and Shadlen's effects begin about 300 ms after pulse onset and why variable NDT smears that onset ([Huk & Shadlen 2005, Fig. 4](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005); [Okazawa et al. 2018, Fig. 3j–k](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05797-y)). An unconstrained NDT lets non-integration mimic integration and biases leak estimates ([Stine et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55365)), trades off against threshold and decay in the hyperbolic collapse ([Hadian Rasanan 2026](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.109730.1)), and a dead time before integration makes drift-diffusion mimic the leak's onset profile ([Zhou et al. 2009, Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.2009.07-08-817)). Holmes et al.'s delay of more than 0.4 s before a motion reversal is integrated is the empirical caution against assuming that a 50-ms pulse is incorporated instantly ([Holmes et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2015.11.002)); in the mouse model the motion-energy filter latency plays this role and must be fixed, not fitted alongside leak.

Across-trial drift variability produces the slow-error and conditional-accuracy patterns usually attributed to urgency ([Evans et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-16694-7); [Voskuilen et al. 2016, Fig. 11](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmp.2016.04.008)), starting-point variability produces the fast errors seen in rats ([Nguyen & Reinagel 2022, Fig. 2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.794681)), and a fixed bound with drift variability cross-fits into a collapsing-bound model with near-zero collapse ([Hadian Rasanan 2026, Fig. 7](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.109730.1)). Fast-guess mixtures do the same for RT shape ([Ratcliff & Kang 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-94451-7)); the project already carries a fast-guess component, rats produce express responses on a third of trials ([Molano-Mazón et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-53586-7)), and the project's own check found that excess errors on ±100 % trials are slow, not fast guesses (`.agents/reports/2026-10-27-pulse-asymmetry-lapse-checks.md`). Pulse gain is the pitfall specific to this dataset: no primate or human study derives the pulse's effect from its physical strength (Huk and Shadlen set it by hand; Wong et al. used an 11 % model pulse for a 1.6 % behavioural effect), a robust integrator predicts that brief strong pulses exceed their area-equivalent ([Cain et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00976.2012)), and the project's 2–7-fold underprediction means that any mechanism test with the gain fixed at its motion-energy value is a test of the front end, not of leak or urgency. Freeing the gain removes the magnitude constraint, so the discriminating information must come from *shapes* across onset, pulse-to-response interval and coherence. Four lesser hazards complete the list: MT adaptation makes later pulses weaker at the input ([Yates et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.4611)); an abrupt evidence increase can suppress motor output briefly, producing a dip in the RT distribution that a generalised DDM with leak and urgency cannot fit ([Shinn et al. 2022, Results](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-27697-4)); early evidence changes how later evidence is processed ("relative" processing, [Trueblood et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)); and censoring at a response window "is a data-censoring process that can bias estimation" ([Smith & Ratcliff 2022, Method](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000301)), which for this project means the 5-s window and the responded-only rule must be reproduced in every simulation.

## A calibrated design for the ten mice

### Framing, data set and what the literature licenses

The question is posed as **parameter inference over the continuous family (leak L, urgency u, pulse gain w)**, with the fitted model's remaining components (motion-energy drive, across-trial drift variability, fast-guess mixture) kept, rather than as a contest between "the leaky model" and "the urgency model". This is the only framing the identifiability literature supports (Trueblood et al.'s k–L family; Murrow and Holmes's linear-urgency/exponential-collapse equivalence; Evans et al.'s "contrasting different models based on how well they fit data, rather than basing conclusions on parameters" when the form is not recoverable), and it also matches the project's model space, in which u on accumulated evidence is the hyperbolic collapse a/(1 + u·t). The data set is responded trials only (author rule of 2026-10-27, `.agents/STATUS.md`), symmetric cells with both pulse signs scheduled, |coherence| ≤ 36 % for pulse contrasts, mouse as the unit of inference, and rig entered only as a covariate (memory rule: no location split). **Inference** on scale: for decision times of 0.6–1.3 s, Trueblood's floor (L > 1 /s) and Stine's rule (τ shorter than the longest decision times) mean that only leak rates above about 1 /s and urgency slopes that change the effective bound appreciably within a second (u of order 1 /s, since (1 + u·t) at t = 1.3 s is only 1.18 for the largest control-fitted u = 0.14 /s) are testable; the control-fitted leak range 0.07–2.7 /s crosses that floor, so the answerable question is "is L ≥ 1 /s or u ≥ 1 /s present, or is neither?", not "what is the exact leak". **Inference** on onsets: from Wong's formula a leak of 1 /s makes a 417-ms pulse 1.4 times as effective per undecided trial as a 55-ms pulse (ΔT = 0.36 s; 3.0 times for 3 /s; 1.2 and 1.7 times between the 237- and 417-ms onsets), whereas the recorded early-versus-middle difference is 0.14 log-odds with a 95 % interval of [−0.08, 0.37] on a pooled effect of about 0.3 log-odds (`.agents/reports/2026-10-28-pulse-time-comparison.md`); the earliest onset sits inside the motion-energy latency plus non-decision time (Zhou's dead time), so the middle-versus-late contrast is the informative one, and thresholding pulls in the other direction, which is why this arithmetic motivates but does not replace the simulation.

### Three pre-specified statistics, each computed identically on data and on simulations

Only analyses with a published precedent are used, and each one's reading is fixed in advance by the model simulations rather than by its raw shape. The pulse-to-response profile (S1) follows Huk and Shadlen's Fig. 4 and Stine et al.'s persistence analysis: per mouse and onset, the choice effect in bins of RT minus pulse onset, with control trials assigned the onset's median as a virtual onset and only trials responding after the pulse treated as eligible ([Huk & Shadlen 2005, Fig. 4](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4684-04.2005)); `scripts/ddm/pulse_leak_urgency_signatures.py` already implements it with bins of 0.3–5 s, a trial bootstrap of 300 resamples per bin and simulation of matched leak (1, 3, 10 /s) and urgency (0.3, 1, 3 /s) variants at pulse gains of 1 and 4 (the latter about the choice-matched gain in PSUIM6/7, script docstring). The onset-by-coherence profile (S2) follows Kiani et al.'s Fig. S2 logic and Huk and Shadlen's additivity test: the log-odds effect per onset within |coherence| bins of 0, 9, 18 and 36 %, where bounded integration predicts an onset decline that begins earlier at higher coherence, leak predicts a per-undecided-trial rise with onset that is the same at all coherences (inference, Table 1), and urgency predicts an accelerated decline. The existing onset test (no change across onsets) becomes one row of this table; its coherence dimension is new, and the prior report that the effect-versus-coherence-change slope was inconclusive (0.27 [−0.16, 0.69] logit per 100 %, `.agents/reports/2026-10-27-pulse-effect-vs-coherence-change.md`) warns that the design range is narrow. The RT signature (S3) follows Huk and Shadlen's Fig. 3B and Zhou et al.'s Figs. 2–3: quantile shifts of RT for choices toward versus against the pulse (already in the script), the mean-RT change for positive versus negative pulses by onset, and, as a tertiary statistic that Zhou et al. say "may not be detectable", the sign of the SD-of-RT change by onset. The pulse–antipulse ratio and the policy manipulation are listed as unavailable: the first needs paired opposite pulses within a trial, the second needs a within-mouse change of decision policy independent of the stimulus, and a within-mouse RT split (Carland et al.'s Fig. 6) confounds evidence with policy and is reserved for a labelled exploratory check using session-level RT as a proxy.

### The model comparison

Per mouse, the (L, u, w) family is fitted jointly to control and pulse trials with the project's simulation-based quantile likelihood, the responded-only censoring at 5 s, five-fold cross-validation, and the non-decision time **fixed or tightly bounded from the 100 % coherence trials and the motion-energy latency before** L and u are freed, following Stine et al. and Hadian Rasanan et al. The nested restrictions L = 0, u = 0 and L = u = 0 are compared by held-out negative log-likelihood, but held-out likelihood is the **secondary** statistic: the primary decision is Palminteri's ex-post test, in which each fitted model is simulated on the real control and pulse schedules and must reproduce S1, S2 and S3 within the data's bootstrap uncertainty, with a model that fails the qualitative shape of S1 (effect inside the 1.3-s window, none later, no reversal) disqualified regardless of its likelihood. The competitor set must contain the mimics the literature names: the fixed-bound integrator with drift variability and fast guesses (the project's primary model), the same with L free, with u free, with both, and, as robustness alternatives rather than hypotheses, a stochastic-deadline or timed-response variant ([Hawkins & Heathcote 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000259); [Malhotra et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000286)) and the extrema-detection model the project already has. Both AIC-type and held-out comparisons are reported, and the result is stated as a region of the (L, u) plane consistent with the data rather than as a single winner ([Chandrasekaran & Hawkins 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jneumeth.2019.108432); [Trueblood et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000255)). Nothing else is added at the same time: a flexible pulse kernel, a free sensory delay and latent engagement states would each absorb the leak signal (section 3), and the earlier synthesis's rule against adding them simultaneously stands.

### Recovery and power checks, in the order they should run

The feasibility illustration comes first because it is cheap and can end the programme. The existing script, run on the two or three best-sampled mice with the primary control fit, produces S1 under base, leak (1, 3, 10 /s) and urgency (0.3, 1, 3 /s) with bound and drift re-matched to the control trials and the pulse gain at 1 and 4; the question it answers is whether the leak and urgency profiles differ from the base profile by more than the observed per-bin bootstrap SE in at least some bins. If they do not, the pulses carry no information about L or u at these rates and trial counts, and the literature's own gap (no recovery study of brief pulses at multiple onsets) is closed negatively for this dataset. If they do, the second check is parameter recovery in the real regime: simulate from the fitted family on the actual per-mouse schedules (onsets, coherences, pulse signs, 5-s window, responded-only selection) with generating values on a grid spanning L ∈ {0, 1, 3, 10} /s and u ∈ {0, 0.3, 1, 3} /s and the gain matched to the observed 0.3 log-odds, refit with the same pipeline, and report bias and R² for L, u, w and NDT, with at least 20 datasets per cell and Monte Carlo intervals (a run of 0 false positives in 20 null datasets has an upper 95 % bound of 0.168, `dissemination/reports/pulse-thesis-research-2026-10-23.md`). The third check is model recovery: the confusion matrix and the inversion matrix for {neither, leak, urgency, both} at each mouse's trial counts ([Wilson & Collins 2019](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.49547)), with the explicit expectation from Evans et al. that the bound–collapse trade-off and the sv–collapse mimicry will make "both" and "urgency" the hardest cells. The fourth is null calibration of every pre-specified test (false-positive rate with its binomial interval) and a check that the NDT constraint is tight enough to matter, since the leak–NDT trade-off is the one Stine et al. identify as decisive. Power is read from the recovery grid as the smallest L and u at which the inversion matrix exceeds the threshold below; by the literature it will not be below 1 /s.

Reporting follows the project's rules: every number traced to a script and commit, per-mouse results shown with leave-one-mouse-out sensitivity, and the three descriptive signatures retained as the primary empirical contribution whatever the model comparison returns, since the literature does not contain a mouse RT pulse dataset of this kind at all.

### What this design cannot do

It cannot separate a gain on accumulated evidence from a collapsing bound (they are the same model), cannot estimate a leak slower than about 1 /s, cannot distinguish a short sensory filter from a decision-stage leak if the motion-energy latency is left free, and cannot test the urgency-gating model's defining prediction (the policy-dependent shift of the effective onset) without a policy manipulation. It also cannot turn the RT-conditioned absence of an effect after 1.3 s into a time constant: in a 250-ms filter a pulse's trace at 1 s is e^{−4} ≈ 0.018 of its initial value (inference), so a filter of that scale would need the choice effect to be carried almost entirely by fast trials with a steep urgency signal, which is exactly the configuration Carland et al. describe and which the S1 profile per onset, not the pooled window, is designed to expose.

## Conclusion

The field's apparent disagreement between "integration windows of 300 ms" and "persistence to a second" dissolves once the statistics are separated: fixed-duration and RT tasks measure different conditional quantities, and the RT-task persistence that Huk and Shadlen, Stine et al. and this project observe is what a bounded integrator predicts before any leak is invoked. What the literature has never done is let leak, urgency and the pulse's own gain compete on equal terms in a reaction-time task with brief, strong pulses at several onsets; the methods papers say this is possible only if the data contain an evidence change early relative to the decision and the non-decision time is nailed down elsewhere, and this dataset has both ingredients (three onsets inside the first 420 ms and a 100 % coherence condition). The honest expectation from the recovery results is that the test will bound leak above about 1 /s and bound urgency to slopes that collapse the bound within a second, and will otherwise return a region of the (L, u) plane rather than a verdict.

That is still a result worth having, because it converts the project's 2–7-fold pulse-gain failure from an embarrassment into the measurement: a free gain fitted jointly with L and u on pulse and control trials either reconciles the control-trial parameters with the pulse shapes, in which case the integrator with a nonlinear front end stands, or it does not, in which case the shapes across onset, interval and coherence say which dynamic is missing. The feasibility simulation that opens the design costs a few CPU hours and settles, before any cluster time is spent, whether these pulses can carry that information at all.
