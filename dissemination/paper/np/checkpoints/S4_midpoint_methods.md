## Figure 1: apparatus, task and analysis sets

Head-fixed mice viewed random-dot motion and reported leftward or rightward motion by licking the corresponding port. Correct responses were rewarded and errors were followed by a timeout. Responses could register after 0.3 s. Reaction time was measured from motion onset. On the UW rig, the stored response clock began when the response window opened, so preprocessing added the minimum-viewing interval; on the UCLA rig, the response clock began at motion onset. Signed coherence encoded direction, and the analysed baseline levels were 0; 9; 18; 36; 100 percent absolute coherence. Scheduled pulse trials used scheduled replacement at +100 or -100; UW early middle late; UCLA early middle and no late percent coherence; onset strata. Rig-specific replay and assignment operations retained these different schedules. Because the task stimulus could continue after the modelled period, 5 s defined the analysis cutoff and the no-response outcome; it was not treated as the task deadline or evidence that the animal never responded.

Descriptive analyses combined processed valid-response and censored-trial tables, giving 25508 trials within-window responses and 4537 trials censored trials. Censored trials included recorded responses after the cutoff and trials without a recorded response. Within the modelled window they were assigned infinite response time and no choice; later recorded responses were not back-classified as choices. The legacy model input excluded 15 trials: 7 positive; 7 negative; 1 null; onset 0.25-0.28 trials; seconds because their pulse duration was unknown, while descriptive analyses retained them. We therefore kept analysis-specific denominators distinct and did not modify raw data.

Pulse analyses reconstructed scheduled pulse assignment from the pulse flag, baseline and pulse coherences, and onset. They retained trials with responses before onset or during the scheduled pulse. An earlier preprocessing label could reclassify a pulse as control when the response preceded pulse completion, making that label outcome-dependent. We did not use it for assignment-based contrasts. The legacy model input also contained 48 historically labelled control; 43 directions recovered; 38 genuine non-null; 5 recovered null; 5 remaining zero-coherence directions unknowable trials. We left that input unchanged and restricted model–data overlays to common rows.

The descriptive and model sets consequently answered different questions. Descriptive panels counted scheduled categories in the accepted behavioural table, including invalid-duration trials whose outcomes were known. Model fitting required reconstructable input traces and used the smaller legacy table. Assignment-based estimates returned to the joined behavioural table and corrected scheduled labels, whereas model diagnostics used its intersection with legacy inputs. Each Results destination therefore retained its own population; no denominator was silently transferred between analyses.

## Figure 2: stimulus replay and motion-energy input

We regenerated each trial from its recorded seed with rig-specific implementations under 5 s at 60 frames/s; native rendering area-averaged to 0.5 degrees per sample seconds; frames per second; degrees per sample. The replays reproduced programmed coherence changes but were not treated as frame-perfect display records. UCLA acquisition frame rate, dot lifetime, monitor refresh, vertical synchronisation and full screen geometry were not recorded; replay update frames may have reduced its estimated pulse energy. The UW replay placed the pulse at the logged time but left the background one dot step behind the task convention. These uncertainties affected reconstructed input, not scheduled assignment.

The paired stimulus audit used 184 trials: 76 UCLA and 108 UW; 532 trial-by-convention replays; 180 frames; 3 sampled per rig-sign-coherence-onset cell plus 4 atypical UCLA trials; seed=20261025 trials; replays; frames; RNG seed. Each sampled seed was rendered with the pulse present and physically omitted. The branches were identical before onset, but returning to baseline redrew noise-dot directions and caused subsequent random streams to diverge. The audit therefore tested sign-aligned artefacts under one pre-onset coupling, not a unique no-pulse counterfactual. The model counterfactual instead removed the fitted expected pulse component while retaining that trial's residual motion energy.

The audit compared rendered frames, reconstructed motion-energy traces and programmed dot states. Equality before onset checked both replay branching and causal filter support. Comparisons after offset separated the signed mean from the unsigned trial-level difference: a redraw could increase trial-specific disagreement without producing a pulse-direction bias. We evaluated labelled UCLA display-loop alternatives rather than choosing an undocumented acquisition convention. These checks bounded the implemented representation; they did not verify display timestamps or the frames seen by each mouse.

The motion-energy representation used oriented spatial and temporal filters to form quadrature pairs. Squared responses were summed within direction, and leftward energy was subtracted from rightward energy:

\[
M(t)=R_1(t)^2+R_2(t)^2-L_1(t)^2-L_2(t)^2.
\]

Opponent spatiotemporal motion energy can be constructed from oriented filters and quadrature-pair energy operations [@adelson1985spatiotemporal]. This precedent supports the filter architecture, not replay fidelity. Filter constants and support were sigma_c=6; sigma_g=3; temporal rate k=34; temporal support=0.55 degrees; inverse seconds; seconds. We zero-padded the start and spatial boundaries, flipped the temporal kernel for causal convolution and excluded future-frame contributions. Motion energy was averaged over the displayed field and calibrated separately for each rig by the slope relating time-averaged energy to nominal signed coherence. Screen geometry was not corrected because the necessary measurements were unavailable.

The quadrature construction paired complementary spatial and temporal components to form directional filters; reversing their relative signs produced the opposite direction. Squaring and summing removed phase dependence before opponent subtraction. Calibration changed the evidence scale but did not retune filters per mouse or use behavioural outcomes. Between-mouse stimulus sensitivity therefore remained available to the fitted drift gain rather than being absorbed into preprocessing.

For model inputs, full-trial motion energy was decomposed as \(M=S+N\). The expected component \(S\) combined the rig-specific onset response with the mean response to a pulse coherence change, estimated from replays after subtracting the onset response and without using choices. The residual \(N=M-S\) retained seed-specific stimulus variation. Input scaling and temporal expansion followed divide equivalent percent coherence by 100; hold 60-Hz samples on a 1-ms grid scale factor; hertz; milliseconds and extended across the full analysis window rather than ending at the animal's response.

## Figure 3: decision models

Diffusion models use accuracy and response-time distributions jointly to constrain latent decision and nondecision components [@ratcliff2008diffusion; @palmer2005effect; @bogacz2006physics]. This rationale does not validate the present objective, reconstructed input or candidate set.

For trial \(i\), the tested accumulator evolved between absorbing bounds at zero and \(a\):

\[
x_i(t+\Delta t)=x_i(t)+[gM_i(t)+b+\eta_i]\Delta t+\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon_t,
\]

where \(x_i(0)=za\), \(g\) was drift gain, \(b\) was drift offset, \(\eta_i\sim\mathcal N(0,s_v^2)\) was an across-trial drift offset and \(\epsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) was within-trial diffusion noise. Fixed integration settings were dt=0.001; within-trial diffusion variance=1.0 seconds; model variance; fitted common-model bounds were ndt [0,0.8]; a [0.3,9.5]; z [0.2,0.8]; drift_gain [0.5,20]; drift_offset [-2,2]; sv [0,3] seconds and model-specific units. A bound crossing determined choice, and fitted nondecision time was added to crossing time. The sensory-filter delay was already present in \(M_i(t)\). The common model retained drift variability and set leak, urgency and starting-point variability to zero.

Bound separation \(a\) controlled the evidence distance required for a response, and \(z\) expressed the starting point as a fraction of that separation. Drift gain scaled reconstructed input, drift offset captured an input-independent directional tendency, and \(s_v\) allowed a trial-level drift displacement shared across the trajectory. Nondecision time shifted a bound-crossing time without changing the decision path. Parameter bounds were fitting constraints rather than physiological ranges; the fixed diffusion scale set arbitrary evidence units.

The fast-response mixture followed p_guess [0,0.6]; P_right [0,1]; exponential mean delay guess_tau [0.01,0.3] s; guess_t0=0.3 s; off values p=0 bias=0.5 tau=0.1 s probability; seconds. On mixture trials, direction was drawn from the fitted right-response probability and time equalled the fixed mixture onset plus an exponentially distributed delay with fitted mean. The delay was not interpreted mechanistically. Simulated times were floored at the minimum-viewing interval; responses after the cutoff and unresolved simulations became censored outcomes.

The rapid-forgetting approximation added leakage toward the starting point:

\[
x_i(t+\Delta t)=x_i(t)+[gM_i(t)+b+\eta_i-\lambda\{x_i(t)-za\}]\Delta t+\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon_t.
\]

Its fitted leak range, nominal time constants and discretisation were leak lambda [20,200]; nominal time constants 5-50; Euler dt=1 inverse seconds; milliseconds. This was an operational rapidly forgetting candidate after sensory filtering, not an exact discrete-sample extrema detector. Both candidates used the same fast-response parameterisation.

## Figure 3: objective, fitting and control comparison

Models were fitted separately for each mouse by minimising a censored quantile negative log likelihood. Within each coherence-by-condition cell, a multinomial term scored left, right and censored outcomes, followed by conditional response-time histograms for each response direction. Binning, sparse-term omission, smoothing, weighting and empty-cell penalties were 9 conditional RT bins; RT term requires at least 9 observed responses per side and cell; alpha=0.5; RT weight=1; empty prediction penalty=20 per data trial bins; responses; pseudocount; score weight. The objective was a simulation-based comparison score, not a calibrated likelihood.

The multinomial first separated whether a response occurred and which bound it reached. Conditional response-time terms then compared simulated and observed distributions within response direction. The observed-data inclusion rule was fixed before fitting each cell; simulated sparsity did not remove a difficult term. Pseudocount smoothing prevented undefined logarithms, while the explicit empty-prediction penalty prevented an optimizer from benefiting when simulation failed to populate an observed category.

Optimisation and fit simulations used seed=42; DE tol=0 atol=1 maxiter=1000 population=15 times fitted dimensions; bounded Nelder-Mead maxiter=1000 xatol=1e-4 fatol=1e-4; model-selection repetitions=15 RNG seed; optimizer settings; repetitions. Frozen pulse predictions used 200 repetitions per trial repetitions per trial.

This compared specified pipelines on held-out trials from the same mice and sessions, not new animals or a population significance test.

Stratification preserved condition composition before within-condition permutation and cyclic fold assignment. Training excluded the held-out fold for both parameter estimation and selection. Held-out evaluation used a fixed independent simulation seed, and scores were not normalised by mouse trial count before aggregation. The total therefore weighted mice by available held-out trials; mouse-level score signs were also inspected rather than treating the aggregate alone as population replication.
