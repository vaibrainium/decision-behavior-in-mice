# Brief motion pulses expose limits of an evidence-accumulation model in mice

## Abstract

Models that predict ordinary behaviour may still fail when evidence changes abruptly. We tested whether a model-and-input pipeline selected under ordinary evidence could predict responses to randomized strong motion pulses. Here, in 10 mice performing a reaction-time random-dot-motion task, joint held-out predictions of response direction, timing and occurrence favoured the tested accumulator over rapid forgetting by 345.7 NLL units among the tested implementations, yet the frozen pipelines failed to predict the pulse responses. We reconstructed motion energy and fitted both models before testing pulses. Positive rather than negative pulse assignment shifted response direction by 5.844356 percentage points (95% CI, 1.799491 percentage points–9.889221 percentage points; Holm-adjusted P = 0.01941029 probability). At the common final time point relative to logged pulse onset, the cumulative response-associated directional contrast reached 5.798136 percentage points. Descriptively, the observed and predicted group time courses differed: the observed contrast emerged later and remained sustained, whereas predictions appeared early and weakened or partly reversed. In two informative mice, gains improving held-out choices worsened the remaining response objective. Both predictive gates failed, and inadequate sensitivity precluded the confirmatory timing test. Thus, model selection and pulse transfer constrain the tested pipeline without identifying a unique sensory, decision or response mechanism.

## Impact statement

This study shows that held-out motion perturbations can expose predictive limitations hidden by successful model selection on ordinary behaviour.
