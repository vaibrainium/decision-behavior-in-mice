## Results

### The experiment provided choice, response-time and no-response constraints during motion discrimination with scheduled directional perturbations

To obtain complementary constraints on perceptual decisions, we analysed a reaction-time random-dot-motion task in which mice reported leftward or rightward motion by licking the corresponding response port (Figure 1A). The dataset comprised 10 mice mice across 50 mouse-sessions mouse-sessions, collected with two task implementations that shared the decision structure but used different scheduled perturbation timings (Figure 1B). Responses could register after a minimum viewing interval of 0.3 s s, and the analysis classified trials without a registered response by 5 s s as no response by 5 s. This cutoff was an analysis boundary rather than a shared task-termination time: the records included later responses and trials without an observed response. Across the descriptive dataset, 25508 trials trials contained a response within the window and 4537 trials trials did not. The outcome space therefore constrained response direction, response time and response probability rather than choice alone.

Scheduled perturbations temporarily replaced baseline motion with strong motion in one of two directions. Assignment preceded the behavioural outcome, but a scheduled pulse could occur after a response on the latest-onset trials. We therefore used scheduled assignment rather than an outcome-derived pulse label and retained assigned trials regardless of whether a response preceded onset. Control-trial psychometric functions related rightward-response probability to signed coherence, response-time summaries separated correct and error trials, and no-response summaries retained every trial at the fixed cutoff (Figure 1C–E). Individual-mouse and equal-mouse summaries showed heterogeneity, but task implementation, site and cohort were confounded, so the displays did not support biological comparisons between rigs. Together, these panels defined the three behavioural constraints used in the joint models.

The outcome summaries motivated modelling choices without serving as independent model validation. Correct and error response-time distributions were retained separately because similar choice proportions can accompany different timing predictions. No response by 5 s entered the response-probability objective rather than being discarded, because its frequency also varied across conditions and mice. Zero-coherence responses were shown separately in correct/error summaries because neither response direction was defined as correct there. The subsequent comparisons therefore evaluated whether a common implementation accounted jointly for response direction, conditional response time and response probability, rather than whether it reproduced a psychometric curve alone.

### Stimulus reconstruction defined the model input and exposed assumptions and replay uncertainty beyond nominal coherence

To specify the time-varying model input, we regenerated each stimulus from its stored seed and task parameters and passed the rendered frames through causal, direction-opponent motion-energy filters (Figure 2A). We extended replays through the analysis window rather than stopping them at the animal's response, then calibrated motion energy separately for the two task implementations into equivalent-coherence units (Figure 2B). Mean reconstructed evidence followed programmed coherence, while trials at the same programmed coherence retained seed-dependent variation, including a persistent component created by the sampled dot directions (Figure 2C). The reconstruction represented trial-specific evidence beyond nominal coherence, but it remained a model input rather than a direct recording of displayed frames. Its causal filtering was also an assumed input transformation, not a measure of sensory or neural processing time.

Extending the replay beyond the observed response allowed a simulated decision to occur later than the animal's recorded response without ending its evidence stream at an outcome-dependent time. Separate calibration expressed the two implementations on a common input scale while leaving fitted behavioural sensitivity to the decision model. Neither operation established that the reconstructed frames exactly matched acquisition. Rather, Figure 2 makes the pipeline's evidence representation explicit so that later prediction failure can be attributed to the complete model-and-input object instead of to decision dynamics alone.

Pulse-present and pulse-omitted replays sharing an initial seed were identical before scheduled onset, and the causal filter introduced no pre-onset motion-energy difference (Figure 2D). At pulse offset, the task redrew background-dot directions, so the streams diverged and acquired trial-specific noise; their sign-aligned mean did not reveal persistent pulse-direction bias apart from a small deterministic rounding shift in specified cells. Labelled UCLA alternatives changed pulse-energy magnitude, while screen geometry, display-loop settings and the acquisition convention remained unresolved (Figure 2E). Thus, replay constrained the tested model-and-input pipeline without establishing frame-perfect fidelity, a universal pulse-energy error bound or neural latency.

The paired replay was a software counterfactual under a specified random-number coupling. Once pulse-present and pulse-omitted branches consumed different draws, their later noise realizations were not held identical. Consequently, the audit tested pre-onset equality, causal filtering and sign-aligned artefacts in the implemented representation, but it did not validate every physical display frame or establish that all post-pulse differences were caused by the programmed pulse component. The labelled alternatives exposed this residual input uncertainty next to the reconstruction result rather than treating it as a later generic limitation.

Supplementary Figure S2 reports a historical control-labelled hidden-coherence analysis in which a pooled common-ratio model estimated a hidden-to-nominal weight ratio of 0.5 dimensionless. This descriptive ratio was conditional on the shared model and historical control labels and did not establish a population-wide discount; no scheduled-control-label sensitivity analysis was available. Because persistent within-trial evidence also prevents a time-specific choice association from isolating time-specific weighting, this analysis remained supplementary.

### Joint held-out prediction on control trials favoured the tested accumulator among the tested implementations

We next asked whether control behaviour distinguished evidence retention from rapid forgetting. Both implementations used the reconstructed input, response mapping and fast-response mixture; the tested accumulator retained evidence to a bound, whereas the rapid-forgetting approximation operationalized an extrema-like alternative (Figure 3A). Selection and testing were separated by scoring held-out control trials with a joint objective for choices, response probabilities and response times. Summed across folds and mice, rapid-forgetting minus accumulator negative log likelihood was 345.7 NLL units NLL units, so the sign favoured the tested accumulator (Figure 3B). The working per-mouse score threshold represented approximate Monte Carlo variability rather than a population significance test, and the joint score did not authorize separate claims for its component objectives. The result supported the tested accumulator only relative to the specified rapid-forgetting implementation.

The score combined the probability of responding, response direction conditional on response and conditional response-time bins. Cross-validation held out control trials from the same mice and sessions rather than new animals. A positive summed difference therefore indicated better within-cohort held-out prediction by the tested accumulator as a complete implementation. It did not imply a group hypothesis test, and the displayed per-mouse working threshold was an operational reference for noisy simulated scores rather than a calibrated universal cutoff. Keeping these distinctions explicit prevented the relative score from being misread as unique identification of an accumulation mechanism.

### At the group level, the tested model-and-input pipelines predicted a different temporal profile from the observed pulse-associated response contrast

To test predictive transfer, we compared observed and simulated pulse-aligned curves on the historical model-input rows intersected with the behavioural common-support set, using identical cells and mouse weights (Figure 5A–B). The overlap excluded the disclosed outcome-dependent legacy rows and retained the historical UCLA no-response pulse labels rather than silently modifying fitted inputs. With control parameters fixed, stored joint pulse gains and gains chosen to improve pulse choices placed more of the contrast early and produced a later partial decline, whereas the observed group contrast emerged mainly from 0.5 to 2 s and remained positive. Intervals described between-mouse variation within observed or predicted quantities, not a paired inferential test of observed-minus-model differences. The overlay also inherited finite-simulation uncertainty. Thus, the tested model-and-input pipelines failed to generalize their temporal profile without localizing the error to sensory encoding, decision dynamics or response generation.

Using the same rows and weights made the observed and predicted curves comparable at the plotted-estimand level. ### The original and gain-revised predictive gates failed, sensitivity was low, and the confirmatory timing test was not run

A subsequent revision, explicitly prompted by failure, fitted one pulse gain per mouse by cross-validation while keeping control parameters fixed. Revised choice departures remained 0.24 logit and 0.31 logit logit, while fractions 0.62 fraction and 0.7 fraction of mouse-by-quantile response-time cells lay inside simulated ranges for the primary and secondary baselines, respectively. Choice departure measured residual calibration toward scheduled pulse direction; response-time coverage measured cells inside model simulations. Both gain-revised baselines therefore failed the combined gate. Cross-validation prevented own-trial gain fitting but could not restore independence from the observed failure that motivated revision.

The original gates asked whether frozen control-selected predictions were sufficiently adequate on pulse-trial choices, response times and no-response probabilities to support a later timing contrast. Their failure stopped that confirmatory path before any early-versus-middle timing coefficient was interpreted. The gain revisions then asked whether one time-invariant scalar per mouse could repair pulse magnitude without changing the control models. Because those adapted pipelines also failed their combined adequacy screens, neither became a replacement confirmatory baseline.

This was not a null result for primacy, recency or flat temporal weighting.
