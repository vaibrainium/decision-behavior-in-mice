## Results

### Mice provided choice, response-time and no-response constraints in a reaction-time motion task

We analysed a reaction-time random-dot-motion task in which mice reported leftward or rightward motion by licking the corresponding response port (Figure 1A). The dataset comprised 10 mice across 50 mouse-sessions, collected with two task implementations that shared the decision structure but used different perturbation schedules (Figure 1B). Each trial provided up to three complementary observations: response direction, response time and whether the mouse responded within the analysis window. Responses could register after a minimum viewing interval of 0.3 s; trials without a registered response by 5 s were classified as no response, an analysis boundary rather than a common task-termination time. Across the descriptive dataset, 25,508 trials contained a response within this window and 4,537 did not.

The three outcomes constrained different aspects of behaviour. Signed-coherence psychometric functions described which response the animals made, while correct and error response-time distributions described when they made it (Figure 1C–D). No-response probabilities retained trials that would otherwise disappear from an analysis conditional on responding (Figure 1E). Zero-coherence trials were displayed separately in correct/error summaries because neither direction was correct by definition. Considering these outcomes together was important because two models could produce similar choice proportions while differing in response timing or in the probability of reaching a response.

On scheduled perturbation trials, strong motion briefly replaced the baseline stimulus in one of two directions. Assignment preceded the behavioural outcome, although a late pulse could be scheduled after the mouse had already responded. We therefore retained trials according to scheduled assignment rather than defining pulse exposure from the eventual response. Control-trial psychometric functions, correct and error response times, and no-response probabilities summarized the three outcomes later fitted jointly (Figure 1C–E). These summaries revealed variation among mice, but task implementation, site and cohort were confounded and could not support biological comparisons between rigs.

This design established two roles for the data. Control trials were used to fit and compare candidate decision models, whereas randomized pulse assignments tested whether a model selected under ordinary stimulus conditions could predict responses to a brief, strong perturbation. The descriptive panels defined those behavioural targets but were not themselves independent validation of a fitted model.

### Stimulus reconstruction provided trial-specific estimates of visual evidence

Nominal coherence did not capture all of the evidence presented on an individual trial. We therefore regenerated each stimulus from its stored seed and task parameters and passed the rendered frames through causal, direction-opponent motion-energy filters (Figure 2A). Replays continued through the analysis window rather than ending at the animal's response, and motion energy was calibrated separately for the two task implementations into equivalent-coherence units (Figure 2B). Mean reconstructed evidence followed programmed coherence, while different seeds at the same programmed coherence produced persistent trial-to-trial variation (Figure 2C). This reconstruction supplied the time-varying input to the decision models, but it was not a direct recording of the displayed frames, and filter delay was not interpreted as sensory or neural latency.

Continuing the replay beyond the observed response prevented the simulated evidence stream from ending at a behaviour-dependent time. It allowed a simulated decision that occurred later than the recorded response to receive a stimulus of the same nominal duration as other simulations. Separate calibration placed the two task implementations on a common equivalent-coherence scale while leaving behavioural sensitivity to be estimated by the decision model. Together, these operations allowed a common model to respond to trial-specific evidence without assuming that the two rigs generated identical raw motion-energy scales.

Paired pulse-present and pulse-omitted replays were identical before scheduled onset, with no pre-onset motion-energy difference introduced by the causal filter (Figure 2D). At pulse offset, however, the task redrew background-dot directions; the two streams then consumed different random draws and acquired trial-specific differences. Their sign-aligned mean showed no persistent pulse-direction bias apart from a small deterministic rounding shift in specified cells. Labelled UCLA replay alternatives changed pulse-energy magnitude, while screen geometry, display-loop settings and the acquisition convention remained unresolved (Figure 2E). The reconstruction thus made the tested input explicit without establishing frame-perfect replay fidelity or a universal pulse-energy error bound.

This distinction mattered for every subsequent model prediction. Each prediction arose jointly from the reconstructed input, its temporal filtering and the decision-and-response model. Agreement would support that complete pipeline, whereas disagreement could not by itself identify which stage was responsible. Figure 2 therefore places the replay uncertainties next to the input representation rather than reserving them as an after-the-fact explanation for model failure.

Supplementary Figure S2 reports a historical control-labelled analysis in which a pooled common-ratio model estimated a hidden-to-nominal coherence weight ratio of 0.50. This descriptive estimate was conditional on a shared model and historical labels; no scheduled-control-label sensitivity analysis was available. Because persistent within-trial evidence also prevents a time-specific choice association from isolating time-specific weighting, we did not interpret this ratio as a population-wide discount or a temporal weighting kernel.

### Held-out control trials favoured the tested accumulator over rapid forgetting

We first asked whether control behaviour distinguished evidence retention from rapid forgetting. Both candidate implementations received the reconstructed input and shared the response mapping and fast-response mixture. The accumulator retained evidence to a bound, whereas the rapid-forgetting approximation implemented an extrema-like alternative (Figure 3A). On held-out control trials, we scored their joint predictions for response probability, response direction and response time. Summed across folds and mice, rapid-forgetting minus accumulator negative log likelihood was 345.7 NLL units, favouring the tested accumulator (Figure 3B). This was a within-cohort predictive comparison, not a population significance test: the displayed per-mouse threshold approximated Monte Carlo score variability, and the result applied only to the two complete implementations tested here.

The joint score was essential to the comparison. It combined the probability of responding, response direction conditional on a response, and conditional response-time bins. Cross-validation held out control trials from the same mice and sessions, separating parameter fitting from scoring without testing generalization to new animals. A positive score difference therefore meant that the accumulator made better joint predictions for unseen trials within this cohort; it did not imply that every mouse or every component of the objective independently distinguished the candidates.

Final fits reproduced the broad choice and correct-response-time trends but retained errors in some error-response-time and no-response predictions (Figure 3C–D). These displays described fit adequacy on contributing control trials rather than providing an additional model-selection test. Recovery comprised 500 fits spanning five generators and five candidate models, with 10 mice × 2 replicates, independent training and test data, and a decision threshold greater than 10 NLL (20 datasets per mechanism state). The fast-response component had a hit rate of 1.00 when present, whereas drift variability had a false-positive rate of 0.35 when absent. Across pooled generating and fitted datasets, the fast-response delay correlation was r = 0.18; this was not a within-mouse precision estimate. These recovery limits and near-bound parameters discouraged component-level interpretation, but did not change the relative held-out preference between the complete candidates.

The recovery results separated two questions that are easily conflated. Component hit and false-positive rates asked whether the fitting procedure selected a simulated feature under the registered rule, whereas the generating-to-recovered correlation asked how closely a fitted parameter tracked its simulated value. Both pooled simulated replicates across mice and therefore did not provide additional biological subjects or mouse-specific precision. In particular, poor recovery of the fast-response delay and false selection of drift variability limited claims about those parameters even though the complete accumulator predicted held-out control behaviour better.

Control trials therefore supported the tested accumulator over the specified rapid-forgetting approximation without uniquely identifying accumulation or excluding other non-integrating policies. We next used the scheduled pulses as a more demanding, out-of-condition test of behaviour under a brief, strong change in evidence.

### Randomized motion pulses reliably redirected choices

Pulse direction changed the animals' responses (Figure 4A–B). Across mice, positive rather than negative assignment shifted response direction by 5.84 percentage points (95% CI, 1.80–9.89; two-sided one-sample t test, t(9) = 3.27, raw p = 0.0097; Holm-adjusted p = 0.019 across three comparisons). Separate changes in right responses, left responses and no responses summed to zero within mouse, indicating redirection rather than a common increase in responding.

The directional statistic compared the positive-minus-negative change in right responses with the corresponding change in left responses, using a symmetric scaling and retaining no-response outcomes. Its sign therefore followed the assigned motion direction rather than overall response propensity. The confidence interval and multiplicity-controlled probability value treated the mouse as the unit of group inference; trials and matched cells supplied within-mouse information rather than independent group replicates.

The primary comparison used 10281 trials in 717 cells defined by mouse × session × signed coherence × onset and containing both scheduled signs. Cells were weighted by their smaller arm count, weights were normalized within mouse, and mice contributed equally. The analysis retained 118 trials that preceded pulse onset and 1709 trials without a response by 5 s, preserving the randomized assignment estimand rather than conditioning it on when or whether the mouse responded. Timing-matched controls used recorded sham onsets for UW and outcome-independent pseudo-onsets for UCLA (Figure 4C). Because the two pulse-versus-control contrasts occupied different common-support cells, they described outcome components and alignment sensitivity but did not decompose the primary signed comparison or test pulse-sign asymmetry.

All three comparisons formed the registered Holm family. The signed positive-versus-negative assignment comparison was primary, while each pulse-versus-control comparison located one assigned direction relative to aligned trials without a scheduled signed pulse. UCLA pseudo-onsets were generated without reference to behavioural outcomes, so changing the pseudo-onset seed could alter control alignment but could not redefine the randomized signed contrast. An inconclusive control comparison was therefore not interpreted as evidence that its corresponding effect was absent.

Available task-code snapshots and a pre-outcome balance audit supported the assignment procedure, although code versions were not pinned to each collection session. The assignment-based behavioural analysis was unaffected by known limitations in the legacy model inputs. Those inputs excluded 15 trials whose responses occurred 0.25–0.28 s after onset (7 positive, 7 negative and 1 null), and historically labelled 48 UCLA no-response pulse trials as controls. Directions were recovered for 43 of these trials: 38 had genuine non-null directions and 5 were null; directions remained unknowable for 5 zero-coherence trials. These limitations instead qualified the later comparison with model predictions.

The response-associated pulse effect developed over time (Figure 4D). Across all scheduled trials, the cumulative directional contrast reached 5.80 percentage points at the shared 4.58-s endpoint. Curves were evaluated in 0.01-s steps across 10 mice, with pointwise 95% Student-t intervals. The contrast was small in the first 0.5 s and increased mainly from 0.5 to 2 s after logged onset, when many responses occurred. These windows were descriptive, not a multiplicity-corrected early-versus-late test. Because cumulative response curves carry earlier differences forward and include no-response trials, they locate response-associated divergence rather than the time of evidence entry, an integration kernel, neural latency or a processing stage.

Right and left responses entered these curves as competing outcomes, and trials without either response remained in the no-response probability. The display therefore avoided selecting only eventual responders or conditioning on survival beyond pulse onset. This inclusive construction preserved assignment, but it also meant that a late cumulative difference could carry forward an earlier effect and that a small early difference could simply reflect few emitted responses. The temporal pattern nevertheless posed a clear predictive target: the model had to reproduce not only the final directional shift but also how that shift appeared across response times.

### Control-fitted models predicted the wrong temporal profile of the pulse response

We next compared the observed pulse-aligned curves with simulations from the control-fitted models (Figure 5A–B). Observations and predictions used identical common-support cells and mouse weights on the historical model-input rows that overlapped the behavioural analysis. Across stored joint pulse gains and gains chosen to improve pulse choices, the models concentrated the directional contrast early and predicted a later partial decline. The mice instead showed little initial contrast, followed by a positive difference that emerged mainly from 0.5 to 2 s and persisted. Thus, the tested model-and-input pipelines failed to generalize the temporal profile of the pulse response.

The discrepancy centred on temporal shape rather than overall direction. Both mice and simulations moved in the assigned direction over part of the response window, but the simulated effect arrived too early and later weakened or partially reversed. Increasing pulse gain strengthened the predicted response without moving it into the delayed, sustained profile observed in the animals. The pulse trials therefore revealed a limitation that was not apparent from the control-trial fits alone.

The comparison was deliberately descriptive. Its intervals summarized between-mouse variation within the observed or predicted curves rather than a paired test of their difference, and simulations added Monte Carlo uncertainty. The common-row restriction also preserved the historical UCLA no-response labels and did not integrate parameter uncertainty or repair legacy exclusions. Pulse gain could rescale the reconstructed pulse while leaving control parameters fixed, but changing gain did not remove the temporal-shape mismatch. This result constrained an amplitude-only repair of the tested pipeline without locating the failure in sensory encoding, decision dynamics or response generation.

Using the same rows and weights made the plotted estimands comparable, but it did not convert their visual separation into a formal paired group test. This keeps the central conclusion predictive: the frozen control solution generated the wrong qualitative time course under strong transient evidence. It does not establish that the accumulator itself failed, because a different stimulus representation, pulse transformation or response mapping could alter the same prediction.

The gain profiles revealed a related tension between choice and timing predictions in the two mice that carried enough information for this diagnostic (Figure 5C–D). In PSUIM6 and PSUIM7, gains selected to improve held-out choices reduced choice NLL by 10.40 and 10.01 units relative to the joint-gain solution, but increased the remaining response and response-time objective by 96.01 and 106.61 units. The registered search covered gains from 0 to 50 with a 15-point grid, 10 refinement steps, five folds and 50 common-random-number fit repetitions; each dense-mouse diagnostic used one main Monte Carlo seed. These QMLE score changes retained the original objective weights, were not calibrated significance statistics and had no direct repeated-seed precision estimate for either mouse. Choice profiles were flatter in most other mice, so this was a two-mouse explanation of the group-level mismatch rather than evidence for a cohort-wide conflict or a unique mechanism.

### Failed predictive checks precluded the planned timing test

The pulse mismatch also appeared in the prespecified adequacy gates (Supplementary Figure S6). The primary baseline was the tested accumulator pipeline; the secondary `L1U1V1Z0G1` baseline added leak and urgency within the same input framework. In the original gates, observed-minus-predicted choice departure toward the pulse was 0.296 logit for the primary baseline and 0.317 logit for the secondary baseline, while response-time coverage fell below the requirement for both. The gate required the 95% choice interval to include zero or the absolute departure to fall below 25% of the predicted pulse effect, together with at least 80% coverage of both response-time-quantile and censoring cells. These criteria screened adequacy rather than testing equivalence. A revision prompted by this failure fitted one cross-validated pulse gain per mouse while fixing the control parameters. Choice departures remained 0.24 and 0.31 logit, while only 0.62 and 0.70 of mouse-by-quantile response-time cells fell inside simulated ranges for the primary and secondary baselines. Both revised pipelines therefore failed the combined gate.

The sequence of checks was designed to prevent a timing contrast from being interpreted on top of an inadequate predictive model. The original gates asked whether the frozen control-selected pipelines reproduced pulse-trial choices, response times and no-response probabilities well enough to serve as timing baselines. After they failed, the cross-validated gain revision tested whether one time-invariant scale factor per mouse could repair pulse magnitude while leaving all control parameters fixed. Cross-validation prevented a trial from being predicted using a gain fitted to that same trial, but the revision remained exploratory because the observed failure had prompted it. Its failure showed that amplitude rescaling alone did not create an adequate replacement baseline.

Simulations showed that the intended pulse tests were also insensitive under their registered generators. The frozen strategy procedure returned the correct verdict in 1/20 extrema-generated datasets and 0/20 accumulator-generated datasets; poor recovery did not imply equivalence between accumulation and rapid forgetting. The gain-revised timing calibration used 20 full-cohort datasets at each of four decay levels (0%, 10%, 20% and 40% over 0.5 s), with fitted pulse gains and fixed control parameters. It detected 0/20 datasets at 10% decay, 1/20 at 20% decay and 0/20 at 40% decay, with 0/20 false detections under the null. The two simulations addressed different questions and neither propagated uncertainty in the fitted control parameters. Their empirical rates therefore characterized these specific procedures and generators, not general power against every form of rapid forgetting or temporal weighting.

The two sensitivity analyses also answered different questions. Strategy recovery tested whether the frozen pulse procedure could distinguish data generated by the two fitted candidate strategies. Timing calibration instead passed synthetic data with imposed decay through the amended gain-fitting, gating and timing pipeline. Keeping their denominators and generators separate prevented a low rate in one exercise from being treated as evidence about the other.

Because the original and revised gates failed and the calibrated procedure had inadequate sensitivity, the confirmatory timing test was not run and the prespecified stopping rule ended further baseline revision. This outcome was not evidence for primacy, recency or flat temporal weighting. Instead, the randomized pulses established a robust directional behavioural effect while exposing a specific failure of the tested control-fitted models to predict its temporal expression.
