## Discussion

Control behavior and pulse responses placed different demands on the decision models [[C008]]. Joint held-out prediction on control trials favored the tested accumulator over the rapid-forgetting approximation, whereas randomized motion pulses reliably redirected the animals' responses [[C008]]. Yet the control-fitted pipelines predicted that the pulse effect would appear too early and then weaken or partly reverse, rather than reproduce the delayed, sustained response-associated contrast observed in the mice [[C008]]. The failed predictive gates and low sensitivity of the planned timing procedure prevented us from using that mismatch to estimate a temporal weighting profile [[C008]]. These results therefore support a relative control-model preference and a robust behavioral response to pulse direction, but they do not uniquely identify the sensory, decision or response process that generated the pulse-associated time course [[C008]]. The tension between successful model selection under ordinary evidence and failed transfer to strong transient evidence is the main result, not a contradiction to be resolved by choosing one of those observations over the other [[C008]]. The pulse trials therefore add information even though they did not yield the intended timing estimate: they expose a boundary on the model's predictive domain that control-trial fit quality alone could not reveal [[C008]].

This distinction is consistent with a broader model-identification literature. Choice accuracy and apparently stable temporal weighting can be reproduced by non-integrating strategies, while joint choice and response-time distributions can separate particular implementations more effectively [@stine2020differentiating]. Even then, the preferred mechanism can change across datasets: accumulation and urgency models are distinguishable in suitable designs, but each has prevailed in different behavioral regimes [@hawkins2015discriminating]. Time-varying perturbations add leverage only when candidate predictions remain separated after realistic sensory noise, delays and response rules are included [@zhou2009time; @tsetsos2012timevarying]. Pulse efficacy can also change with decision policy, including manipulations that favor stronger leak or urgency [@carland2016evidence]. Conversely, weak randomized pulses in macaques have produced persistent behavioral and LIP effects alongside drift-diffusion-like population dynamics [@stine2023termination; @steinemann2024direct] [[C013]]. Those studies provide positive evidence for accumulation in a different species, perturbation regime and neural preparation; they did not report the same control-only fit followed by a frozen strong-pulse transfer test [[C013]]. Thus, perturbations do not have a fixed diagnostic meaning: their ability to discriminate models depends on prediction separability under the noise, timing and response regime actually tested [[C013]].

Several mechanisms could produce the mismatch without overturning the randomized behavioral effect. Sensory dynamics are a concrete possibility: time-dependent responses in sensory cortex can be inherited by a downstream integrator, and behavioral temporal kernels generally combine sensory, decision and response processes [@yates2017functional; @okazawa2018psychophysical] [[C014]]. Here, the replay was reconstructed rather than measured from displayed frames, and the unresolved display convention, screen geometry, pulse-offset redraw and chosen motion-energy filters could change the input delivered to the model. Photodiode-synchronized frame logging and a prespecified comparison of plausible spatial and temporal filters would directly test that explanation. Decision or response dynamics also remain plausible because the control fits missed aspects of high-coherence error timing and late responses [[C008]]; a richer candidate should first improve held-out control prediction and parameter recovery before being credited with explaining pulses. The model overlay further inherited legacy pulse labels and exclusions, which should be resolved by versioned corrected inputs followed by a complete refit rather than a post hoc relabeling of predictions. Sparse response-time contributions in some mice, limited simulation seeds in the dense-mouse gain diagnostic, poorly recovered or near-bound parameters and the confounding of site, cohort and task implementation all limit finer localization. More simulation replicates, expanded bounds with renewed recovery, and within-rig replication would narrow those uncertainties. Each remedy addresses a different link in the prediction chain: display measurements test the input, recovery tests estimability, and new held-out conditions test transfer. None of these limitations removes the assignment-based directional pulse effect; they instead identify which parts of the failed predictive pipeline require a cleaner test.

Accordingly, these data do not establish accumulation as the unique decision strategy, nor do they show that the pulse mismatch arose from the tested accumulator itself. They do not support a conclusion about primacy, recency, a memory or integration time constant, or the stage at which the transient was transformed. The response-aligned curves describe when behavioral outcomes diverged, not when evidence entered or persisted in a neural integrator. The two-mouse choice-versus-timing gain tradeoff cannot be generalized to the cohort, and the confounded rigs cannot support biological site comparisons. Withholding the confirmatory timing test after failed adequacy and sensitivity checks was therefore substantive: it prevented a precise mechanistic label from being attached to a pipeline that had not earned that interpretation.

The most informative next experiment is not simply a larger version of the present dataset. It should combine photodiode-verified pulse delivery and synchronized response timing with several randomized pulse amplitudes and onset times, so that input magnitude and temporal shape can be estimated rather than absorbed into one gain. An independent retention manipulation—for example, separated motion samples with a controlled evidence-free gap—would test whether information survives when no new motion is available, providing leverage that response latency after a single pulse cannot supply. Candidate sensory transformations, decision dynamics and response mappings should be specified in advance, fitted on control trials and tested on the perturbations with joint choice, response-time and no-response predictions. Before data collection, simulations should establish which candidate pairs are separable at the intended trial counts and define what happens if the predictive gate fails. A reserved validation set should then remain untouched until the candidates and their input transformations are frozen. Neural measurements could localize where a sustained or transformed signal appears, but they are not required to validate the behavioral perturbation effect. This design would ask whether the mismatch reflects the visual input representation, the accumulation dynamics or their mapping to action.

Model selection and perturbation transfer answer different questions. Control trials showed that the tested accumulator predicted ordinary behavior better than the specified rapid-forgetting approximation; pulse trials showed that this advantage did not guarantee prediction of behavior under a strong transient [[C008]]. Treating both outcomes as constraints turns the failed transfer into useful evidence: it defines where the current explanation stops working and what a successor model must predict. Brief motion pulses therefore contribute most strongly here not as a direct assay of primacy or memory duration, but as an out-of-condition challenge that exposed a limitation hidden by otherwise successful control fits [[C008]].
