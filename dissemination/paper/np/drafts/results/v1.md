## Results

### The experiment provided choice, response-time and no-response constraints during motion discrimination with scheduled directional perturbations [[C002]]

To obtain complementary constraints on perceptual decisions, we analysed a reaction-time random-dot-motion task in which mice reported leftward or rightward motion by licking the corresponding response port (Figure 1A). The dataset comprised {{N001}} mice across {{N004}} mouse-sessions, collected with two task implementations that shared the decision structure but used different scheduled perturbation timings (Figure 1B) [[C002]]. Responses could register after a minimum viewing interval of {{N009}} s, and the analysis classified trials without a registered response by {{N010}} s as no response by 5 s [[C002]]. This fixed cutoff was an analysis boundary rather than a shared task-termination time: the underlying records included both later responses and trials without an observed response [[C002]]. Across the descriptive dataset, {{N005}} trials contained a response within the analysis window and {{N006}} trials did not [[C002]]. The resulting outcome space therefore constrained response direction, response time and response probability rather than choice alone [[C002]].

Scheduled perturbations temporarily replaced the baseline motion with strong motion in one of two directions, with onset schedules set by the task implementation (Figure 1B) [[C002]]. Assignment preceded the behavioural outcome, but a scheduled pulse could occur after a response on the latest-onset trials [[C002]]. We therefore distinguished scheduled assignment from a pulse encountered before response throughout the analysis [[C002]]. This distinction was especially important for no response by 5 s, because outcome-derived labels would otherwise remove some assigned trials or reclassify them after their result was known [[C002]]. The behavioural summaries instead used scheduled classifications and showed the unconditioned outcome constraints that the models had to predict [[C002]].

Control-trial psychometric functions related rightward-response probability to signed coherence, while response-time summaries separated correct and error trials and retained zero-coherence responses without assigning them a correct direction (Figure 1C–D) [[C002]]. Response probability also varied with coherence, and the no-response summary retained every trial at the fixed cutoff instead of converting later or absent responses into an additional choice category (Figure 1E) [[C002]]. Individual-mouse values and equal-mouse group summaries showed heterogeneity in each outcome [[C002]]. Because task implementation, site and cohort were confounded, these displays were descriptive and did not support biological comparisons between rigs [[C002]]. Together, the task supplied the three behavioural constraints used for the subsequent joint model comparisons [[C002]].

The descriptive panels also motivated two modelling choices without independently validating them [[C002]]. First, correct and error response-time distributions were analysed separately because equal choice proportions could conceal different temporal predictions [[C002]]. Second, no response by 5 s entered the response-probability objective rather than being discarded, because censoring differed across conditions and mice [[C002]]. The later model comparisons therefore asked whether a common implementation accounted jointly for all displayed outcomes, not whether it reproduced a psychometric curve in isolation [[C002]].

### Stimulus reconstruction defined the model input and exposed assumptions and replay uncertainty beyond nominal coherence [[C003]]

To specify the time-varying input to the decision models, we regenerated each stimulus from its stored seed and task parameters and passed the rendered frames through causal, direction-opponent motion-energy filters (Figure 2A) [[C003]]. We extended each replay through the analysis window rather than stopping it at the animal's response, then calibrated motion energy separately for the two task implementations into equivalent-coherence units (Figure 2B) [[C003]]. Mean reconstructed evidence followed programmed coherence, while trials with the same programmed coherence retained seed-dependent variation, including a persistent component created by the sampled dot directions (Figure 2C) [[C003]]. The reconstruction thus represented trial-specific input beyond nominal coherence, but it remained a model input rather than a direct recording of the displayed frames [[C003]].

This distinction separated numerical reproducibility from physical validation [[C003]]. Replaying a stored seed through a specified task implementation reproduced the evidence presented to the fitted models, but unrecorded display-loop and geometry settings could still separate that reconstruction from acquisition [[C003]]. Likewise, the causal filter transformed a brief programmed coherence change into a temporally extended input response [[C003]]. That extension was a property of the assumed evidence representation, so its peak or width could not be interpreted as sensory or neural processing time [[C003]]. Figure 2 therefore documents what the pipelines received and which input assumptions remained open [[C003]].

Pulse-present and pulse-omitted replays sharing an initial seed were identical before scheduled onset, and the causal filter introduced no pre-onset motion-energy difference (Figure 2D) [[C003]]. At pulse offset, the task redrew background-dot directions, so the paired streams diverged and acquired trial-specific noise; their sign-aligned mean did not reveal a persistent pulse-direction bias, apart from a small deterministic rounding shift in specified cells [[C003]]. Labelled UCLA replay alternatives changed pulse-energy magnitude, and screen geometry, display-loop settings and the exact acquisition convention remained unresolved (Figure 2E) [[C003]]. The reconstruction therefore constrained the tested model-and-input pipeline without establishing frame-perfect display fidelity, a universal pulse-energy error bound or neural latency [[C003]]. Supplementary Figure S2 reports the historical control-labelled hidden-coherence analysis: a shared model estimated a hidden-to-nominal weight ratio of {{N011}}, a one-sided mouse-level positive-weight test gave {{N013}}, and a paired mouse-level comparison gave {{N012}} [[C003]]. The shared estimate was conditional on its pooled model and did not establish a population-wide discount [[C003]].

Persistent seed-dependent evidence also limited what could be learned from noise-based temporal weighting [[C003]]. When evidence fluctuations remain correlated within a trial, a choice association at one time does not isolate the weight assigned at that moment [[C003]]. For this reason, the hidden-coherence result remained supplementary and the main temporal test used externally scheduled perturbations with explicit forward predictions [[C003]]. That design choice did not remove reconstruction uncertainty, but it made the time-varying input specified rather than inferred from correlated background noise [[C003]].

### Joint held-out prediction on control trials favoured the tested accumulator among the tested implementations [[C004]]

We next asked whether control behaviour distinguished evidence retention from rapid forgetting [[C004]]. Both candidate implementations used the reconstructed input, the same response mapping and a fast-response mixture; the tested accumulator retained evidence to a bound, whereas the rapid-forgetting approximation operationalized an extrema-like alternative (Figure 3A) [[C004]]. Selection and testing were separated by scoring held-out control trials with a joint objective for choices, response probabilities and response times [[C004]]. Summed across folds and mice, the negative-log-likelihood difference for the rapid-forgetting approximation minus the tested accumulator was {{N016}} NLL units, so the sign favoured the tested accumulator (Figure 3B) [[C004]]. The working per-mouse score threshold represented approximate Monte Carlo scoring variability, not a population significance test, and the joint score did not authorize independent claims for its component objectives [[C004]]. Thus, the comparison supported the tested accumulator only relative to the specified rapid-forgetting implementation [[C004]].

The comparison addressed a deliberately restricted model-identification question [[C004]]. A positive joint score difference meant that one complete implementation better predicted held-out control outcomes than the other under the fitted input, likelihood and response mapping [[C004]]. It did not show that every mouse separately distinguished the candidates, that every score component favoured the same model or that all non-integrating policies were excluded [[C004]]. Nor did good control prediction guarantee transfer to inputs outside the fitted regime [[C004]]. The randomized pulses consequently served as an out-of-condition prediction test rather than a second model-selection dataset [[C004]].

Component comparisons and final fit-quality displays provided context for that held-out result (Figure 3C–D) [[C004]]. The final common model reproduced broad control-trial choice and correct-response-time trends but retained systematic errors in some error-response-time and no-response predictions [[C004]]. Because those displays evaluated final fits on contributing control trials, they described adequacy and were not an independent model-selection test [[C004]]. The associated recovery exercise comprised {{N018}} fit files across candidate and generating models [[C004]]. The fast-response component was recovered with hit rate {{N019}}, whereas across-trial drift variability had false-positive rate {{N020}} under its absent-generator condition; the fitted fast-response delay was poorly recovered, with generating-to-recovered correlation {{N021}} across the pooled recovery set [[C004]]. These recovery results limited mechanistic interpretation and did not establish within-mouse parameter precision [[C004]]. Near-bound parameters, approximate Monte Carlo scores and remaining fit errors therefore accompanied, but did not overturn, the relative held-out control-model preference [[C004]].

Recovery further separated component detection from parameter interpretation [[C004]]. A component could improve held-out prediction while its fitted magnitude or timing remained weakly identified, and a pooled correlation across simulated mice and replicates did not measure precision for an individual animal [[C004]]. Conversely, false selection of a component in simulations warned against reading the selected common model as a literal decomposition of sensory, decision and response processes [[C004]]. Figure 3 therefore establishes the relative predictive result, while Supplementary Figure S3 carries the parameter and recovery boundaries needed to interpret it [[C004]].

### Randomized signed pulse assignment shifted response direction, and the cumulative response-associated contrast emerged mainly 0.5–2 s after logged onset [[C005]]

Control-trial selection did not establish whether the fitted pipelines generalized to a transient, strong input [[C005]]. We therefore used the randomized scheduled pulse assignment as a separate behavioural perturbation (Figure 4A) [[C005]]. Positive and negative assignments were compared within mouse × session × signed-coherence × onset cells that contained both signs; each cell was weighted by its smaller arm count, weights were normalized within mouse and mice contributed equally [[C005]]. The directional contrast was the positive-minus-negative change in right responses minus the corresponding change in left responses, divided symmetrically, with no response by 5 s retained as an outcome [[C005]]. This common-support analysis included {{N055}} trials in {{N056}} supported cells, including {{N057}} responses emitted before scheduled onset and {{N058}} trials with no response by 5 s [[C005]]. Retaining those trials preserved the pre-assignment estimand and prevented response-conditioned pulse labelling [[C005]].

The support restriction addressed asymmetric scheduling in some task cells [[C005]]. Cells containing only one pulse direction could inform neither the signed contrast nor a balanced pulse-versus-control comparison and were excluded [[C005]]. Within retained cells, weighting by the smaller arm count prevented the more frequent assignment from dominating, and equal weighting across mice kept the animal as the group unit of inference [[C005]]. This estimand consequently described the supported schedule population rather than all pulse trials [[C005]]. It also remained valid when a response preceded onset, because assignment was fixed beforehand and those responses appropriately contributed no post-onset pulse effect [[C005]].

Across {{N001}} mice, assignment to a positive rather than negative pulse shifted response direction by {{N051}} percentage points, with a {{N052}} to {{N053}} two-sided Student-t confidence interval across mice and Holm-adjusted p value {{N054}} for the declared family (Figure 4A–B) [[C005]]. Separate right-response, left-response and no-response components summed to zero within mouse and showed that the primary contrast mainly reflected redirection rather than a common increase in responding [[C005]]. Comparisons with timing-matched control trials used recorded sham onsets for UW and outcome-independent pseudo-onsets for UCLA (Figure 4C) [[C005]]. Because the positive-versus-control and control-versus-negative comparisons occupied different matched cells, their magnitudes did not test pulse-sign asymmetry, and an inconclusive secondary contrast did not establish absence of an effect [[C005]]. The randomized assignment supported a directional behavioural effect within the analysed common-support population, independently of whether either decision pipeline explained it [[C005]].

The control comparisons addressed a different question from the signed primary contrast [[C005]]. They placed each pulse direction relative to an aligned control condition, but UCLA required constructed pseudo-onsets because control trials lacked recorded sham events [[C005]]. The pseudo-onsets were sampled without outcome information from the corresponding session schedule, and alternative seeds changed the alignment without redefining the primary effect [[C005]]. These controls helped describe the outcome components while leaving the positive-versus-negative randomized contrast as the principal behavioural result [[C005]].

We then described when the response-associated contrast appeared after logged onset by computing cumulative right-response, left-response and no-response probabilities on the same assignment basis (Figure 4D) [[C005]]. Across all scheduled trials, the directional contrast reached {{N059}} percentage points at the shared pulse-aligned endpoint [[C005]]. The curve remained small in the earliest window and increased mainly from 0.5 to 2 s after onset, when many responses were emitted [[C005]]. Its bands were pointwise, not simultaneous, Student-t intervals across mice, and the inspected windows were descriptive rather than a corrected early-versus-late test [[C005]]. Moreover, cumulative curves carry an earlier difference forward: they locate emitted responses associated with assignment, not entry or persistence of evidence, an integration kernel, neural latency or a processing stage [[C005]].

The pulse-aligned display preserved the joint outcome distribution rather than conditioning on trials that eventually responded [[C005]]. A right response and a left response were competing events, while trials without either event remained in the no-response probability [[C005]]. This made a late cumulative difference compatible with an earlier divergence that was merely carried forward [[C005]]. Accordingly, the emergence between 0.5 and 2 s was a description of response-associated mass, not evidence that the perturbation itself persisted for that duration [[C005]].

### At the group level, the tested model-and-input pipelines predicted a different temporal profile from the observed pulse-associated response contrast [[C006]]

To test predictive transfer, we compared observed and simulated pulse-aligned curves on the intersection of legacy model-input rows and the behavioural common-support set, using identical cells and mouse weights (Figure 5A–B) [[C006]]. With control parameters fixed, both the stored joint pulse gains and gains chosen to improve pulse choices placed more of the directional contrast early and produced a later partial decline, whereas the observed group contrast emerged mainly from 0.5 to 2 s and remained positive [[C006]]. The same qualitative profile mismatch appeared for the secondary pipeline with leak and urgency [[C006]]. These group comparisons were descriptive: intervals quantified between-mouse variation within observed or predicted quantities, not a paired inferential test of observed-minus-model differences [[C006]]. They also inherited the legacy input's historical pulse-label omissions and finite-simulation uncertainty [[C006]]. The result therefore identified failed temporal-profile generalization by the tested model-and-input pipelines, without isolating the error to sensory encoding, decision dynamics or response generation [[C006]].

The use of common rows and weights made the temporal shapes directly comparable at the level of the plotted estimand [[C006]]. It did not, however, repair the historical input labels or convert the overlay into a preregistered paired test [[C006]]. The pulse gain altered the amplitude of the reconstructed pulse component while control parameters stayed fixed, so failure across gains showed that simple amplitude rescaling within these pipelines did not reproduce the observed joint profile [[C006]]. It did not rule out a different input representation, model form or response process [[C006]].

Gain profiles clarified why amplitude rescaling did not resolve that mismatch (Figure 5C–D) [[C009]]. In PSUIM6 and PSUIM7, the two mice with dense response-time coverage, selecting pulse gain to improve held-out choices reduced choice NLL by {{N063}} and {{N064}} units, respectively, relative to the joint-gain solution, but increased the remaining response and response-time objective by {{N065}} and {{N066}} units [[C009]]. These diagnostic score changes used the original objective weights and were not calibrated significance statistics [[C009]]. Choice profiles were comparatively flat and gain estimates weakly identified in most other mice, and the dense-mouse simulations used limited Monte Carlo seeds [[C009]]. Consequently, PSUIM6 and PSUIM7 demonstrated a choice-versus-remaining-objective tradeoff, but they did not establish a cohort-wide gain conflict or a unique missing mechanism [[C009]].

The diagnostic decomposition linked the two observations without promoting C009 to a second Figure 5 headline [[C009]]. A gain large enough to improve final pulse-direction choices also strengthened early predicted responses, which the remaining response and response-time objective penalized in the two informative mice [[C009]]. In mice with flatter choice profiles or sparse response-time terms, the same comparison was not identified well enough to support that conclusion [[C009]]. The subordinate diagnostic therefore explained one concrete way in which the group profile mismatch constrained amplitude-only revision, while C006 remained the figure-level result [[C009]].

### The original and gain-revised predictive gates failed, sensitivity was low, and the confirmatory timing test was not run [[C007]]

Supplementary Figure S6 preserves the prespecified failures that bounded interpretation of the pulse analyses. In the original predictive gate, observed-minus-predicted pooled choice departures toward the pulse were {{N026}} logit for the primary pipeline and {{N027}} logit for the secondary pipeline, and reaction-time coverage also fell below the prespecified requirement [[C007]]. A subsequent, explicitly failure-prompted revision fitted one pulse gain per mouse by cross-validation while leaving control parameters fixed [[C007]]. The revised choice departures remained {{N030}} and {{N034}} logit, while only fractions {{N035}} and {{N036}} of mouse-by-quantile response-time cells lay inside the corresponding simulated ranges [[C007]]. Here, choice departure measured residual calibration toward scheduled pulse direction, and response-time coverage measured the fraction of mouse-by-quantile cells inside model simulations; both revised pipelines failed the combined predictive gate [[C007]].

The revision was evaluated as a response to model failure, not as part of the original confirmatory design [[C007]]. Cross-validation prevented a trial from being predicted with a pulse gain estimated from that same trial, but it could not restore independence from the observed inadequacy that motivated the revision [[C007]]. Reporting the original and revised gates together therefore preserved the analysis sequence and prevented the adapted model from replacing the failed baseline silently [[C007]].

Separate simulations showed why an inconclusive pulse comparison could not rescue the mechanistic claim. Under the frozen pulse-specific rule, only {{N024}} extrema-generated and {{N025}} accumulator-generated full-cohort datasets produced the correct strategy verdict [[C007]]. The observed pulse comparison was classified as not distinguished, but this low procedural sensitivity was not evidence that the strategies were equivalent [[C007]]. In a distinct calibration of the gain-revised timing procedure, detections occurred in {{N039}}, {{N040}} and {{N041}} simulated datasets at the tested increasing decay levels [[C007]]. The confirmatory timing test was therefore not run after the gates failed, and the prespecified stopping rule ended further baseline revision [[C007]]. These outcomes did not provide a null result for primacy, recency or flat temporal weighting; they showed that the planned procedure had inadequate sensitivity to resolve timing under the tested conditions [[C007]]. The complete record supports a relative control-model preference and a randomized directional pulse effect, while leaving the processing source of the pipeline mismatch unresolved [[C007]].

The two simulation exercises addressed different failure modes and were not pooled [[C007]]. Strategy sensitivity asked whether frozen pulse predictions could recover which tested candidate generated a full cohort, whereas timing calibration asked whether the revised gate-and-test procedure detected imposed temporal decay [[C007]]. Poor performance in both cases limited the corresponding inference but did not measure the biological absence of either strategy differences or temporal weighting [[C007]]. This stopping record is therefore part of the main Results logic: the behavioural perturbation exposed failed transfer, but the planned model-adjusted procedure could not localize why [[C007]].
