## Results

### Mice provided choice, response-time and no-response constraints in a reaction-time motion task [[C002]]

We analysed a reaction-time random-dot-motion task in which mice reported leftward or rightward motion by licking the corresponding response port (Figure 1A) [[C002]]. The dataset comprised {{N001}} across {{N004}}, collected with two task implementations that shared the decision structure but used different perturbation schedules (Figure 1B) [[C002]]. Each trial provided up to three complementary observations: response direction, response time and whether the mouse responded within the analysis window [[C002]]. Responses could register after a minimum viewing interval of {{N009}}; trials without a registered response by {{N010}} were classified as no response by 5 s, an analysis boundary rather than a common task-termination time [[C002]]. Across the descriptive dataset, {{N005}} contained a response within this window and {{N006}} did not [[C002]].

The three outcomes constrained different aspects of behaviour [[C002]]. Signed-coherence psychometric functions described which response the animals made, while correct and error response-time distributions described when they made it (Figure 1C–D) [[C002]]. No-response probabilities retained trials that would otherwise disappear from an analysis conditional on responding (Figure 1E) [[C002]]. Zero-coherence trials were displayed separately in correct/error summaries because neither direction was correct by definition [[C002]]. Considering these outcomes together was important because two models could produce similar choice proportions while differing in response timing or in the probability of reaching a response [[C002]].

On scheduled perturbation trials, strong motion briefly replaced the baseline stimulus in one of two directions [[C002]]. Assignment preceded the behavioural outcome, although a late pulse could be scheduled after the mouse had already responded [[C002]]. We therefore retained trials according to scheduled assignment rather than defining pulse exposure from the eventual response [[C002]]. Control-trial psychometric functions, correct and error response times, and no-response probabilities summarized the three outcomes later fitted jointly (Figure 1C–E) [[C002]]. These summaries revealed variation among mice, but task implementation, site and cohort were confounded and could not support biological comparisons between rigs [[C002]].

This design established two roles for the data [[C002]]. Control trials were used to fit and compare candidate decision models, whereas randomized pulse assignments tested whether a model selected under ordinary stimulus conditions could predict responses to a brief, strong perturbation [[C002]]. The descriptive panels defined those behavioural targets but were not themselves independent validation of a fitted model [[C002]].

### Stimulus reconstruction provided trial-specific estimates of visual evidence [[C003]]

Nominal coherence did not capture all of the evidence presented on an individual trial [[C003]]. We therefore regenerated each stimulus from its stored seed and task parameters and passed the rendered frames through causal, direction-opponent motion-energy filters (Figure 2A) [[C003]]. Replays continued through the analysis window rather than ending at the animal's response, and motion energy was calibrated separately for the two task implementations into equivalent-coherence units (Figure 2B) [[C003]]. Mean reconstructed evidence followed programmed coherence, while different seeds at the same programmed coherence produced persistent trial-to-trial variation (Figure 2C) [[C003]]. This reconstruction supplied the time-varying input to the decision models, but it was not a direct recording of the displayed frames, and filter delay was not interpreted as sensory or neural latency [[C003]].

Continuing the replay beyond the observed response prevented the simulated evidence stream from ending at a behaviour-dependent time [[C003]]. It allowed a simulated decision that occurred later than the recorded response to receive a stimulus of the same nominal duration as other simulations [[C003]]. Separate calibration placed the two task implementations on a common equivalent-coherence scale while leaving behavioural sensitivity to be estimated by the decision model [[C003]]. Together, these operations allowed a common model to respond to trial-specific evidence without assuming that the two rigs generated identical raw motion-energy scales [[C003]].

Paired pulse-present and pulse-omitted replays were identical before scheduled onset, with no pre-onset motion-energy difference introduced by the causal filter (Figure 2D) [[C003]]. At pulse offset, however, the task redrew background-dot directions; the two streams then consumed different random draws and acquired trial-specific differences [[C003]]. Their sign-aligned mean showed no persistent pulse-direction bias apart from a small deterministic rounding shift in specified cells [[C003]]. Labelled UCLA replay alternatives changed pulse-energy magnitude, while screen geometry, display-loop settings and the acquisition convention remained unresolved (Figure 2E) [[C003]]. The reconstruction thus made the tested input explicit without establishing frame-perfect replay fidelity or a universal pulse-energy error bound [[C003]].

This distinction mattered for every subsequent model prediction [[C003]]. Each prediction arose jointly from the reconstructed input, its temporal filtering and the decision-and-response model [[C003]]. Agreement would support that complete pipeline, whereas disagreement could not by itself identify which stage was responsible [[C003]]. Figure 2 therefore places the replay uncertainties next to the input representation rather than reserving them as an after-the-fact explanation for model failure [[C003]].

A supplementary hidden-coherence analysis reports a historical control-labelled analysis in which a pooled common-ratio model estimated a hidden-to-nominal coherence weight ratio of {{N011}} [[C003]]. This descriptive estimate was conditional on a shared model and historical labels; no scheduled-control-label sensitivity analysis was available [[C003]]. Because persistent within-trial evidence also prevents a time-specific choice association from isolating time-specific weighting, we did not interpret this ratio as a population-wide discount or a temporal weighting kernel [[C003]].

### Held-out control trials favoured the tested accumulator over the rapid-forgetting approximation [[C004]]

We first asked whether control behaviour distinguished the tested accumulator from the rapid-forgetting approximation [[C004]]. Both candidate implementations received the reconstructed input and shared the response mapping and fast-response mixture [[C004]]. The tested accumulator retained evidence to a bound, whereas the rapid-forgetting approximation implemented an extrema-like alternative (Figure 3A) [[C004]]. On held-out control trials, we scored their joint predictions for response probability, response direction and response time [[C004]]. Summed across folds and mice, rapid-forgetting-approximation minus tested-accumulator negative log likelihood was {{N016}}, favouring the tested accumulator (Figure 3B) [[C004]]. This was a within-cohort predictive comparison, not a population significance test: the displayed per-mouse threshold approximated Monte Carlo score variability, and the result applied only to the two complete implementations tested here [[C004]].

The joint score was essential to the comparison [[C004]]. It combined the probability of responding, response direction conditional on a response, and conditional response-time bins [[C004]]. Cross-validation held out control trials from the same mice and sessions, separating parameter fitting from scoring without testing generalization to new animals [[C004]]. A positive score difference therefore meant that the tested accumulator made better joint predictions for unseen trials within this cohort; it did not imply that every mouse or every component of the objective independently distinguished the candidates [[C004]].

Final fits reproduced the broad choice and correct-response-time trends but retained errors in some error-response-time and no-response predictions (Figure 3C–D) [[C004]]. These displays described fit adequacy on contributing control trials rather than providing an additional model-selection test [[C004]]. Recovery comprised {{N018}} under the generator, replicate, train/test and decision rule registered as {{N083}} [[C004]]. The fast-response component had hit rate {{N019}} when present, whereas drift variability had false-positive rate {{N020}} when absent [[C004]]. The fast-response delay correlation was {{N021}} across pooled generating and fitted datasets, not a within-mouse precision estimate [[C004]]. These recovery limits and near-bound parameters discouraged component-level interpretation, but did not change the relative held-out preference between the complete candidates [[C004]].

The recovery results separated two questions that are easily conflated [[C004]]. Component hit and false-positive rates asked whether the fitting procedure selected a simulated feature under the registered rule, whereas the generating-to-recovered correlation asked how closely a fitted parameter tracked its simulated value [[C004]]. Both pooled simulated replicates across mice and therefore did not provide additional biological subjects or mouse-specific precision [[C004]]. In particular, poor recovery of the fast-response delay and false selection of drift variability limited claims about those parameters even though the complete accumulator predicted held-out control behaviour better [[C004]].

Control trials therefore supported the tested accumulator over the specified rapid-forgetting approximation without uniquely identifying accumulation or excluding other non-integrating policies [[C004]]. We next used the scheduled pulses as a more demanding, out-of-condition test of behaviour under a brief, strong change in evidence [[C004]].

### Randomized motion pulses reliably redirected choices [[C005]]

Pulse direction changed the animals' responses (Figure 4A–B) [[C005]]. Across mice, positive rather than negative assignment shifted response direction by {{N051}}, with limits {{N052}} and {{N053}} [[C005]]. The complete mouse-level inference—including the two-sided one-sample test, statistic, degrees of freedom, 95% interval, raw probability value and Holm family—is registered as {{N088}}; the Holm-adjusted probability value was {{N054}} [[C005]]. Separate changes in right responses, left responses and no responses summed to zero within mouse, indicating redirection rather than a common increase in responding [[C005]].

The directional statistic compared the positive-minus-negative change in right responses with the corresponding change in left responses, using a symmetric scaling and retaining no-response outcomes [[C005]]. Its sign therefore followed the assigned motion direction rather than overall response propensity [[C005]]. The confidence interval and multiplicity-controlled probability value treated the mouse as the unit of group inference; trials and matched cells supplied within-mouse information rather than independent group replicates [[C005]].

The primary comparison used {{N055}} in {{N056}} defined by mouse × session × signed coherence × onset and containing both scheduled signs [[C005]]. Cells were weighted by their smaller arm count, weights were normalized within mouse, and mice contributed equally [[C005]]. The analysis retained {{N057}} that preceded pulse onset and {{N058}} without a response by 5 s, preserving the randomized assignment estimand rather than conditioning it on when or whether the mouse responded [[C005]]. Timing-matched controls used recorded sham onsets for UW and outcome-independent pseudo-onsets for UCLA (Figure 4C) [[C005]]. Because the two pulse-versus-control contrasts occupied different common-support cells, they described outcome components and alignment sensitivity but did not decompose the primary signed comparison or test pulse-sign asymmetry [[C005]].

All three comparisons formed the registered Holm family [[C005]]. The signed positive-versus-negative assignment comparison was primary, while each pulse-versus-control comparison located one assigned direction relative to aligned trials without a scheduled signed pulse [[C005]]. UCLA pseudo-onsets were generated without reference to behavioural outcomes, so changing the pseudo-onset seed could alter control alignment but could not redefine the randomized signed contrast [[C005]]. An inconclusive control comparison was therefore not interpreted as evidence that its corresponding effect was absent [[C005]].

Available task-code snapshots and a pre-outcome balance audit supported the assignment procedure, although code versions were not pinned to each collection session [[C005]]. The assignment-based behavioural analysis was unaffected by known limitations in the legacy model inputs: those inputs excluded {{N070}} and historically mislabelled the UCLA no-response pulse trials summarized in {{N071}} [[C005]]. Those limitations instead qualified the later comparison with model predictions [[C005]].

The response-associated pulse effect developed over time (Figure 4D) [[C005]]. Across all scheduled trials, the cumulative directional contrast reached {{N059}} at the shared endpoint. The registered curve grid, endpoint, mouse denominator and interval convention were {{N090}} [[C005]]. The contrast was small in the first 0.5 s and increased mainly from 0.5 to 2 s after logged onset, when many responses occurred [[C005]]. These windows were descriptive, not a multiplicity-corrected early-versus-late test [[C005]]. Because cumulative response curves carry earlier differences forward and include no-response trials, they locate response-associated divergence rather than the time of evidence entry, an integration kernel, neural latency or a processing stage [[C005]].

Right and left responses entered these curves as competing outcomes, and trials without either response remained in the no-response probability [[C005]]. The display therefore avoided selecting only eventual responders or conditioning on survival beyond pulse onset [[C005]]. This inclusive construction preserved assignment, but it also meant that a late cumulative difference could carry forward an earlier effect and that a small early difference could simply reflect few emitted responses [[C005]]. The temporal pattern nevertheless posed a clear predictive target: the model had to reproduce not only the final directional shift but also how that shift appeared across response times [[C005]].

### Control-fitted models predicted the wrong temporal profile of the pulse response [[C006]]

We next compared the observed pulse-aligned curves with simulations from the control-fitted models (Figure 5A–B) [[C006]]. Observations and predictions used identical common-support cells and mouse weights on the historical model-input rows that overlapped the behavioural analysis [[C006]]. Across stored joint pulse gains and gains chosen to improve pulse choices, the models concentrated the directional contrast early and predicted a later partial decline [[C006]]. The mice instead showed little initial contrast, followed by a positive difference that emerged mainly from 0.5 to 2 s and persisted [[C006]]. Thus, the tested model-and-input pipelines failed to generalize the temporal profile of the pulse response [[C006]].

The discrepancy centred on temporal shape rather than overall direction [[C006]]. Both mice and simulations moved in the assigned direction over part of the response window, but the simulated effect arrived too early and later weakened or partially reversed [[C006]]. Increasing pulse gain strengthened the predicted response without moving it into the delayed, sustained profile observed in the animals [[C006]]. The pulse trials therefore revealed a limitation that was not apparent from the control-trial fits alone [[C006]].

The comparison was deliberately descriptive [[C006]]. Its intervals summarized between-mouse variation within the observed or predicted curves rather than a paired test of their difference, and simulations added Monte Carlo uncertainty [[C006]]. The common-row restriction also preserved the historical UCLA no-response labels and did not integrate parameter uncertainty or repair legacy exclusions [[C006]]. Pulse gain could rescale the reconstructed pulse while leaving control parameters fixed, but changing gain did not remove the temporal-shape mismatch [[C006]]. This result constrained an amplitude-only repair of the tested pipeline without locating the failure in sensory encoding, decision dynamics or response generation [[C006]]. This descriptive mismatch does not establish that the tested accumulator itself failed, because a different stimulus representation, pulse transformation or response mapping could alter the same prediction [[C006]].

The gain profiles revealed a related tension between choice and timing predictions in the two mice that carried enough information for this diagnostic (Figure 5C–D) [[C009]]. In PSUIM6 and PSUIM7, gains selected to improve held-out choices reduced choice NLL by {{N063}} and {{N064}} relative to the joint-gain solution, but increased the remaining response and response-time objective by {{N065}} and {{N066}} [[C009]]. The registered search and diagnostic settings were {{N093}}; each dense-mouse diagnostic used one main Monte Carlo seed [[C009]]. These QMLE score changes retained the original objective weights, were not calibrated significance statistics and had no direct repeated-seed precision estimate for either mouse [[C009]]. Choice profiles were flatter in most other mice, so this was a two-mouse explanation of the group-level mismatch rather than evidence for a cohort-wide conflict or a unique mechanism [[C009]].

### Failed predictive checks precluded the planned timing test [[C007]]

The pulse mismatch also appeared in the prespecified adequacy gates (Supplementary Figure S6) [[C007]]. The primary baseline was the tested accumulator pipeline; the secondary `L1U1V1Z0G1` baseline added leak and urgency within the same input framework [[C007]]. In the original gates, observed-minus-predicted choice departure toward the pulse was {{N026}} for the primary baseline and {{N027}} for the secondary baseline, while response-time coverage fell below the requirement for both [[C007]]. The registered criteria, {{N095}}, screened adequacy rather than testing equivalence [[C007]]. A revision prompted by this failure fitted one cross-validated pulse gain per mouse while fixing the control parameters [[C007]]. Choice departures remained {{N030}} and {{N034}}, and coverage was only {{N035}} and {{N036}} of mouse-by-quantile response-time cells for the primary and secondary baselines [[C007]]. Both revised pipelines therefore failed the combined gate [[C007]].

The sequence of checks was designed to prevent a timing contrast from being interpreted on top of an inadequate predictive model [[C007]]. The original gates asked whether the frozen control-selected pipelines reproduced pulse-trial choices, response times and no-response probabilities well enough to serve as timing baselines [[C007]]. After they failed, the cross-validated gain revision tested whether one time-invariant scale factor per mouse could repair pulse magnitude while leaving all control parameters fixed [[C007]]. Cross-validation prevented a trial from being predicted using a gain fitted to that same trial, but the revision remained exploratory because the observed failure had prompted it [[C007]]. Its failure showed that amplitude rescaling alone did not create an adequate replacement baseline [[C007]].

Simulations showed that the intended pulse tests were also insensitive under their registered generators [[C007]]. The frozen strategy procedure returned the correct verdict counts and denominators in {{N097}}; poor recovery did not imply equivalence between the tested accumulator and rapid-forgetting approximation [[C007]]. The gain-revised timing calibration used {{N096}}, and its decay-level detection counts were {{N098}} [[C007]]. The two simulations addressed different questions and neither propagated uncertainty in the fitted control parameters [[C007]]. Their empirical rates therefore characterized these specific procedures and generators, not general power against every form of rapid forgetting or temporal weighting [[C007]].


Because the original and revised gates failed and the calibrated procedure had inadequate sensitivity, the confirmatory timing test was not run and the prespecified stopping rule ended further baseline revision [[C007]]. This outcome was not evidence for primacy, recency or flat temporal weighting [[C007]]. Instead, the randomized pulses established a robust directional behavioural effect while exposing a specific failure of the tested control-fitted models to predict its temporal expression [[C007]].
