### Figure 1: apparatus, task and analysis sets

Head-fixed mice viewed random-dot motion and reported leftward or rightward motion by licking the corresponding port. Correct responses were rewarded and errors were followed by a timeout. Responses could register after {{N009}}. Reaction time was measured from motion onset. On the UW rig, the stored response clock began when the response window opened, so preprocessing added the minimum-viewing interval; on the UCLA rig, the response clock began at motion onset. Signed coherence encoded direction, and the analyzed baseline levels were {{N068}}. Scheduled pulse trials used {{N069}}. Rig-specific replay and assignment operations retained these different schedules. Because the task stimulus could continue after the modeled period, {{N010}} defined the analysis cutoff and the no-response outcome; it was not treated as the task deadline or evidence that the animal never responded.

Descriptive analyses combined processed valid-response and censored-trial tables, giving {{N005}} with a response in the window and {{N006}} classified as censored. Censored trials included recorded responses after the cutoff and trials without a recorded response. Within the modeled window they were assigned infinite response time and no choice; later recorded responses were not back-classified as choices. The legacy model input excluded {{N070}} because their pulse duration was unknown, while descriptive analyses retained them. We therefore kept analysis-specific denominators distinct and did not modify raw data.

Pulse analyses reconstructed scheduled pulse assignment from the pulse flag, baseline and pulse coherences, and onset. They retained trials with responses before onset or during the scheduled pulse. An earlier preprocessing label could reclassify a pulse as control when the response preceded pulse completion, making that label outcome-dependent. We did not use it for assignment-based contrasts. The legacy model input also contained {{N071}}. We left that input unchanged and restricted model–data overlays to common rows.

The descriptive and model sets consequently answered different questions. Descriptive panels counted scheduled categories in the accepted behavioral table, including invalid-duration trials whose outcomes were known. Model fitting required reconstructable input traces and used the smaller legacy table. Assignment-based estimates returned to the joined behavioral table and corrected scheduled labels, whereas model diagnostics used its intersection with legacy inputs. Each Results destination therefore retained its own population; no denominator was silently transferred between analyses.

### Figure 2: stimulus replay and motion-energy input

We regenerated each trial from its recorded seed with rig-specific implementations under {{N072}}. The replays reproduced programmed coherence changes but were not treated as frame-perfect display records. UCLA acquisition frame rate, dot lifetime, monitor refresh, vertical synchronization and full screen geometry were not recorded; replay update frames may have reduced its estimated pulse energy. The UW replay placed the pulse at the logged time but left the background one dot step behind the task convention. These uncertainties affected reconstructed input, not scheduled assignment.

The paired stimulus audit used {{N099}}. Each sampled seed was rendered with the pulse present and physically omitted. The branches were identical before onset, but returning to baseline redrew noise-dot directions and caused subsequent random streams to diverge. The audit therefore tested sign-aligned artifacts under one pre-onset coupling, not a unique no-pulse counterfactual. The model counterfactual instead removed the fitted expected pulse component while retaining that trial's residual motion energy.

The audit compared rendered frames, reconstructed motion-energy traces and programmed dot states. Equality before onset checked both replay branching and causal filter support. Comparisons after offset separated the signed mean from the unsigned trial-level difference: a redraw could increase trial-specific disagreement without producing a pulse-direction bias. We evaluated labeled UCLA display-loop alternatives rather than choosing an undocumented acquisition convention. These checks bounded the implemented representation; they did not verify display timestamps or the frames seen by each mouse.

The motion-energy representation used oriented spatial and temporal filters to form quadrature pairs. Squared responses were summed within direction, and leftward energy was subtracted from rightward energy:

\[
M(t)=R_1(t)^2+R_2(t)^2-L_1(t)^2-L_2(t)^2.
\]

Opponent spatiotemporal motion energy can be constructed from oriented filters and quadrature-pair energy operations [[C017]] [@adelson1985spatiotemporal]. This precedent supports the filter architecture, not replay fidelity. Filter constants and support were {{N073}}. We zero-padded the start and spatial boundaries, flipped the temporal kernel for causal convolution and excluded future-frame contributions. Motion energy was averaged over the displayed field and calibrated separately for each rig by the slope relating time-averaged energy to nominal signed coherence. Screen geometry was not corrected because the necessary measurements were unavailable.

The quadrature construction paired complementary spatial and temporal components to form directional filters; reversing their relative signs produced the opposite direction. Squaring and summing removed phase dependence before opponent subtraction. Calibration changed the evidence scale but did not retune filters per mouse or use behavioral outcomes. Between-mouse stimulus sensitivity therefore remained available to the fitted drift gain rather than being absorbed into preprocessing.

For model inputs, full-trial motion energy was decomposed as \(M=S+N\). The expected component \(S\) combined the rig-specific onset response with the mean response to a pulse coherence change, estimated from replays after subtracting the onset response and without using choices. The residual \(N=M-S\) retained seed-specific stimulus variation. Input scaling and temporal expansion used the registered operations: {{N074}}. The expanded input extended across the full analysis window rather than ending at the animal's response.

The shared-ratio model fitted each mouse's intercept and nominal-coherence slope while replacing its predictor by nominal coherence plus \(k\) times hidden coherence. ### Figure 3: decision models

Diffusion models use accuracy and response-time distributions jointly to constrain latent decision and nondecision components [[C016]] [@ratcliff2008diffusion; @palmer2005effect; @bogacz2006physics]. This rationale does not validate the present objective, reconstructed input or candidate set.

For trial \(i\), the tested accumulator evolved between absorbing bounds at zero and \(a\):

\[
x_i(t+\Delta t)=x_i(t)+[gM_i(t)+b+\eta_i]\Delta t+\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon_t,
\]

where \(x_i(0)=za\), \(g\) was drift gain, \(b\) was drift offset, \(\eta_i\sim\mathcal N(0,s_v^2)\) was an across-trial drift offset and \(\epsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) was within-trial diffusion noise. Fixed integration settings were {{N075}}; fitted common-model bounds were {{N076}}. A bound crossing determined choice, and fitted nondecision time was added to crossing time. The sensory-filter delay was already present in \(M_i(t)\). The common model retained drift variability and set leak, urgency and starting-point variability to zero.

Bound separation \(a\) controlled the evidence distance required for a response, and \(z\) expressed the starting point as a fraction of that separation. Drift gain scaled reconstructed input, drift offset captured an input-independent directional tendency, and \(s_v\) allowed a trial-level drift displacement shared across the trajectory. Nondecision time shifted a bound-crossing time without changing the decision path. Parameter bounds were fitting constraints rather than physiological ranges; the fixed diffusion scale set arbitrary evidence units.

The fast-response mixture followed {{N077}}. On mixture trials, direction was drawn from the fitted right-response probability and time equaled the fixed mixture onset plus an exponentially distributed delay with fitted mean. The delay was not interpreted mechanistically. Simulated times were floored at the minimum-viewing interval; responses after the cutoff and unresolved simulations became censored outcomes.

The rapid-forgetting approximation added leakage toward the starting point:

\[
x_i(t+\Delta t)=x_i(t)+[gM_i(t)+b+\eta_i-\lambda\{x_i(t)-za\}]\Delta t+\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon_t.
\]

Its fitted leak range, nominal time constants and discretization were {{N078}}. This was an operational rapid-forgetting approximation after sensory filtering, not an exact discrete-sample extrema detector. Both candidates used the same fast-response parameterization.

### Figure 3: objective, fitting and control comparison

Models were fitted separately for each mouse by minimizing a censored quantile negative log likelihood. Within each coherence-by-condition cell, a multinomial term scored left, right and censored outcomes, followed by conditional response-time histograms for each response direction. The registered binning, sparse-term, smoothing, weighting and empty-cell settings were: {{N079}}. The objective was a simulation-based comparison score, not a calibrated likelihood.

The multinomial first separated whether a response occurred and which bound it reached. Conditional response-time terms then compared simulated and observed distributions within response direction. The observed-data inclusion rule was fixed before fitting each cell; simulated sparsity did not remove a difficult term. Pseudocount smoothing prevented undefined logarithms, while the explicit empty-prediction penalty prevented an optimizer from benefiting when simulation failed to populate an observed category.

Optimization and fit simulations used {{N080}}. Common random numbers made the simulated objective deterministic within a fit. Targeted warm starts were used when a nested larger model fitted worse than a smaller model. Frozen pulse predictions used {{N023}}.

Control-only cross-validation used the registered scheme ({{N081}}). Folds were constructed within mouse and condition, each mouse-fold was fitted independently, held-out negative log likelihood was summed across folds and then mice, and lower score indicated better prediction. Candidate minus tested-accumulator score was therefore positive when the tested accumulator was favored; the observed sum was {{N016}}. This compared specified pipelines on held-out trials from the same mice and sessions, not new animals or a population significance test.

Stratification preserved condition composition before within-condition permutation and cyclic fold assignment. Training excluded the held-out fold for both parameter estimation and selection. Held-out evaluation used a fixed independent simulation seed, and scores were not normalized by mouse trial count before aggregation. The total therefore weighted mice by available held-out trials; mouse-level score signs were also inspected rather than treating the aggregate alone as population replication.

Monte Carlo rescoring used {{N082}}. The approximate per-mouse working threshold was non-inferential and was not a universal calibrated cutoff for {{N016}}. Components were retained under the prespecified parsimony rule only when held-out improvement exceeded simulation noise in most mice, with recovery and interpretability considered separately.

### Figure 3: recovery and identifiability

Recovery used {{N083}}. In words, the common model, a model without drift variability, a model without the fast-response mixture, a leak model and an urgency model each served as generator and candidate. Each synthetic dataset had independent training and test simulations. A mechanism “hit” meant that its present candidate beat its absent counterpart by the registered held-out threshold when the mechanism generated the data; a false positive applied the same rule when it did not. Denominators pooled mice and replicates, so recovery rates did not establish within-mouse identifiability. The complete design yielded {{N018}}; full matrices and bound flags were stored with the recovery outputs.

Generating parameters came from the corresponding fitted mouse-level model. Each candidate was refitted to a synthetic training dataset and scored on an independent synthetic test dataset. Mechanism-specific rates contrasted the same present-versus-absent pair under generators with and without that component. Because some fitted generating effects lay near their null values, a nominally present generator did not always produce a practically separable dataset. Parameter correlations pooled mice and replicates and were descriptive checks, not proof of local identifiability.

Evidence-accumulation parameter estimates should be accompanied by recovery or identifiability checks before mechanistic interpretation [[C018]] [@wiecki2013hddm; @luken2025parameter]. These sources motivate validation discipline but do not describe our estimator. We therefore treated near-zero generating effects, pooled correlations, weak recovery, parameter bounds, high-coherence error misfit and late-response misfit as limits on component-level claims.

The exploratory pulse analysis joined valid-response, censored, aggregate and retained model-input tables by unique trial identity without modifying them. For each mouse, right, left and no-response probabilities were standardized over supported cells. For positive minus negative assignment,

\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{direction}}=[\Delta P(\mathrm{right})-\Delta P(\mathrm{left})]/2.
\]

The three outcome changes summed to zero. The primary test and tail, degrees of freedom, raw probability and Holm adjustment were {{N088}}; the registered estimate and interval limits were {{N051}}, {{N052}} and {{N053}}, and the adjusted probability was {{N054}}. The Holm family comprised the primary positive-versus-negative contrast plus the positive-versus-control and control-versus-negative contrasts.

The one-sample test operated on mouse-level standardized contrasts, not cell or trial rows. We reported both its raw probability and the Holm-adjusted probability for the declared family, without applying an additional correction to the interval. Leave-one-mouse-out, session-half, within-session-half, zero-coherence, matched-seed and responder-only analyses were robustness or secondary descriptions; they did not replace the primary supported-cell estimand or enter a new multiplicity family.
