# Methods

## Animals and experimental oversight

The descriptive behavioural dataset comprised {{N001}} mice tested in {{N004}} mouse-sessions across two cohorts and two experimental rigs. The mouse was the unit of inference for group-level behavioural estimates. Rig, site and cohort were completely confounded, so we used rig only to reconstruct its corresponding stimulus and schedule and did not test or interpret differences between rigs. ## Figure 1: apparatus, task and behavioural analysis sets

Mice were head-fixed and viewed random-dot motion. They reported leftward or rightward motion by licking the corresponding response port. Correct responses were rewarded and errors were followed by a timeout. A response could register only after a minimum viewing interval of {{N009}} s. Reaction time was measured from motion onset. On the UW rig, the stored response time began when the response window opened, so preprocessing added the minimum viewing interval; on the UCLA rig, the response clock began at motion onset. [MISSING: training stages/criteria, session rules and complete rig/display hardware]

Baseline absolute coherences represented in the analysed dataset were 0%, 9%, 18%, 36% and 100%; signed coherence encoded motion direction. On scheduled pulse trials, the task briefly replaced the baseline coherence with positive or negative 100% coherence. The UW schedule used early, middle and late pulse or sham categories; the UCLA schedule used two onset clusters and no late category. Because the two implementations used different timing and display loops, all replay and assignment operations retained the rig-specific rules. The task stimulus could continue beyond the modelled period. We therefore defined no response by {{N010}} s as an analysis outcome rather than a task timeout or evidence that the animal never responded.

Descriptive analyses combined processed valid-response and censored-trial tables, giving {{N005}} responses within the analysis window and {{N006}} censored trials. Censored trials included both responses recorded after the cutoff and trials without a recorded response. Within the modelled window, they were assigned an infinite response time and no choice. Actual responses after the cutoff were not back-classified as choices. Fifteen UCLA trials had missing or non-positive pulse durations because responses occurred during the pulse; these trials remained in descriptive figures but were excluded from the legacy decision-model input because their stimulus duration was unknown. The resulting analysis-specific denominators were kept distinct rather than reconciled by modifying raw data.

Pulse analyses used the scheduled pulse assignment reconstructed from the pulse flag, baseline and pulse coherences, and onset. This definition retained trials on which a response occurred before pulse onset or while the pulse was in progress. An earlier preprocessing label set a pulse to control when the response preceded pulse completion and could therefore depend on the outcome. We did not use that historical label for assignment-based contrasts. Forty-eight UCLA no-response pulse trials were historically labelled as controls in the legacy decision-model input; direction could be recovered for 43, including 38 genuine non-null pulses, whereas five 0%-coherence assignments had unknowable direction. We retained the legacy model inputs unchanged and restricted model–data overlays to common rows. No raw data were modified.

## Figure 2: stimulus replay and motion-energy input

We regenerated each trial from its recorded random seed with separate UCLA and UW replay implementations. The replay rendered 5 s at 60 frames/s at the rig's native pixel resolution, then area-averaged frames to a common spatial sampling of 0.5° per sample. The two implementations reproduced their recorded programmed coherence changes but were not treated as frame-perfect records of the display. In particular, UCLA acquisition frame rate, dot lifetime, monitor refresh, vertical synchronisation and full screen geometry were not recorded; the UCLA replay inserted update frames that may have reduced pulse energy. UW display-frame convention placed the pulse at the correct recorded time but left the replayed background one dot step behind the task implementation. These uncertainties affected quantitative input reconstruction, not the randomized assignment labels.

Paired stimulus audits replayed the same initial seed with the pulse present or physically omitted. The paired branches were identical before onset. Returning to baseline coherence redrew the noise-dot directions, after which their random-number streams diverged. Thus the physical-omission replay preserved the pre-onset stimulus but did not hold every future noise direction fixed. We used it to test sign-aligned artefacts, not as a unique no-pulse counterfactual. The legacy model counterfactual instead removed the fitted expected pulse component and retained that trial's residual motion energy. We kept these two counterfactuals conceptually separate.

The motion-energy representation followed an oriented spatiotemporal energy construction. Spatial filters were paired with fast and slow temporal filters to form two quadrature components for each direction. Squared responses were summed within direction, and leftward energy was subtracted from rightward energy:

\[
M(t)=R_1(t)^2+R_2(t)^2-L_1(t)^2-L_2(t)^2.
\]

This sequence of oriented filtering, quadrature energy and opponent subtraction is an established way to construct direction-selective motion energy [[C017]] [@adelson1985spatiotemporal]. The precedent supports the filter architecture, not the physical fidelity of the replay.

We set the spatial constants to 6° and 3° and the temporal rate constant to 34 s\(^{-1}\), which yielded a nominal preference near the programmed dot speed. Temporal support extended to 0.55 s. We padded the start of the temporal sequence and spatial boundaries with zeros, flipped the temporal kernel for true causal convolution and never allowed future frames to contribute to earlier samples. Motion energy was averaged over the displayed field and calibrated separately for each rig by the slope relating time-averaged motion energy to nominal signed coherence. The resulting input was expressed in equivalent percent coherence; screen geometry was not corrected because the necessary measurements were unavailable.

For model inputs, full-trial motion energy was decomposed as \(M=S+N\). The expected component \(S\) combined the rig-specific response to baseline coherence onset with the measured mean response to the pulse coherence change. The pulse response was estimated from replayed pulse trials after subtracting the onset response, without reference to choices. The residual \(N=M-S\) retained seed-specific stimulus variation. Both components were divided by 100 to give coherence fractions, expanded from 60-Hz samples onto the model's 1-ms grid and available for the full analysis window rather than truncated at the animal's response.

## Figure 3: decision models

We used diffusion models because choice proportions and response-time distributions jointly constrain decision and nondecision components more strongly than either summary alone [[C016]] [@ratcliff2008diffusion; @palmer2005effect; @bogacz2006physics]. This general rationale does not validate the present likelihood, input reconstruction or candidate set.

For trial \(i\), the tested accumulator evolved in 1-ms steps between absorbing bounds at 0 and \(a\):

\[
x_i(t+\Delta t)=x_i(t)+\left[gM_i(t)+b+\eta_i\right]\Delta t+\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon_t,
\]

where \(x_i(0)=za\), \(g\) was the fitted drift gain, \(b\) was a fitted drift offset, \(\eta_i\sim\mathcal N(0,s_v^2)\) was the across-trial drift offset and \(\epsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) was within-trial diffusion noise. A lower or upper bound crossing determined left or right choice, respectively, and fitted nondecision time was added to the crossing time. The stimulus filter's causal delay was already present in \(M_i(t)\). The common model retained drift variability and set leak, urgency and starting-point variability to zero.

A fitted fast-response mixture assigned a fraction \(p_g\) of trials to stimulus-independent responses. Mixture times began at the minimum viewing interval and added an exponentially distributed delay; direction followed a fitted right-response probability rather than being fixed at 0.5. Simulated response times were floored at the minimum viewing interval. Responses later than the analysis cutoff, and simulations that did not reach a bound, were mapped to the censored outcome.

The rapid-forgetting approximation added a leak term toward the starting point,

\[
x_i(t+\Delta t)=x_i(t)+\left[gM_i(t)+b+\eta_i-\lambda\{x_i(t)-za\}\right]\Delta t+\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon_t,
\]

with \(\lambda\) restricted to 20–200 s\(^{-1}\). It operated after sensory filtering and was an operational, rapidly forgetting candidate, not an exact discrete-sample extrema detector. At the largest rates, its nominal 5–50-ms time constants were also subject to the 1-ms Euler discretisation. The same free fast-response bias was used for the tested accumulator and rapid-forgetting approximation.

## Figure 3: likelihood, fitting, comparison and recovery

Models were fitted separately for each mouse by minimising a censored quantile negative log likelihood. Within each coherence-by-condition cell, a multinomial term scored left, right and censored outcomes. Conditional response-time histograms were then scored separately for left and right responses, using nine quantile bins when at least nine observed responses were available for that direction. A response-time term was omitted when this observed-data criterion was not met. We added 0.5 to simulated counts in each category before evaluating log probabilities. This objective was a simulation-based comparison score, not a calibrated conventional likelihood.

Optimisation used differential evolution followed by Nelder–Mead refinement. Differential evolution used zero relative tolerance, an absolute tolerance of one score unit and a default maximum of 1,000 iterations. Fits used 15 simulation repetitions during the model-selection grid, with targeted warm starts when a nested larger model fitted worse than a smaller model. Frozen pulse predictions and out-of-fold revised predictions used {{N023}} repetitions per trial. Exact execution commits and environments remained part of the unresolved software lock rather than being inferred from library names.

Five-fold cross-validation assigned trials to folds stratified by condition. The decision-strategy comparison was restricted to control trials for both fitting and held-out scoring, which prevented pulse outcomes from selecting the baseline. We reported candidate-minus-tested-accumulator score differences so that a positive value favoured the tested accumulator; the summed held-out comparison was {{N016}} negative-log-likelihood units. This was a relative comparison of two specified pipelines, not a population-level test or proof that behaviour used a unique mechanism. Cross-validation measured prediction of held-out trials from the same animals and sessions, not generalisation to new mice.

Monte Carlo rescoring with multiple seeds estimated the working variability of score differences. Parsimony retained a component only when its held-out improvement exceeded this simulation noise across most mice and remained interpretable. Recovery generated synthetic datasets from five model variants and fitted five candidate variants for each mouse and replicate, yielding {{N018}} fit files. Because recovery and identifiability checks are required before mechanistic interpretation of evidence-accumulation parameters [[C018]] [@wiecki2013hddm; @luken2025parameter], we treated weak recovery, boundary estimates and high-coherence error or late-response misfit as limits on component-level claims. These sources motivate the validation discipline but do not describe the present maximum-likelihood estimator.

## Figure 4: scheduled pulse assignment and directional estimand

The exploratory pulse analysis joined valid-response, censored, aggregate and retained model-input tables by unique trial identity without altering source files. Task-code inspection indicated that UW drew a pulse or sham category before each trial conditional on signed coherence, whereas UCLA shuffled fixed pulse categories within coherence blocks. Neither assignment was announced to the mouse. The inspected task repositories were current snapshots and were not pinned to each collection session.

The primary analysis included baseline absolute coherence no greater than 36%. We compared positive and negative scheduled pulse assignments within cells defined by mouse, session, signed coherence and onset stratum. A cell contributed only if both signs occurred. Its weight was the smaller arm count, weights were normalised within mouse, and mice were then weighted equally. UW cells from an asymmetric schedule containing only rightward pulses were unsupported and excluded. The supported primary set contained {{N055}} trials in {{N056}} cells, including {{N057}} pre-onset responses and {{N058}} trials with no response by the cutoff.

Within each mouse, we standardised the probabilities of right response, left response and no response over the supported cells. For positive minus negative assignment, the directional contrast was

\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{direction}}=\frac{\Delta P(\mathrm{right})-\Delta P(\mathrm{left})}{2}.
\]

The three outcome changes summed to zero. Group estimates were unweighted means across mice with two-sided 95% Student-\(t\) intervals; the primary estimate, interval limits and Holm-adjusted probability were represented by {{N051}}, {{N052}}, {{N053}} and {{N054}}, respectively. The correction family contained the primary positive-versus-negative contrast and two pulse-versus-control contrasts.

UW control trials used their recorded scheduled sham onsets. UCLA control trials received outcome-independent pseudo-onsets drawn with a fixed seed from observed pulse onsets in the same session and absolute-coherence stratum. Five additional seeds tested sensitivity. We retained all assigned pulse trials regardless of realised response time and made no adjustment for post-assignment response or motion energy.

## Figure 4: pulse-aligned outcome curves and statistical validation

Pulse-aligned curves treated right and left responses as competing cumulative outcomes and no response as the third state. For elapsed time \(u\) after scheduled or pseudo-onset, the three estimated probabilities obeyed \(F_R(u)+F_L(u)+S(u)=1\). All-scheduled curves retained pre-onset responses as mass present at \(u=0\). Onset-eligible curves instead included trials still unresponded immediately before onset and reapplied the same common-support weighting after eligibility. The shared endpoint was 4.58 s after onset, the largest follow-up available for every onset category; its all-scheduled directional contrast was {{N059}} percentage points. Intervals on curves and window increments were pointwise Student-\(t\) intervals across mice and were not multiplicity-corrected. These curves described when responses were emitted, not when evidence entered or persisted in the decision process.

We tested the behavioural estimators with simulations that preserved the observed trials, schedules, arms, cells, onsets and 5-s censoring rule while replacing only response side and time. Outcomes were drawn from each mouse-by-coherence control distribution. Declared generators included no effect, three directional-effect magnitudes, a censoring perturbation and an effect confined to a post-onset time window. We generated 200 datasets per scenario, used exact expectations under each generator as truth and applied the analysis code itself. Across scenarios, Student-\(t\) interval coverage ranged from {{N060}} to {{N061}}; the Holm-adjusted primary test had power {{N062}} for the six-percentage-point generator. Mouse-bootstrap intervals were retained only as a sensitivity analysis because they undercovered in these simulations.

## Figure 5: frozen pulse predictions and gain revision

Before recorded pulse outcomes were compared, we froze {{N022}} prediction files: three candidate baselines for each mouse. Each file contained matched predictions with the expected pulse component present and removed, using the control-fit parameters and {{N023}} repetitions per trial. The original strategy statistic compared models by their improvement in observed pulse-choice log likelihood when the pulse component was included. It summed paired, per-trial differences and favoured a model only if the total exceeded three estimated sampling standard deviations and had the same sign in at least six mice. Synthetic full-cohort datasets were generated and scored with the same rule to assess its sensitivity.

After the original predictive gate failed, the amended analysis fitted one mouse-specific pulse gain from 0 to 50 while holding all control parameters fixed. Training-fold pulse trials selected the gain by grid search followed by golden-section search under common random numbers and 50 repetitions; held-out folds were predicted with {{N023}} repetitions. We retained original, gain-revised and choice-optimal predictions as distinct objects. Because recovered UCLA no-response pulse trials were absent from the legacy model inputs, all comparisons with the assignment-based curves used only rows shared by both datasets.

## Figure 5: exploratory objective and temporal diagnostics

For diagnosis, we decomposed the censored quantile objective exactly into response-versus-censored, choice-given-response and conditional left/right response-time terms, retaining the original 0.5 smoothing, bin definitions and fewer-than-nine-response omission rule. The components reproduced the original objective numerically. We profiled pulse gain while control parameters remained fixed, selected gains on training folds and scored the held-out fold with the same common-random-number simulations. These analyses were developed after inspection of the failed predictive checks and were therefore exploratory.

We defined a demonstrated choice-versus-remaining-objective tradeoff before reading its diagnostic output: the choice-optimal gain had to exceed the joint optimum in every available seed, improve the in-sample choice term by more than the working 1.92-score-unit threshold, worsen the remaining terms by more than that threshold, and show positive summed choice improvement and remaining-objective cost on held-out folds. The evidence met this rule only in PSUIM6 and PSUIM7. Their held-out choice improvements were {{N063}} and {{N064}} score units, while the corresponding remaining-objective costs were {{N065}} and {{N066}}. We did not generalise this diagnostic to the other mice, whose choice profiles or response-time terms were often weakly informative.

Observed and simulated directional curves were then computed on the same common rows, cells and weights. Comparisons were descriptive: they assessed the time course of model mismatch, not a new inferential timing test. Control psychometric residuals and response-time-term coverage were inspected to distinguish pulse-specific disagreement from baseline fit limitations.

## Supplementary Figure S6: predictive gates, sensitivity and stopping rules

The prespecified predictive gate was an adequacy screen, not an equivalence test. It pooled pulse timings and required the 95% interval for choice-probability departure to include zero, or the absolute departure to be less than 25% of the predicted pulse effect on the logit scale. At least 80% of response-time quantile cells and 80% of censoring cells also had to fall within their simulated 95% ranges. The original tested-accumulator and secondary baselines both failed the choice and response-time requirements.

The gain-revised gate retained the same adequacy logic using out-of-fold gain predictions. Its calibration generated 20 full-cohort datasets at each of four levels of drift-gain decline over 0.5 s: 0%, 10%, 20% and 40%. Generating pulse gains were the fitted gains, control fits were held fixed, and the complete gate-plus-timing procedure was applied. We recorded gate passage, false detections under no decline and detections overall and conditional on passing the gate. The calibration showed inadequate sensitivity to the specified declines.

The confirmatory timing model would have used predicted choice log odds as an offset in a Bayesian logistic mixed model, with fixed effects for timing and rig, mouse-specific intercepts and timing slopes, normal unit-scale priors for fixed effects and half-normal unit-scale priors for random-effect standard deviations. Middle-versus-early timing was primary; late-versus-early and UW timing trends were secondary. Under the stopping rule, a failed revised gate or inadequate calibration sensitivity ended the procedure. Both conditions occurred, so the timing model and all planned decay or signed-leak extensions were not run. No null primacy, recency or flat-weighting result was estimated.

## Reproducibility, reporting and availability

All group behavioural inference used mice rather than trials as independent units; stimulus-level replay summaries instead used trials and were labelled as such. Randomisation was evaluated from task code and balance checks, multiplicity correction was specified for the three primary behavioural contrasts, and exploratory diagnostics were separated from the closed gate procedure. Analysis tables and derived outputs were versioned; raw data were neither modified nor overwritten.

We used the ARRIVE 2.0 and MDAR reporting frameworks to keep unresolved animal, ethics, design, blinding, software and availability records explicit pending author verification.
