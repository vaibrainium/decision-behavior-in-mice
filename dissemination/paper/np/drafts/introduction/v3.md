## Introduction

Perceptual decisions are often described as an accumulation of noisy sensory evidence toward a bound. This account links stimulus strength to both choice and response time, but familiar behavioral signatures are not unique to accumulation. Tested non-integrating, leaky and urgency-based strategies can reproduce choice accuracy or temporal-weighting patterns that appear integration-like [@stine2020differentiating; @tsetsos2012timevarying; @cisek2009decisions; @okazawa2018psychophysical] [[C010]]. Joint choice and response-time distributions provide stronger constraints than accuracy alone because a strategy that predicts the same choices may predict different decision times [@stine2020differentiating; @ratcliff2008diffusion; @palmer2005effect; @winkel2014early] [[C011]]. This makes the full response distribution useful for rejecting particular implementations, although success within one stimulus regime still identifies a winner only among the candidates that were compared. Indeed, integration models have outperformed tested extrema and snapshot models across several discrete-sample datasets, although those comparisons do not exhaust possible non-integration policies [@hyafil2023temporal] [[C015]]. The central challenge is therefore not whether an accumulation model can describe behavior, but whether it predicts observations that plausible alternatives do not under a genuinely new condition.

Brief changes in sensory evidence can sharpen that test. Motion pulses can alter both choices and response times, yet their efficacy depends on when they occur, whether a decision has already reached commitment and which policy the observer adopts [@huk2005neural; @kiani2008bounded; @tsetsos2012timevarying; @carland2016evidence] [[C012]]. Weak randomized pulses in macaques have also produced persistent behavioral and parietal-cortical effects alongside evidence-accumulation models [@stine2023termination; @steinemann2024direct]. These results make pulses informative, but not self-interpreting: an early or lasting behavioral effect can reflect sensory dynamics, bounded commitment, decision policy or response generation. The same final choice shift may also conceal distinct effects on response timing or on the probability that a response occurs at all. A stringent use of perturbations is therefore predictive. Candidate models can first be fitted under ordinary stimulus conditions, frozen, and then asked to predict the joint response distribution under a separately randomized transient. Transfer failure then identifies a boundary of the complete prediction pipeline rather than automatically locating the error in its decision rule.

The mouse literature provides each component needed for such a test. Mice discriminate direction in continuous noisy random-dot displays across freely moving, touchscreen and head-fixed preparations [@douglas2006motion; @stirman2016touchscreen; @marques2018mousev1; @samonds2018motion] [[C019]]. They also accumulate stochastic sequences of discrete flashes or visual-tower pulses, including tasks used to study the corresponding cortical responses [@pinto2018visualpulses; @odoemene2018visual; @koay2020amplitude] [[C020]], and diffusion decision models have been fitted to mouse visual choices and response-time distributions [@you2022dynamics; @caballero2022rt] [[C021]]. These approaches establish continuous-motion perception, accumulation of discrete visual evidence and response-time modeling in mice, but they pose different predictive questions. Thus, neither visual evidence accumulation nor dynamic visual decision-making is new in mice. In a bounded search, however, we did not find the exact combination used here: continuous noisy motion, randomized signed perturbations, and a control-trained model tested out of condition against choices, response probabilities and response times. This is a preparation-specific gap rather than a claim of broad priority.

We considered three possibilities: control behavior could favor evidence retention by the tested accumulator (H1), favor the rapid-forgetting approximation (H2), or select one candidate while revealing that both model-and-input pipelines omit a process engaged by transient strong evidence (H3). We reconstructed trial-specific motion energy, compared the candidate models by joint held-out prediction on control trials, and then evaluated pulses whose direction was assigned before the behavioral outcome, retaining scheduled assignments independently of response timing. Control trials favored the tested accumulator among the tested implementations, and randomized signed pulse assignment shifted response direction [[C005]], but the frozen and gain-revised pipelines predicted the wrong temporal response profile [[C001]]. Because the predictive gates failed and calibration showed inadequate sensitivity, the confirmatory timing test was not run; the experiment therefore tests predictive transfer without claiming primacy, recency or a uniquely sensory, decision or response mechanism [[C007]].
