# Brief motion pulses expose limits of an evidence-accumulation model in mice

[MISSING: author list, order, affiliations and corresponding author]

## Abstract

Models that predict ordinary behavior may still fail when evidence changes abruptly. We tested whether a model-and-input pipeline selected on control trials could predict responses to randomized strong motion pulses. Here, in {{N001}} performing a reaction-time random-dot-motion task, joint held-out predictions of response direction, timing and occurrence favored the tested accumulator over the rapid-forgetting approximation by {{N016}} among the tested implementations, yet the frozen model-and-input pipelines failed to predict the pulse responses [[C001]] [[C004]]. We reconstructed motion energy and fitted both models before testing pulses. Positive rather than negative pulse assignment shifted response direction by {{N051}} (95% CI, {{N052}}–{{N053}}; Holm-adjusted P = {{N054}}) [[C005]]. At the common final time point relative to logged pulse onset, the cumulative response-associated directional contrast reached {{N059}} [[C005]]. Descriptively, the observed and predicted group time courses differed: the observed contrast emerged later and remained sustained, whereas predictions appeared early and weakened or partly reversed [[C006]]. In two informative mice, gains improving held-out choices worsened the remaining response objective [[C009]]. Both predictive gates failed, and inadequate sensitivity precluded the confirmatory timing test [[C007]]. Thus, model selection and pulse transfer constrain the tested pipeline without identifying a unique sensory, decision or response mechanism [[C008]].

## Impact statement

This study shows that held-out motion perturbations can expose predictive limitations hidden by successful model selection on control-trial behavior [[C008]].
