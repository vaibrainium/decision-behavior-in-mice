## Figure 1. Motion discrimination with brief directional perturbations in mice

**(A)** Task schematic and trial sequence. Head-fixed mice reported leftward or rightward random-dot motion by licking the corresponding response port. Responses could register after a minimum viewing interval of 0.3 s. The 5 s cutoff used in the analyses is indicated separately from the task sequence; trials could continue beyond this cutoff. **(B)** Scheduled coherence profiles for unperturbed trials and positive or negative motion pulses. Pulses temporarily replaced baseline coherence with +100% or −100% coherence. UCLA used two onset distributions centred near 60 and 240 ms with a nominal 50 ms pulse; UW used onsets near 50, 233 and 417 ms and four display frames, approximately 67 ms. Schedules are schematic; the UCLA acquisition/replay uncertainty is described in Methods. **(C)** Probability of a rightward response as a function of signed baseline coherence on control trials, conditional on responding by 5 s. **(D)** Median reaction times within each mouse for correct and error responses over absolute coherence; zero-coherence responses are displayed separately because neither direction is correct. **(E)** Probability of no response by 5 s, retaining both trials with later responses and trials without an observed response. Individual mouse values are shown alongside equal-mouse summaries; error bars indicate 95% Student-t confidence intervals across contributing mice. The experiment comprised 10 mice and 50 mouse-sessions. These behavioural summaries are descriptive. [claim:C201] [evidence:E101]

**Author/production notes:** use the audited scheduled-control classification and display the resulting trial counts and per-point mouse counts in the source-data table. The old Figure 1 includes a different set and must not supply an unchanged denominator. Panels C–E require rendering with the proposed confidence intervals; existing plots sometimes use SEM. Treat zero coherence as all responses, with an explicit separate symbol/label in D. Do not make rig comparisons inferential.

**Draft alt text:** A task diagram and pulse schedules accompany mouse-level choice, response-time and no-response summaries across motion coherence. Individual animals and group uncertainty remain visible.

## Figure 2. Reconstruction characterizes motion evidence beyond nominal coherence

**(A)** Trial seeds and task parameters were used to replay the random-dot stimuli and compute direction-opponent motion energy. **(B)** Calibrated time-averaged motion energy versus programmed coherence, displayed separately for the two stimulus implementations. **(C)** Trial-specific variation in the reconstructed motion signal at the same nominal coherence, including a persistent component arising from the sampled dot directions. **(D)** Per-mouse choice coefficients for nominal coherence and reconstructed hidden coherence from the existing control-labelled analysis. This panel describes association with the reconstructed stimulus; it does not establish a common discount across mice or an integration duration. **(E)** Pulse-aligned motion-energy response under the original replay and the tested alternative UCLA replay conventions. The alternatives illustrate reconstruction sensitivity; they do not exhaust acquisition uncertainty. Stimulus summaries and their spread are calculated across trials or seeds, whereas the behavioural coefficients in D correspond to individual mice (n = 10). Motion energy is expressed in equivalent coherence units; temporal filtering is part of the assumed evidence representation, not a measurement of neural latency. ## Figure 3. Control-trial prediction favours the tested accumulator over rapid forgetting

**(A)** Candidate models driven by reconstructed motion energy. The reference accumulator includes across-trial drift variability and a fast stimulus-independent response component with a fitted directional bias. The rapid-forgetting model provides an operational approximation to extrema detection. **(B)** Held-out control-trial score differences for each mouse, defined as the negative log likelihood of the rapid-forgetting model minus that of the accumulator; positive values favour accumulation. The summed difference was 345.7 units. Six of 10 mice exceeded the working 10-unit criterion in favour of the accumulator, and none exceeded it in favour of rapid forgetting. This criterion is a model-comparison reference, not a population significance threshold. **(C)** Control-trial score changes for removing drift variability or the fast-response component and for adding leak or urgency, with individual mice shown. **(D)** Descriptive control-trial fit quality from the final all-trial common-model fits: response choice, correct/error reaction times and probability of no response by 5 s. The final fits used 50 simulation repetitions per objective evaluation. Broad behavioural trends were captured, with systematic errors remaining in some error-response-time and no-response predictions. Panels B and C use held-out control scores; D evaluates fits on trials contributing to fitting and is not an independent validation. All panels include 10 mice; complete individual fit checks and parameter estimates are provided in Supplementary Figure S3 and its table. [claim:C203] [evidence:E103,E110]

Preserve the distinction between unit diffusion variance, evidence-input scaling and biological parameter interpretation. Use “near the fitting boundary” for the flagged parameters, not established lower limits. Do not relabel the control comparison as proof against every possible non-integrating strategy.

**Draft alt text:** Model diagrams and mouse-level held-out score differences favour the tested accumulator over rapid forgetting. Component comparisons and observed-versus-fitted behaviour show both predictive strengths and remaining response-time errors.

## Figure 4. Brief motion pulses shift response direction

**(A)** Per-mouse directional effects of positive versus negative scheduled pulses. For each mouse, ΔR and ΔL are the standardized differences in the probabilities of right and left responses by 5 s, respectively; the directional contrast is (ΔR−ΔL)/2. The group mean was +5.84 percentage points (95% Student-t confidence interval, 1.80–9.89; n = 10 mice; two-sided t test with Holm-adjusted p = 0.019). Nine mice had positive estimates. **(B)** Components of the same contrast: changes in right, left and no-response probabilities. These three component differences sum to zero within each mouse. **(C)** Directional effects for positive pulses versus controls (+4.86 percentage points, 95% CI 2.43–7.29; Holm p = 0.0043) and controls versus negative pulses (+1.40 percentage points, 95% CI −2.13–4.92; Holm p = 0.393). The three directional comparisons in A/C form the Holm correction family. Different matched trial sets contribute to the two control comparisons, so their magnitudes do not establish an asymmetry between pulse directions. [claim:C204] [evidence:E104]

**(D)** Cumulative directional contrast versus time after scheduled pulse onset, retaining trials that responded before onset. At 4.58 s after onset the contrast was +5.80 percentage points (pointwise 95% t CI, 1.82–9.78). This pulse-aligned endpoint differs from the fixed motion-onset deadline used in A–C. Most of the increase occurred between 0.5 and 2 s after onset. The curve describes when pulse-associated responses occurred; a later cumulative difference does not establish continued internal evidence or memory retention. [claim:C205] [evidence:E105]

Analyses were exploratory and used baseline coherences with absolute value at most 36%. The primary positive-versus-negative comparison retained 10,281 pulse trials in 717 supported mouse × session × coherence × onset cells, including 118 responses before onset and 1,709 trials unresponded by the deadline. Cells were weighted by the smaller arm count and normalized within mouse; mice contributed equally to the group estimate. UW conditions scheduled with only one pulse sign were excluded from signed comparisons. Controls used recorded sham onsets on UW and outcome-independent pseudo-onsets on UCLA. Points in A–C denote mice, and emphasized estimates/intervals show group means and 95% Student-t intervals. Bands in D are pointwise, not simultaneous. The primary effect pertains to the common-support population; robustness and full joint response curves are shown in Supplementary Figure S4. [claim:C206] [evidence:E101,E104,E105]

**Author/production notes:** the A/B/C effect sizes are probability-point contrasts, not odds ratios and not accuracy differences. The C intervals cover differing common-support populations. Use the all-scheduled D curves for this caption; substituting onset-eligible curves changes both the estimand and the endpoint value. Match cell/trial totals to the frozen run version. The original primacy test is not performed by this figure.

**Draft alt text:** Most mice show a positive directional response contrast after rightward versus leftward pulse assignment. Separate right, left and no-response changes, pulse-versus-control comparisons and a pulse-aligned cumulative curve describe the effect and its uncertainty.

## Figure 5. Pulse gain alone does not reproduce the observed response time course

**(A)** Observed cumulative directional pulse contrast and predictions from the reference model with the original pulse gain, the stored gain fitted jointly to outcomes and response times, and the gain optimizing the choice component alone. Comparisons use the same trial rows, common-support cells and mouse weights. The diagnostic gains/curves are in-sample, with control parameters held fixed. **(B)** Changes in the directional contrast within successive windows after pulse onset. Between 2 and 4.58 s, the observed increment was +0.12 percentage points (pointwise 95% t CI, −1.26–1.50), compared with −1.44 (−2.59 to −0.28) for the stored joint-gain prediction and −2.98 (−4.21 to −1.75) for the choice-best-gain prediction. The negative model increments describe a partial decline from an earlier positive cumulative contrast, rather than an overall reversal of pulse direction. Intervals describe between-mouse variation in each quantity and are not a paired test of their difference. [claim:C207] [evidence:E106,E110]

**(C)** Choice and joint objective profiles over pulse gain for PSUIM6 and PSUIM7, the two mice in which the diagnostic tradeoff was supported most clearly. Their in-sample choice-best gains were 7 and 4, whereas the joint optima were approximately 2.45 and 1.40. These mice are shown for diagnosis, not as representative examples of every mouse. **(D)** Held-out changes when gain was selected using choices rather than the joint objective, with gains selected within training folds. In PSUIM6 and PSUIM7, the summed choice-score improvements were 10.4 and 10.0 NLL units, accompanied by costs of 96.0 and 106.6 units in the remaining response-time/response-probability terms. Quasi-likelihood score components are kept at their original weights; these differences are diagnostic score changes rather than calibrated significance statistics. The group curves in A/B include 10 mice. Full profiles, RT-term coverage and the leak/urgency baseline are in Supplementary Figure S5. [claim:C208] [evidence:E106]

All analyses in this figure are exploratory. They use the legacy fitted model inputs, with the observational comparison restricted to matching rows; reconstructed pulse labels absent from those inputs are not silently inserted into the model overlay. The original and pulse-gain-revised predictive gates failed, and the original confirmatory primacy test remained unperformed. Gate results and their separate sensitivity analyses are reported in Supplementary Figure S6. The failure identifies a limitation of the tested model/input combination; it does not locate the missing process in sensory encoding, evidence accumulation or response generation. [claim:C209] [evidence:E106,E107,E108,E110]

**Author/production notes:** A/B must use the observational rows in E, not the slightly different D curve from Figure 4. Keep the mean prediction at the stored joint gain distinct from the choice-best diagnostic. Do not include the gain-10/20 curves in the same compressed y-range as the principal comparison; retain them in S5. Before a final corrected-input version, regenerate affected comparisons and update these numbers/records together. Show both fit uncertainty limitations and the single-seed limitation of the dense-mouse diagnostic. A subsequent filter pilot is not part of this caption until its results are evaluated.

**Draft alt text:** Observed and model-predicted directional response curves differ over time. Window summaries show a later decline in the model contrast. Gain profiles and held-out objective changes illustrate why increasing pulse gain can improve choices while worsening response-time prediction in two informative mice.
