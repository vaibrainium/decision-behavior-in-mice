# Brief motion pulses expose limits of an evidence-accumulation model in mice

[MISSING: author list, order, affiliations and corresponding author]

## Abstract

Models that predict ordinary behavior may still fail when evidence changes abruptly. We tested whether a model-and-input pipeline selected on control trials could predict responses to randomized strong motion pulses. Here, in 10 mice performing a reaction-time random-dot-motion task, joint held-out predictions of response direction, timing and occurrence favored the tested accumulator over the rapid-forgetting approximation by 345.7 NLL units among the tested implementations, yet the frozen model-and-input pipelines failed to predict the pulse responses. We reconstructed motion energy and fitted both models before testing pulses. Positive rather than negative pulse assignment shifted response direction by 5.844356 percentage points (95% CI, 1.799491–9.889221 percentage points; Holm-adjusted P = 0.01941029). At the common final time point relative to logged pulse onset, the cumulative response-associated directional contrast reached 5.798136 percentage points. Descriptively, the observed and predicted group time courses differed: the observed contrast emerged later and remained sustained, whereas predictions appeared early and weakened or partly reversed. In two informative mice, gains improving held-out choices worsened the remaining response objective. Both predictive gates failed, and inadequate sensitivity precluded the confirmatory timing test. Thus, model selection and pulse transfer constrain the tested pipeline without identifying a unique sensory, decision or response mechanism.

## Impact statement

This study shows that held-out motion perturbations can expose predictive limitations hidden by successful model selection on control-trial behavior.

## Introduction

Perceptual decisions are often described as an accumulation of noisy sensory evidence toward a bound. This account links stimulus strength to both choice and response time, but familiar behavioral signatures are not unique to accumulation. Tested non-integrating, leaky and urgency-based strategies can reproduce choice accuracy or temporal-weighting patterns that appear integration-like [@stine2020differentiating; @tsetsos2012timevarying; @cisek2009decisions; @okazawa2018psychophysical]. Joint choice and response-time distributions provide stronger constraints than accuracy alone because a strategy that predicts the same choices may predict different decision times [@stine2020differentiating; @ratcliff2008diffusion; @palmer2005effect; @winkel2014early]. This makes the full response distribution useful for rejecting particular implementations, although success within one stimulus regime still identifies a winner only among the candidates that were compared. Indeed, integration models have outperformed tested extrema and snapshot models across several discrete-sample datasets, although those comparisons do not exhaust possible non-integration policies [@hyafil2023temporal]. The central challenge is therefore not whether an accumulation model can describe behavior, but whether it predicts observations that plausible alternatives do not under a genuinely new condition.

Brief changes in sensory evidence can sharpen that test. Motion pulses can alter both choices and response times, yet their efficacy depends on when they occur, whether a decision has already reached commitment and which policy the observer adopts [@huk2005neural; @kiani2008bounded; @tsetsos2012timevarying; @carland2016evidence]. Weak randomized pulses in macaques have also produced persistent behavioral and parietal-cortical effects alongside evidence-accumulation models [@stine2023termination; @steinemann2024direct]. These results make pulses informative, but not self-interpreting: an early or lasting behavioral effect can reflect sensory dynamics, bounded commitment, decision policy or response generation. The same final choice shift may also conceal distinct effects on response timing or on the probability that a response occurs at all. A stringent use of perturbations is therefore predictive. Candidate models can first be fitted under ordinary stimulus conditions, frozen, and then asked to predict the joint response distribution under a separately randomized transient. Transfer failure then identifies a boundary of the complete prediction pipeline rather than automatically locating the error in its decision rule.

The mouse literature provides each component needed for such a test. Mice discriminate direction in continuous noisy random-dot displays across freely moving, touchscreen and head-fixed preparations [@douglas2006motion; @stirman2016touchscreen; @marques2018mousev1; @samonds2018motion]. They also accumulate stochastic sequences of discrete flashes or visual-tower pulses, including tasks used to study the corresponding cortical responses [@pinto2018visualpulses; @odoemene2018visual; @koay2020amplitude], and diffusion decision models have been fitted to mouse visual choices and response-time distributions [@you2022dynamics; @caballero2022rt]. These approaches establish continuous-motion perception, accumulation of discrete visual evidence and response-time modeling in mice, but they pose different predictive questions. Thus, neither visual evidence accumulation nor dynamic visual decision-making is new in mice. In a bounded search, however, we did not find the exact combination used here: continuous noisy motion, randomized signed perturbations, and a control-trained model tested out of condition against choices, response probabilities and response times. This is a preparation-specific gap rather than a claim of broad priority.

We considered three possibilities: control behavior could favor evidence retention by the tested accumulator (H1), favor the rapid-forgetting approximation (H2), or select one candidate while revealing that both model-and-input pipelines omit a process engaged by transient strong evidence (H3). We reconstructed trial-specific motion energy, compared the candidate models by joint held-out prediction on control trials, and then evaluated pulses whose direction was assigned before the behavioral outcome, retaining scheduled assignments independently of response timing. Control trials favored the tested accumulator among the tested implementations, and randomized signed pulse assignment shifted response direction, but the frozen and gain-revised pipelines predicted the wrong temporal response profile. Because the predictive gates failed and calibration showed inadequate sensitivity, the confirmatory timing test was not run; the experiment therefore tests predictive transfer without claiming primacy, recency or a uniquely sensory, decision or response mechanism.

## Results

### Mice provided choice, response-time and no-response constraints in a reaction-time motion task

We analyzed a reaction-time random-dot-motion task in which mice reported leftward or rightward motion by licking the corresponding response port (Figure 1A). The dataset comprised 10 mice across 50 mouse-sessions, collected with two task implementations that shared the decision structure but used different perturbation schedules (Figure 1B). Each trial provided up to three complementary observations: response direction, response time and whether the mouse responded within the analysis window. Responses could register after a minimum viewing interval of 0.3 s; trials without a registered response by 5 s were classified as no response by 5 s, an analysis boundary rather than a common task-termination time. Across the descriptive dataset, 25508 trials contained a response within this window and 4537 trials did not.

The three outcomes constrained different aspects of behavior. Signed-coherence psychometric functions described which response the animals made, while correct and error response-time distributions described when they made it (Figure 1C–D). No-response probabilities retained trials that would otherwise disappear from an analysis conditional on responding (Figure 1E). Zero-coherence trials were displayed separately in correct/error summaries because neither direction was correct by definition. Considering these outcomes together was important because two models could produce similar choice proportions while differing in response timing or in the probability of reaching a response.

On scheduled perturbation trials, strong motion briefly replaced the baseline stimulus in one of two directions. Assignment preceded the behavioral outcome, although a late pulse could be scheduled after the mouse had already responded. We therefore retained trials according to scheduled assignment rather than defining pulse exposure from the eventual response. Control-trial psychometric functions, correct and error response times, and no-response probabilities summarized the three outcomes later fitted jointly (Figure 1C–E). These summaries revealed variation among mice, but task implementation, site and cohort were confounded and could not support biological comparisons between rigs.

This design established two roles for the data. Control trials were used to fit and compare candidate decision models, whereas randomized pulse assignments tested whether a model selected under ordinary stimulus conditions could predict responses to a brief, strong perturbation. The descriptive panels defined those behavioral targets but were not themselves independent validation of a fitted model.

### Stimulus reconstruction provided trial-specific estimates of visual evidence

Nominal coherence did not capture all of the evidence presented on an individual trial. We therefore regenerated each stimulus from its stored seed and task parameters and passed the rendered frames through causal, direction-opponent motion-energy filters (Figure 2A). Replays continued through the analysis window rather than ending at the animal's response, and motion energy was calibrated separately for the two task implementations into equivalent-coherence units (Figure 2B). Mean reconstructed evidence followed programmed coherence, while different seeds at the same programmed coherence produced persistent trial-to-trial variation (Figure 2C). This reconstruction supplied the time-varying input to the decision models, but it was not a direct recording of the displayed frames, and filter delay was not interpreted as sensory or neural latency.

Continuing the replay beyond the observed response prevented the simulated evidence stream from ending at a behavior-dependent time. It allowed a simulated decision that occurred later than the recorded response to receive a stimulus of the same nominal duration as other simulations. Separate calibration placed the two task implementations on a common equivalent-coherence scale while leaving behavioral sensitivity to be estimated by the decision model. Together, these operations allowed a common model to respond to trial-specific evidence without assuming that the two rigs generated identical raw motion-energy scales.

Paired pulse-present and pulse-omitted replays were identical before scheduled onset, with no pre-onset motion-energy difference introduced by the causal filter (Figure 2D). At pulse offset, however, the task redrew background-dot directions; the two streams then consumed different random draws and acquired trial-specific differences. Their sign-aligned mean showed no persistent pulse-direction bias apart from a small deterministic rounding shift in specified cells. Labeled UCLA replay alternatives changed pulse-energy magnitude, while screen geometry, display-loop settings and the acquisition convention remained unresolved (Figure 2E). The reconstruction thus made the tested input explicit without establishing frame-perfect replay fidelity or a universal pulse-energy error bound.

This distinction mattered for every subsequent model prediction. Each prediction arose jointly from the reconstructed input, its temporal filtering and the decision-and-response model. Agreement would support that complete pipeline, whereas disagreement could not by itself identify which stage was responsible. Figure 2 therefore places the replay uncertainties next to the input representation rather than reserving them as an after-the-fact explanation for model failure.

A supplementary hidden-coherence analysis reports a historical control-labeled analysis in which a pooled common-ratio model estimated a hidden-to-nominal coherence weight ratio of 0.5 dimensionless. This descriptive estimate was conditional on a shared model and historical labels; no scheduled-control-label sensitivity analysis was available. Because persistent within-trial evidence also prevents a time-specific choice association from isolating time-specific weighting, we did not interpret this ratio as a population-wide discount or a temporal weighting kernel.

### Held-out control trials favored the tested accumulator over the rapid-forgetting approximation

We first asked whether control behavior distinguished the tested accumulator from the rapid-forgetting approximation. Both candidate implementations received the reconstructed input and shared the response mapping and fast-response mixture. The tested accumulator retained evidence to a bound, whereas the rapid-forgetting approximation implemented an extrema-like alternative (Figure 3A). On held-out control trials, we scored their joint predictions for response probability, response direction and response time. Summed across folds and mice, rapid-forgetting-approximation minus tested-accumulator negative log likelihood was 345.7 NLL units, favoring the tested accumulator (Figure 3B). This was a within-cohort predictive comparison, not a population significance test: the displayed per-mouse threshold approximated Monte Carlo score variability, and the result applied only to the two complete implementations tested here.

The joint score was essential to the comparison. It combined the probability of responding, response direction conditional on a response, and conditional response-time bins. Cross-validation held out control trials from the same mice and sessions, separating parameter fitting from scoring without testing generalization to new animals. A positive score difference therefore meant that the tested accumulator made better joint predictions for unseen trials within this cohort; it did not imply that every mouse or every component of the objective independently distinguished the candidates.

Final fits reproduced the broad choice and correct-response-time trends but retained errors in some error-response-time and no-response predictions (Figure 3C–D). These displays described fit adequacy on contributing control trials rather than providing an additional model-selection test. Recovery comprised 500 fit files under the generator, replicate, train/test and decision rule registered as variants L0U0V1Z0G1 L0U0V0Z0G1 L0U0V1Z0G0 L1U0V1Z0G1 L0U1V1Z0G1; 10 mice x 2 replicates; independent train and test; decision threshold greater than 10 NLL; denominator 20 per mechanism state. The fast-response component had hit rate 1.0 fraction when present, whereas drift variability had false-positive rate 0.35 fraction when absent. The fast-response delay correlation was 0.18 Pearson r across pooled generating and fitted datasets, not a within-mouse precision estimate. These recovery limits and near-bound parameters discouraged component-level interpretation, but did not change the relative held-out preference between the complete candidates.

The recovery results separated two questions that are easily conflated. Component hit and false-positive rates asked whether the fitting procedure selected a simulated feature under the registered rule, whereas the generating-to-recovered correlation asked how closely a fitted parameter tracked its simulated value. Both pooled simulated replicates across mice and therefore did not provide additional biological subjects or mouse-specific precision. In particular, poor recovery of the fast-response delay and false selection of drift variability limited claims about those parameters even though the complete accumulator predicted held-out control behavior better.

Control trials therefore supported the tested accumulator over the specified rapid-forgetting approximation without uniquely identifying accumulation or excluding other non-integrating policies. We next used the scheduled pulses as a more demanding, out-of-condition test of behavior under a brief, strong change in evidence.

### Randomized motion pulses reliably redirected choices

Pulse direction changed the animals' responses (Figure 4A–B). Across mice, positive rather than negative assignment shifted response direction by 5.844356 percentage points, with limits 1.799491 and 9.889221 percentage points. The complete mouse-level inference—including the two-sided one-sample test, statistic, degrees of freedom, 95% interval, raw probability value and Holm family—is registered as two-sided one-sample mouse t test versus zero; n=10; df=9; 95% Student-t CI; raw p=0.00970514512; Holm p=0.01941029024; family=3; t=3.26855; the Holm-adjusted probability value was 0.01941029. Separate changes in right responses, left responses and no responses summed to zero within mouse, indicating redirection rather than a common increase in responding.

The directional statistic compared the positive-minus-negative change in right responses with the corresponding change in left responses, using a symmetric scaling and retaining no-response outcomes. Its sign therefore followed the assigned motion direction rather than overall response propensity. The confidence interval and multiplicity-controlled probability value treated the mouse as the unit of group inference; trials and matched cells supplied within-mouse information rather than independent group replicates.

The primary comparison used 10281 trials in 717 cells defined by mouse × session × signed coherence × onset and containing both scheduled signs. Cells were weighted by their smaller arm count, weights were normalized within mouse, and mice contributed equally. The analysis retained 118 trials that preceded pulse onset and 1709 trials without a response by 5 s, preserving the randomized assignment estimand rather than conditioning it on when or whether the mouse responded. Timing-matched controls used recorded sham onsets for UW and outcome-independent pseudo-onsets for UCLA (Figure 4C). Because the two pulse-versus-control contrasts occupied different common-support cells, they described outcome components and alignment sensitivity but did not decompose the primary signed comparison or test pulse-sign asymmetry.

All three comparisons formed the registered Holm family. The signed positive-versus-negative assignment comparison was primary, while each pulse-versus-control comparison located one assigned direction relative to aligned trials without a scheduled signed pulse. UCLA pseudo-onsets were generated without reference to behavioral outcomes, so changing the pseudo-onset seed could alter control alignment but could not redefine the randomized signed contrast. An inconclusive control comparison was therefore not interpreted as evidence that its corresponding effect was absent.

Available task-code snapshots and a pre-outcome balance audit supported the assignment procedure, although code versions were not pinned to each collection session. The assignment-based behavioral analysis was unaffected by known limitations in the legacy model inputs: those inputs excluded 15 trials: 7 positive; 7 negative; 1 null; onset 0.25-0.28 and historically mislabeled the UCLA no-response pulse trials summarized in 48 historically labeled control; 43 directions recovered; 38 genuine non-null; 5 recovered null; 5 remaining zero-coherence directions unknowable. Those limitations instead qualified the later comparison with model predictions.

The response-associated pulse effect developed over time (Figure 4D). Across all scheduled trials, the cumulative directional contrast reached 5.798136 percentage points at the shared endpoint. The registered curve grid, endpoint, mouse denominator and interval convention were 0-4.58 s in 0.01-s steps; endpoint=4.58 s; n=10; pointwise 95% Student-t intervals. The contrast was small in the first 0.5 s and increased mainly from 0.5 to 2 s after logged onset, when many responses occurred. These windows were descriptive, not a multiplicity-corrected early-versus-late test. Because cumulative response curves carry earlier differences forward and include no-response trials, they locate response-associated divergence rather than the time of evidence entry, an integration kernel, neural latency or a processing stage.

Right and left responses entered these curves as competing outcomes, and trials without either response remained in the no-response probability. The display therefore avoided selecting only eventual responders or conditioning on survival beyond pulse onset. This inclusive construction preserved assignment, but it also meant that a late cumulative difference could carry forward an earlier effect and that a small early difference could simply reflect few emitted responses. The temporal pattern nevertheless posed a clear predictive target: the model had to reproduce not only the final directional shift but also how that shift appeared across response times.

### Control-fitted models predicted the wrong temporal profile of the pulse response

We next compared the observed pulse-aligned curves with simulations from the control-fitted models (Figure 5A–B). Observations and predictions used identical common-support cells and mouse weights on the historical model-input rows that overlapped the behavioral analysis. Across stored joint pulse gains and gains chosen to improve pulse choices, the models concentrated the directional contrast early and predicted a later partial decline. The mice instead showed little initial contrast, followed by a positive difference that emerged mainly from 0.5 to 2 s and persisted. Thus, the tested model-and-input pipelines failed to generalize the temporal profile of the pulse response.

The discrepancy centered on temporal shape rather than overall direction. Both mice and simulations moved in the assigned direction over part of the response window, but the simulated effect arrived too early and later weakened or partially reversed. Increasing pulse gain strengthened the predicted response without moving it into the delayed, sustained profile observed in the animals. The pulse trials therefore revealed a limitation that was not apparent from the control-trial fits alone.

The comparison was deliberately descriptive. Its intervals summarized between-mouse variation within the observed or predicted curves rather than a paired test of their difference, and simulations added Monte Carlo uncertainty. The common-row restriction also preserved the historical UCLA no-response labels and did not integrate parameter uncertainty or repair legacy exclusions. Pulse gain could rescale the reconstructed pulse while leaving control parameters fixed, but changing gain did not remove the temporal-shape mismatch. This result constrained an amplitude-only repair of the tested pipeline without locating the failure in sensory encoding, decision dynamics or response generation. This descriptive mismatch does not establish that the tested accumulator itself failed, because a different stimulus representation, pulse transformation or response mapping could alter the same prediction.

The gain profiles revealed a related tension between choice and timing predictions in the two mice that carried enough information for this diagnostic (Figure 5C–D). In PSUIM6 and PSUIM7, gains selected to improve held-out choices reduced choice NLL by 10.3998 negative log likelihood units and 10.0055 negative log likelihood units relative to the joint-gain solution, but increased the remaining response and response-time objective by 96.0103 negative log likelihood units and 106.6094 negative log likelihood units. The registered search and diagnostic settings were gain [0,50]; grid 0 0.5 1 1.5 2 3 4 5 7 10 14 20 28 38 50; 10 golden steps; 5 seed-0 folds; 50 CRN fit repetitions; fold seeds 0-4; all-profile and PSUIM6-PSUIM7 diagnostic seed=50; each dense-mouse diagnostic used one main Monte Carlo seed. These QMLE score changes retained the original objective weights, were not calibrated significance statistics and had no direct repeated-seed precision estimate for either mouse. Choice profiles were flatter in most other mice, so this was a two-mouse explanation of the group-level mismatch rather than evidence for a cohort-wide conflict or a unique mechanism.

### Failed predictive checks precluded the planned timing test

The pulse mismatch also appeared in the prespecified adequacy gates (Supplementary Figure S6). The primary baseline was the tested accumulator pipeline; the secondary `L1U1V1Z0G1` baseline added leak and urgency within the same input framework. In the original gates, observed-minus-predicted choice departure toward the pulse was 0.295968634567205 logit for the primary baseline and 0.3174069864244733 logit for the secondary baseline, while response-time coverage fell below the requirement for both. The registered criteria, choice 95% interval includes zero or absolute departure below 25% of predicted logit pulse effect; at least 80% of RT-quantile cells and 80% of censoring cells inside simulated 95% ranges, screened adequacy rather than testing equivalence. A revision prompted by this failure fitted one cross-validated pulse gain per mouse while fixing the control parameters. Choice departures remained 0.24 logit and 0.31 logit, and coverage was only 0.62 and 0.7 of mouse-by-quantile response-time cells for the primary and secondary baselines. Both revised pipelines therefore failed the combined gate.

The sequence of checks was designed to prevent a timing contrast from being interpreted on top of an inadequate predictive model. The original gates asked whether the frozen control-selected pipelines reproduced pulse-trial choices, response times and no-response probabilities well enough to serve as timing baselines. After they failed, the cross-validated gain revision tested whether one time-invariant scale factor per mouse could repair pulse magnitude while leaving all control parameters fixed. Cross-validation prevented a trial from being predicted using a gain fitted to that same trial, but the revision remained exploratory because the observed failure had prompted it. Its failure showed that amplitude rescaling alone did not create an adequate replacement baseline.

Simulations showed that the intended pulse tests were also insensitive under their registered generators. The frozen strategy procedure returned the correct verdict counts and denominators in extrema correct 1/20; accumulator correct 0/20; poor recovery did not imply equivalence between the tested accumulator and rapid-forgetting approximation. The gain-revised timing calibration used 20 full-cohort datasets at each of 4 decay levels over 0.5 s: 0% 10% 20% 40%; fitted pulse gains and fixed control parameters, and its decay-level detection counts were 10% decay 0/20; 20% decay 1/20; 40% decay 0/20; null false detections 0/20. The two simulations addressed different questions and neither propagated uncertainty in the fitted control parameters. Their empirical rates therefore characterized these specific procedures and generators, not general power against every form of rapid forgetting or temporal weighting.


Because the original and revised gates failed and the calibrated procedure had inadequate sensitivity, the confirmatory timing test was not run and the prespecified stopping rule ended further baseline revision. This outcome was not evidence for primacy, recency or flat temporal weighting. Instead, the randomized pulses established a robust directional behavioral effect while exposing a specific failure of the tested control-fitted models to predict its temporal expression.

## Discussion

Control behavior and pulse responses placed different demands on the decision models. Joint held-out prediction on control trials favored the tested accumulator over the rapid-forgetting approximation, whereas randomized motion pulses reliably redirected the animals' responses. Yet the control-fitted pipelines predicted that the pulse effect would appear too early and then weaken or partly reverse, rather than reproduce the delayed, sustained response-associated contrast observed in the mice. The failed predictive gates and low sensitivity of the planned timing procedure prevented us from using that mismatch to estimate a temporal weighting profile. These results therefore support a relative control-model preference and a robust behavioral response to pulse direction, but they do not uniquely identify the sensory, decision or response process that generated the pulse-associated time course. The tension between successful model selection under ordinary evidence and failed transfer to strong transient evidence is the main result, not a contradiction to be resolved by choosing one of those observations over the other. The pulse trials therefore add information even though they did not yield the intended timing estimate: they expose a boundary on the model's predictive domain that control-trial fit quality alone could not reveal.

This distinction is consistent with a broader model-identification literature. Choice accuracy and apparently stable temporal weighting can be reproduced by non-integrating strategies, while joint choice and response-time distributions can separate particular implementations more effectively [@stine2020differentiating]. Even then, the preferred mechanism can change across datasets: accumulation and urgency models are distinguishable in suitable designs, but each has prevailed in different behavioral regimes [@hawkins2015discriminating]. Time-varying perturbations add leverage only when candidate predictions remain separated after realistic sensory noise, delays and response rules are included [@zhou2009time; @tsetsos2012timevarying]. Pulse efficacy can also change with decision policy, including manipulations that favor stronger leak or urgency [@carland2016evidence]. Conversely, weak randomized pulses in macaques have produced persistent behavioral and LIP effects alongside drift-diffusion-like population dynamics [@stine2023termination; @steinemann2024direct]. Those studies provide positive evidence for accumulation in a different species, perturbation regime and neural preparation; they did not report the same control-only fit followed by a frozen strong-pulse transfer test. Thus, perturbations do not have a fixed diagnostic meaning: their ability to discriminate models depends on prediction separability under the noise, timing and response regime actually tested.

Several mechanisms could produce the mismatch without overturning the randomized behavioral effect. Sensory dynamics are a concrete possibility: time-dependent responses in sensory cortex can be inherited by a downstream integrator, and behavioral temporal kernels generally combine sensory, decision and response processes [@yates2017functional; @okazawa2018psychophysical]. Here, the replay was reconstructed rather than measured from displayed frames, and the unresolved display convention, screen geometry, pulse-offset redraw and chosen motion-energy filters could change the input delivered to the model. Photodiode-synchronized frame logging and a prespecified comparison of plausible spatial and temporal filters would directly test that explanation. Decision or response dynamics also remain plausible because the control fits missed aspects of high-coherence error timing and late responses; a richer candidate should first improve held-out control prediction and parameter recovery before being credited with explaining pulses. The model overlay further inherited legacy pulse labels and exclusions, which should be resolved by versioned corrected inputs followed by a complete refit rather than a post hoc relabeling of predictions. Sparse response-time contributions in some mice, limited simulation seeds in the dense-mouse gain diagnostic, poorly recovered or near-bound parameters and the confounding of site, cohort and task implementation all limit finer localization. More simulation replicates, expanded bounds with renewed recovery, and within-rig replication would narrow those uncertainties. Each remedy addresses a different link in the prediction chain: display measurements test the input, recovery tests estimability, and new held-out conditions test transfer. None of these limitations removes the assignment-based directional pulse effect; they instead identify which parts of the failed predictive pipeline require a cleaner test.

Accordingly, these data do not establish accumulation as the unique decision strategy, nor do they show that the pulse mismatch arose from the tested accumulator itself. They do not support a conclusion about primacy, recency, a memory or integration time constant, or the stage at which the transient was transformed. The response-aligned curves describe when behavioral outcomes diverged, not when evidence entered or persisted in a neural integrator. The two-mouse choice-versus-timing gain tradeoff cannot be generalized to the cohort, and the confounded rigs cannot support biological site comparisons. Withholding the confirmatory timing test after failed adequacy and sensitivity checks was therefore substantive: it prevented a precise mechanistic label from being attached to a pipeline that had not earned that interpretation.

The most informative next experiment is not simply a larger version of the present dataset. It should combine photodiode-verified pulse delivery and synchronized response timing with several randomized pulse amplitudes and onset times, so that input magnitude and temporal shape can be estimated rather than absorbed into one gain. An independent retention manipulation—for example, separated motion samples with a controlled evidence-free gap—would test whether information survives when no new motion is available, providing leverage that response latency after a single pulse cannot supply. Candidate sensory transformations, decision dynamics and response mappings should be specified in advance, fitted on control trials and tested on the perturbations with joint choice, response-time and no-response predictions. Before data collection, simulations should establish which candidate pairs are separable at the intended trial counts and define what happens if the predictive gate fails. A reserved validation set should then remain untouched until the candidates and their input transformations are frozen. Neural measurements could localize where a sustained or transformed signal appears, but they are not required to validate the behavioral perturbation effect. This design would ask whether the mismatch reflects the visual input representation, the accumulation dynamics or their mapping to action.

Model selection and perturbation transfer answer different questions. Control trials showed that the tested accumulator predicted ordinary behavior better than the specified rapid-forgetting approximation; pulse trials showed that this advantage did not guarantee prediction of behavior under a strong transient. Treating both outcomes as constraints turns the failed transfer into useful evidence: it defines where the current explanation stops working and what a successor model must predict. Brief motion pulses therefore contribute most strongly here not as a direct assay of primacy or memory duration, but as an out-of-condition challenge that exposed a limitation hidden by otherwise successful control fits.

The motion-energy representation used oriented spatial and temporal filters to form quadrature pairs. Squared responses were summed within direction, and leftward energy was subtracted from rightward energy:

M(t)=R_1(t)^2+R_2(t)^2-L_1(t)^2-L_2(t)^2.
\]

Opponent spatiotemporal motion energy can be constructed from oriented filters and quadrature-pair energy operations [@adelson1985spatiotemporal]. This precedent supports the filter architecture, not replay fidelity. Filter constants and support were sigma_c=6; sigma_g=3; temporal rate k=34; temporal support=0.55. We zero-padded the start and spatial boundaries, flipped the temporal kernel for causal convolution and excluded future-frame contributions. Motion energy was averaged over the displayed field and calibrated separately for each rig by the slope relating time-averaged energy to nominal signed coherence. Screen geometry was not corrected because the necessary measurements were unavailable.

The quadrature construction paired complementary spatial and temporal components to form directional filters; reversing their relative signs produced the opposite direction. Squaring and summing removed phase dependence before opponent subtraction. Calibration changed the evidence scale but did not retune filters per mouse or use behavioral outcomes. Between-mouse stimulus sensitivity therefore remained available to the fitted drift gain rather than being absorbed into preprocessing.

For model inputs, full-trial motion energy was decomposed as \(M=S+N\). The expected component \(S\) combined the rig-specific onset response with the mean response to a pulse coherence change, estimated from replays after subtracting the onset response and without using choices. The residual \(N=M-S\) retained seed-specific stimulus variation. Input scaling and temporal expansion used the registered operations: divide equivalent percent coherence by 100; hold 60-Hz samples on a 1-ms grid. The expanded input extended across the full analysis window rather than ending at the animal's response.

### Figure 3: decision models

Diffusion models use accuracy and response-time distributions jointly to constrain latent decision and nondecision components [@ratcliff2008diffusion; @palmer2005effect; @bogacz2006physics]. This rationale does not validate the present objective, reconstructed input or candidate set.

For trial \(i\), the tested accumulator evolved between absorbing bounds at zero and \(a\):

\[
x_i(t+\Delta t)=x_i(t)+[gM_i(t)+b+\eta_i]\Delta t+\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon_t,
\]

where \(x_i(0)=za\), \(g\) was drift gain, \(b\) was drift offset, \(\eta_i\sim\mathcal N(0,s_v^2)\) was an across-trial drift offset and \(\epsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) was within-trial diffusion noise. Fixed integration settings were dt=0.001; within-trial diffusion variance=1.0; fitted common-model bounds were ndt [0,0.8]; a [0.3,9.5]; z [0.2,0.8]; drift_gain [0.5,20]; drift_offset [-2,2]; sv [0,3]. A bound crossing determined choice, and fitted nondecision time was added to crossing time. The sensory-filter delay was already present in \(M_i(t)\). The common model retained drift variability and set leak, urgency and starting-point variability to zero.

Bound separation \(a\) controlled the evidence distance required for a response, and \(z\) expressed the starting point as a fraction of that separation. Drift gain scaled reconstructed input, drift offset captured an input-independent directional tendency, and \(s_v\) allowed a trial-level drift displacement shared across the trajectory. Nondecision time shifted a bound-crossing time without changing the decision path. Parameter bounds were fitting constraints rather than physiological ranges; the fixed diffusion scale set arbitrary evidence units.

The fast-response mixture followed p_guess [0,0.6]; P_right [0,1]; exponential mean delay guess_tau [0.01,0.3] s; guess_t0=0.3 s; off values p=0 bias=0.5 tau=0.1 s. On mixture trials, direction was drawn from the fitted right-response probability and time equaled the fixed mixture onset plus an exponentially distributed delay with fitted mean. The delay was not interpreted mechanistically. Simulated times were floored at the minimum-viewing interval; responses after the cutoff and unresolved simulations became censored outcomes.

The rapid-forgetting approximation added leakage toward the starting point:

\[
x_i(t+\Delta t)=x_i(t)+[gM_i(t)+b+\eta_i-\lambda\{x_i(t)-za\}]\Delta t+\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon_t.
\]

Its fitted leak range, nominal time constants and discretization were leak lambda [20,200]; nominal time constants 5-50; Euler dt=1. This was an operational rapid-forgetting approximation after sensory filtering, not an exact discrete-sample extrema detector. Both candidates used the same fast-response parameterization.

### Figure 3: objective, fitting and control comparison

Models were fitted separately for each mouse by minimizing a censored quantile negative log likelihood. Within each coherence-by-condition cell, a multinomial term scored left, right and censored outcomes, followed by conditional response-time histograms for each response direction. The registered binning, sparse-term, smoothing, weighting and empty-cell settings were: 9 conditional RT bins; RT term requires at least 9 observed responses per side and cell; alpha=0.5; RT weight=1; empty prediction penalty=20 per data trial. The objective was a simulation-based comparison score, not a calibrated likelihood.

The multinomial first separated whether a response occurred and which bound it reached. Conditional response-time terms then compared simulated and observed distributions within response direction. The observed-data inclusion rule was fixed before fitting each cell; simulated sparsity did not remove a difficult term. Pseudocount smoothing prevented undefined logarithms, while the explicit empty-prediction penalty prevented an optimizer from benefiting when simulation failed to populate an observed category.

Optimization and fit simulations used seed=42; DE tol=0 atol=1 maxiter=1000 population=15 times fitted dimensions; bounded Nelder-Mead maxiter=1000 xatol=1e-4 fatol=1e-4; model-selection repetitions=15. Common random numbers made the simulated objective deterministic within a fit. Targeted warm starts were used when a nested larger model fitted worse than a smaller model. Frozen pulse predictions used 200 repetitions per trial.

Control-only cross-validation used the registered scheme (5 folds per mouse; condition-stratified default_rng(0) permutation with cyclic labels; held-out evaluation seed=12345; sum test NLL across folds then mice). Folds were constructed within mouse and condition, each mouse-fold was fitted independently, held-out negative log likelihood was summed across folds and then mice, and lower score indicated better prediction. Candidate minus tested-accumulator score was therefore positive when the tested accumulator was favored; the observed sum was 345.7 NLL units. This compared specified pipelines on held-out trials from the same mice and sessions, not new animals or a population significance test.

Stratification preserved condition composition before within-condition permutation and cyclic fold assignment. Training excluded the held-out fold for both parameter estimation and selection. Held-out evaluation used a fixed independent simulation seed, and scores were not normalized by mouse trial count before aggregation. The total therefore weighted mice by available held-out trials; mouse-level score signs were also inspected rather than treating the aggregate alone as population replication.

Monte Carlo rescoring used 10 evaluation seeds 12345 and 1-9; 15 and 50 repetitions; 50-repetition per-mouse differences averaged over seeds; approximate greater-than-10-NLL per-mouse working threshold. The approximate per-mouse working threshold was non-inferential and was not a universal calibrated cutoff for 345.7 NLL units. Components were retained under the prespecified parsimony rule only when held-out improvement exceeded simulation noise in most mice, with recovery and interpretability considered separately.

### Figure 3: recovery and identifiability

Recovery used variants L0U0V1Z0G1 L0U0V0Z0G1 L0U0V1Z0G0 L1U0V1Z0G1 L0U1V1Z0G1; 10 mice x 2 replicates; independent train and test; decision threshold greater than 10 NLL; denominator 20 per mechanism state. Each synthetic dataset had independent training and test simulations. Denominators pooled mice and replicates, so recovery rates did not establish within-mouse identifiability. The complete design yielded 500 fit files; full matrices and bound flags were stored with the recovery outputs.

Generating parameters came from the corresponding fitted mouse-level model. Each candidate was refitted to a synthetic training dataset and scored on an independent synthetic test dataset. Evidence-accumulation parameter estimates should be accompanied by recovery or identifiability checks before mechanistic interpretation [@wiecki2013hddm; @luken2025parameter]. We therefore treated near-zero generating effects, pooled correlations, weak recovery, parameter bounds, high-coherence error misfit and late-response misfit as limits on component-level claims.
