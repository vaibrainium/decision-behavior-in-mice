## Mice used motion direction to guide their choices

Mice performed a reaction-time motion-discrimination task in which they reported the direction of a random-dot stimulus by licking a left or right response port (Figure 1A). Motion strength varied across trials, and responses could register after a minimum viewing interval of 0.3 s. On a subset of trials, a brief pulse replaced baseline motion with strong motion in one direction. Pulses occurred at early or middle times in both task implementations and at a late time in the UW implementation (Figure 1B). This design allowed us to examine ordinary motion decisions and the effect of a transient change in the evidence stream.

Choices followed the direction and strength of motion. On control trials, the probability of a rightward report increased with signed coherence in individual mice and in the group average (Figure 1C). Response times also varied with coherence and choice direction. Plotting mean RT separately for left and right choices preserved the direction of both the stimulus and the report (Figure 1D). These descriptive relationships established stimulus-guided behavior while leaving open how evidence was combined to generate a choice.

The principal analysis set contained 25,508 responses from 10 mice in 50 accepted sessions. Removing trials without reconstructable pulse-duration information left 25,493 model-input trials (Methods). Because the sessions had been selected using the existing behavioral acceptance criteria, the analyses characterize performance in the retained sessions. We next used the control choices and RT distributions to compare accumulation with alternative decision rules.

## Control choices and reaction times favored evidence accumulation

To determine whether control behavior favored evidence accumulation, we compared a bounded accumulator with two matched alternatives: a rapid-forgetting account and a discrete-sample extrema account. The accumulator retains successive samples of input until the decision terminates. The rapid-forgetting account retains input only briefly, whereas extrema detection responds to an individual sample crossing a threshold. The models shared the relevant sensory and response components, including across-trial drift variability and fast guesses, so that these components did not by themselves distinguish accumulation from the alternatives. This comparison follows the distinction between integration and nonintegration strategies developed by Stine et al. (2020).

Five-fold cross-validation favored the accumulator on the 14,014 control responses used for model comparison (Figure 2A). The mean advantage was 12.76 NLL units per 1,000 held-out trials over rapid forgetting and 18.74 over sample extrema. Both mean differences were positive, although their magnitude and direction varied across mice. Mouse-level t-tests gave Holm-adjusted p = 0.0374 and 0.0228, respectively; the corresponding Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon p values were 0.0586 and 0.0547. Thus, the direction of the model preference was consistent across the group summaries, while its conventional significance depended on the group test.

RT distributions supplied most of the discrimination. The mean choice and RT contributions were 2.55 and 10.21 NLL units per 1,000 trials against rapid forgetting, and 1.22 and 17.53 against sample extrema (Figure 2B). Choices alone therefore provided a weaker distinction than the joint choice/RT distributions. The RT contribution was part of the behavioral evidence for the models rather than an independently weighted criterion.

We assessed whether this comparison could distinguish the strategies by fitting the candidates to fixed synthetic training and test datasets. Recovery included 100 dataset pairs generated from the five candidate implementations and 500 candidate fits (Figure 2C). The accumulator had the best test score for 19 of the 20 accumulator-generated datasets, compared with 2 of 20 rapid-forgetting-generated datasets and none of the 20 sample-extrema-generated datasets. The two matched nonintegration candidates were more readily confused with one another. Across all 1,024 recombinations of the two simulated datasets per mouse, accumulator-generated cohorts reproduced the group preference for C over both E and ES, whereas no E- or ES-generated cohort reproduced that preference. These combinations reused the same simulated datasets and were not independent simulated experiments; the recovery result is conditional on the tested generating parameters, trial schedules and candidate set. Within the tested parameter range and candidate set, the recovery supports interpreting the control-model preference as evidence for accumulation. We then asked what the response to brief motion pulses revealed about its dynamics.

## Brief motion pulses influenced choices after their presentation

Pulse direction shifted choices toward the motion conveyed by the pulse. Descriptive psychometric functions separated by physical pulse direction showed this effect most clearly at intermediate baseline coherences (Figure 4A).

Paired psychometric fits showed a pulse-dependent horizontal shift (current Figure 4B). The midpoint α was lower for right-pulse than left-pulse trials in 9 of 10 mice, with a mean right-minus-left difference of -22.28 coherence-percentage units (median -16.38; signed paired effect size dz = -1.08). The two-sided exact paired sign-flip test gave Holm-adjusted p = 0.0117, correcting across the α and β contrasts. The β comparison was inconclusive (Holm-adjusted p = 0.1367). These exploratory comparisons condition on fitted coefficients; sparse coherence support and weak identification of some individual curves limit mechanistic interpretation. Fits were selected by their weighted residual error across multiple initializations, before testing the coefficient differences.

We next asked whether pulse influence depended on when the pulse occurred after motion onset. A joint psychometric regression with mouse-specific baseline offsets and shared pulse terms showed a decline in directional influence across pulse stages coded 1, 2 and 3 (Figure 4C). The estimated slope was −5.40 coherence-percentage units per stage (95% mouse-cluster interval, −9.85 to −0.95; two-sided p = 0.0226; cluster-jackknife sensitivity p = 0.0440). This exploratory analysis included 21,725 eligible responses from 10 mice, with late pulses available in six. The matched-six-mouse sensitivity was inconclusive (slope −1.72, 95% interval −7.25 to 3.82; p = 0.4615). The all-available estimate therefore depends on the shared-effect assumptions; it is an onset-stage effect, distinct from persistence after pulse presentation.

A pulse could bias an immediate response without being incorporated into an ongoing accumulation process. We therefore examined whether its directional influence persisted in responses made after the brief stimulus change, using the approach introduced by Huk and Shadlen (2005). This analysis included 19,313 responses from 10 mice, restricted to eligible early/middle pulse schedules and their matched controls. A logistic decomposition separated the directional influence of the pulse from a shared effect of either pulse direction toward the baseline-correct side. The directional coefficients were 0.43, 0.66 and 0.43 log-odds in the 0.3–0.6-, 0.6–0.9- and 0.9–1.2-s pulse-to-response bins, respectively. Each passed Holm correction over the seven latency bins (adjusted p = 0.0233; supplementary directional decomposition). No shared-effect bin passed the corresponding correction. Thus, the pulse influenced choices well after its presentation, supporting temporal integration of the transient motion signal.

The directional effect decreased among later responses. We asked whether this decrease could be described by a simple dilution account in which the pulse contributes a fixed amount of evidence while the influence of continued motion increases with viewing time. We expressed pulse influence in coherence-equivalent units, γ, and multiplied it by mean RT from motion onset, T. The resulting γT readout was sustained across the three bins spanning 0.3–1.2 s, with an early-bin mean of 15.15 percentage-coherence seconds (95% mouse-bootstrap interval, 11.78–17.75). It was smaller in the later bins, whose mean was 2.29 (interval, −4.01 to 8.23); the late-minus-early difference was −12.86 (interval, −19.78 to −4.08; Figure 4D). This sustained influence followed by a later reduction provided a temporal constraint beyond the aggregate choice shift. A descriptive exponential overlay summarized the decline with τ = 1.11 s (percentile mouse-bootstrap 95% interval, 0.45–3.73 s), conditional on a zero asymptote and the observed mean time coordinates; it did not estimate an intrinsic forgetting constant.

An assignment-based analysis, which retained eligible pulse assignments regardless of eventual response timing, yielded a mean right-pulse minus left-pulse directional response contrast of 5.84 percentage points across mice (95% t interval, 1.80–9.89; Holm-adjusted p = 0.0194; supplementary assignment-based analysis). The size of the effect varied across animals.

## Pulse responses further constrained the fitted accumulation models

We applied the same pulse-to-response analyses to decisions simulated from the fitted models. The control-trained accumulator predicted a directional pulse effect, but its influence was weaker than observed in the sustained portion of the response profile and became negative at later latencies (Figure 4A). Joint fitting to control and pulse trials did not uniformly remove this discrepancy. For example, the high-scoring joint model with a power input mapping, leak and urgency predicted a negative directional influence while the observed influence remained positive. These comparisons show that relative success on ordinary choices and RTs does not ensure that a fitted account reproduces the temporal effect of a brief change in the input.

The dilution-adjusted profiles also differed among models (Figure 4B). In the 0.9–1.2-s bin, the observed γT was 14.81, whereas the joint power/leak/urgency model predicted approximately −0.46 at its fitted parameters. A bounded model with zero leak and zero drift variability, retaining the fitted response mapping and fast-guess mixture, instead predicted a positive influence that declined at longer response latencies. Its early and late γT summaries were 7.51 and 2.14, respectively. The presence of a decline in this lossless accumulation model shows that the decline alone does not specify an intrinsic forgetting constant. The full size and temporal profile of the pulse effect nevertheless provide quantitative constraints that distinguish the tested fitted accounts.

Those constraints were especially apparent in individual mice. The observed 0.3–1.2-s pulse-minus-control effects were 15.50 and 13.48 percentage points in PSUIM6 and PSUIM7, respectively. The fitted onset-weight models predicted smaller effects in these animals, including when drift variability was removed (Figure 4C). At the same time, several predicted cohort means were compatible with the uncertainty of the observed mean, 5.48 percentage points (95% mouse-plus-trial bootstrap interval, 0.88–9.92). The unresolved discrepancy therefore concerns the individual effects and their time course, rather than a uniform failure to explain the average pulse effect.

We examined why models that approached some pulse summaries could lose predictive performance on the joint behavioral data. In a matched comparison of onset-weight models, removing persistent drift variability improved the held-out choice score by 49.29 NLL units but worsened the RT score by 360.22, for a total cost of 310.93 (Figure 4D). This tradeoff was concentrated in PSUIM7, which contributed 280.17 of the total cost. The disadvantage arose mainly in central and fast portions of the conditional RT distributions; the model without drift variability improved the slowest quantile bins (Figure 4E). The largest contributing condition illustrates the remaining RT constraint in correct, strong-coherence control responses (Figure 4F).

Together, the comparisons favor accumulation over the tested nonintegration accounts and show that transient motion has a sustained influence on subsequent choices. The pulse responses add constraints on the dynamics and variability of the accumulation process. Among the fitted accounts examined here, those constraints did not establish a common explanation of both the individual pulse profiles and the joint choice/RT distributions.
