# Why the fitted accumulators miss the mouse motion pulse

**Research review • 19 November 2026 • Data and code at base commit 3b55eca, with the saved Figure 4C simulations and the diagnostic script in the working tree.**

There are two observations to explain. Some mice give the brief motion replacement considerably more weight than the fitted models predict. The steepness of the decline from early to late pulses in the pooled psychometric model is a second observation, with much greater sensitivity to which mice contribute. The first discrepancy survives direct measurements of choices, comparison across sessions, and simulated experiments with the same number of trials. This gives the paper a concrete observation around which to organize the modeling.

## What changes when each mouse has equal weight?

Figure 4C estimates how far a pulse shifts a psychometric curve along the baseline-coherence axis. The fit has a separate baseline bias for each mouse, shared pulse and psychometric parameters, and a straight line over ordinal stages. A cell with more observations contributes more to the fit. A mouse-specific intercept corrects baseline side bias but still lets a densely sampled animal contribute strongly to the estimated pulse effect. PSUIM6 and PSUIM7 supply 4,936 of 8,529 eligible early and middle pulse responses (58%). Neither mouse had late pulses. All 1,228 eligible late pulse responses come from the six UW mice. The two sites differed in pulse duration, timing and stimulus implementation. These data cannot separate site, training, apparatus and animal composition. [Pulse counts](pulse_support.csv)

I repeated precisely the same psychometric readout after rescaling counts so that every mouse contributed the same total weight. Each animal retained its own relative cell counts. The predictions use the normalized urgency-gating model without drift variability, selected by pulse-only held-out likelihood. Both simulation seeds enter equally. No decision-model parameters changed.

| Figure 4C slope, coherence points per ordinal stage | Data | Fitted model |
|---|---:|---:|
| All available mice, original trial weighting | −5.40 | −0.73 |
| All available mice, equal total weight per mouse | −2.39 | −0.41 |
| UCLA only, early and middle, equal mouse weight | −5.34 | −0.10 |
| UW only, all three stages, equal mouse weight | −0.19 | −0.55 |

Weighting alone removes approximately half the observed slope. The model still underpredicts the two-stage slope in the UCLA sample. The original six-mouse, trial-weighted UW estimate was −1.72 with a 95% mouse-cluster interval of −7.25 to +3.82. Neither that interval nor the new point estimates establish a difference between sites. The original three dots are evaluations of a fitted straight line and cannot establish its shape independently. ## Does the discrepancy exist in actual choices?

For each mouse at each signed baseline coherence, subtract the percentage choosing right on left-pulse trials from the percentage choosing right on right-pulse trials. A difference of 17 points means that switching pulse direction changed the percentage choosing right by 17. This contrast uses both pulse directions, requires no psychometric slope or control trials, and is in probability units. I used the Figure 4C response filters, baseline magnitudes up to 36%, and exactly the same signed coherences across stages, directions and models within a mouse. The following comparisons condition on the retained responses.

| Equal weight per signed coherence and mouse | Early, data | Early, model | Middle, data | Middle, model |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| All ten mice | 7.74 | 5.14 | 8.78 | 4.94 |
| Four UCLA mice | 15.40 | 7.19 | 12.74 | 7.48 |
| Six UW mice | 2.64 | 3.77 | 6.14 | 3.24 |
| PSUIM6 | 17.47 | 6.56 | 11.94 | 5.97 |
| PSUIM7 | 25.10 | 11.11 | 13.57 | 8.32 |

All four UCLA mice exceeded the model's early prediction. Across all mice, five of ten showed a larger observed effect early than middle. Sparse conditions can have substantial leverage under equal coherence weighting. For example, DBM15 has a middle-stage coherence-by-direction cell with one response. Giving coherences weight according to the harmonic mean of the four observed cell counts (two signs × two stages) changes the all-mouse comparison to 7.75 early and 7.46 middle, against 5.11 and 4.81 predicted. Either weighting gives little average early–middle difference on this probability scale. The exact values differ from Figure 4C because the scale, coherence range and estimator differ. [Per-mouse estimates](mouse_paired_stages.csv) · [Observed-overlap weighting](overlap_weighted_mouse_by_seed.csv)

**How surprising are the two largest, best-sampled early effects?** An independent saved simulation run provides 60 trial-sized realizations per mouse, with the fitted parameters and schedules fixed. Each realization went through the same response selection and seven-coherence comparison. For PSUIM6 the central 95% simulated range was 1.2–12.1 points, with a maximum of 13.7, against 17.5 observed. For PSUIM7 it was 6.5–17.1, with a maximum of 21.8, against 25.1 observed. The *early-minus-middle* differences in these animals (5.5 and 11.5 points) both fell within the full range of simulated experiments. These checks give stronger evidence of a missing early **amplitude** than of a missing timing slope in each animal. This is a conditional, exploratory comparison in two previously identified mice. Selection, fit uncertainty and model selection are outside the simulated ranges. The two simulation batches have independent seeds and slightly different mean predictions. [Conditional ranges](conditional_prediction_ranges.csv)

The early excess also recurs within sessions. After matching pulse signs on baseline coherence separately in each session, the observed early difference exceeded the fixed model in **12 of 15 PSUIM6 sessions and 9 of 11 PSUIM7 sessions**. The median excess was 10.6 and 8.1 points. The corresponding middle counts were 10/15 and 8/11. These sessions contributed to fitting the model. The signs describe within-animal recurrence and do not increase the biological sample size. [Session differences and support](session_residuals.csv)

## What the primary literature contributes

**Huk and Shadlen describe a remarkably close precedent.** They calibrated a pulse-input amplitude to the monkeys' average behavioral shift and tested a bounded integrator against neural pulse responses. The simulations captured much of the post-pulse decline. In their separate onset comparison, the neural response depended on when the pulse arrived, beyond what the simple account captured. This distinction justifies retaining both mouse panels. It also clarifies the limits of a replication claim. Their Figure 10B measured LIP firing after a background motion pulse. Our Figure 4C estimates a behavioral shift after replacing the baseline motion. [Huk & Shadlen, 2005, Figs. 9–10](https://www.gatsby.ucl.ac.uk/~pel/course_wuhan/2021/papers/huk_shadlen2005.pdf)

**Sensory encoding is the first candidate I would investigate.** In monkeys judging sequences of motion pulses, early pulses elicited stronger responses in area MT. Integrating those measured sensory responses already reproduced a substantial early weighting. In mice making visually guided navigation decisions, the amplitudes of cue-locked cortical responses varied with behavioral and evidence context. More recently, macaque random-dot data related temporal-context effects to both motion adaptation and changes in pupil response, which varied independently. These experiments make it biologically plausible that equal physical motion changes enter accumulation with different effective strengths. They provide no direct measurement of gain in our mice. [Yates et al., 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn.4611) · [Koay et al., 2020](https://elifesciences.org/articles/60628) · [McGaughey & Gold, 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13405624/)

**Commitment and sensory gain have overlapping predictions.** In a leaky integrator at a fixed readout time T, an input at t contributes in proportion to exp[−λ(T−t)]. Positive leak discounts the *earlier* increment. In a free-response task a later pulse can also arrive after commitment, even when it precedes the overt response. A recurrent attractor can be particularly sensitive to a perturbation early in deliberation as the two choices compete. Simulations and experiments show that decision policy and sensory or motor delays can change measured temporal weights. The boundary, leakage, time-varying sensitivity and onset of commitment therefore require simulation under the actual task and filters. [Wong et al., 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2525934/) · [Kiani et al., 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670720/) · [Okazawa et al., 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-05797-y) · [Stine et al., 2020](https://elifesciences.org/articles/55365)

**Attention is a specific hypothesis.** Human and macaque observers changed when they weighted motion as its temporal reliability changed. In a mouse visual change-detection task, learned temporal expectation modulated the motor-cortical response to sensory evidence. In human recordings, target-selection signals predicted when and how rapidly evidence accumulation began. These studies supply two different predictions here: greater *directional* information per physical change, or an increase in the probability that the mouse acts following either direction. The mouse replacement pulses were unannounced and there was no independent attention manipulation in this dataset. [Levi et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6220584/) · [Orsolic et al., 2021](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10127332/1/1-s2.0-S0896627321002014-main.pdf) · [Loughnane et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26853360/)

**Slow choice state is yet another timescale.** Mouse choices can switch between stimulus-guided and biased strategies over successive trials, and mouse pupil and movements can covary with those states. In humans, previous choices can bias evidence accumulation on a subsequent trial. Our own history analyses support persistent behavioral bias. Explaining a pulse whose direction was scheduled before the trial additionally requires a state-dependent response to its direction or a selection interaction. There is little direct support for the first in our existing pilot. [Ashwood et al., 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-021-01007-z) · [Hulsey et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11016285/) · [Urai et al., 2019](https://elifesciences.org/articles/46331)

## What the response timing says, and the next comparison

Using the saved trial-level predictions, I counted responses from pulse onset up to fixed elapsed times. The denominator is trials still awaiting a response at onset that responded by the deadline, in data and simulations separately. This check includes responses before the end of the pulse. It uses recorded or timing-matched sham controls, the same weak-to-moderate signed coherences across conditions and equal mouse weights. Coding a right response as +½, left as −½ and a still-pending response as zero yields a directional cumulative contrast. The separate probability of **any** response compares the average of both pulse directions to sham. These are descriptive cumulative probabilities, and the latter measures directional averaging rather than a hazard or a pure alerting mechanism. [Group time courses](group.csv)

In UCLA mice, at 1 s after an early pulse, the signed response difference was 9.74 points versus 6.37 predicted. The probability of any response was 4.22 points higher after a pulse than after a sham, versus 0.98 predicted. After a middle pulse, the respective any-response differences were +4.04 and −0.03. UW shows a different pattern, and the historical schedule-based analysis including omissions gave weak pooled evidence for a common timing shift. These results leave room for an effect on response timing. Directionally effective evidence can itself accelerate some decisions, so an independent alert or commitment trigger is unproven. [Prior scheduled and onset-eligible analysis](../../../../.agents/reports/2026-10-23-pulse-response_time-20261023T191030Z_fa8de8b.md)

**The next comparison I would fund with analysis time is a pulse-input change against a pulse-locked change in commitment.** Use the best pulse-only model as the common baseline and keep its joint choice/RT objective. One candidate adds a brief signed gain on the reconstructed motion transient, with mouse-specific amplitudes partially pooled across the cohort. Its test is whether it predicts held-out directional choices and their latencies. A second adds a brief sign-independent increase in readiness to commit with the winning side determined by the current evidence; its test is whether it improves the excess of either-choice responses after pulse versus sham as well as the signed choices. Allowing a proportional gain alone on the existing linear joint model has already been tried. Measure the relationship to the **pre-pulse**, causally filtered evidence, accounting for signed baseline coherence, because commitment predicts a dependence on how far the decision had progressed. Gain plus bounds can also generate such a dependence; compare full predictions.

Fit on pulse trials only, hold out complete sessions where feasible, and use sham trials only for forward validation. Estimate individual effects with partial pooling and compare early and middle on the common ten-mouse support. Reserve late for the six mice that received it. Preserve the original response mask, show the separate scheduled-trial sensitivity if making an assignment claim, and keep session boundaries when using history. Score the same held-out choices, both response-time distributions, the direct pulse difference and the pulse-versus-sham cumulative incidence. Check parameter/model recovery on the existing schedules before interpreting mechanisms. The earlier coherence-step analysis already showed that a fixed ±100% replacement couples step size to baseline coherence and polarity. Even a successful predictor may leave transient sensory gain and decision policy observationally similar. [Earlier step-size check](../../../../.agents/reports/2026-10-27-pulse-effect-vs-coherence-change.md)

**Paper direction.** Brief motion pulses reveal a limitation of the models that perform best on overall choice and reaction time. Some mice were repeatedly more susceptible to the directional perturbation than those fits predicted. Pulse onset and time since the pulse provide complementary constraints on an account of that susceptibility. A population-wide temporal attention mechanism or a particular leak constant remains open.

## Reproduction and review scope

From the repository root, run the command below with a fresh output directory. It reads saved Figure 4 data, fixed-parameter simulations from two runs and the earlier response-timing cache. The response masks and random seeds in these sources differ where indicated. The script verifies the original Figure 4C slope and model slopes, common support and the deadline identity for the timing check. All new quantities are descriptive, with no new group hypothesis tests. The model-weighting sensitivities each converged at ten starts with no bound contacts. Full-fit checks do not include parameter uncertainty. No decision-model refits or new experiments entered this report.
